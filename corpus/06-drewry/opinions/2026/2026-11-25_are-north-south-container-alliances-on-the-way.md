# Are North-South Container Alliances on the Way?

*Published on 25 November 2026*

Speculation that Maersk and Ocean Network Express (ONE) could establish a North-South container shipping alliance raises an interesting question: are we approaching a new phase in liner industry cooperation, one in which alliances extend beyond the traditional East-West trades?

Vessel-sharing agreements (VSAs) are not new to North-South routes, but the more structured and longer-lasting alliance partnerships have until now been confined to the Asia-Europe, Transpacific and Transatlantic trades, expanding only recently to the Middle East in a few isolated cases.

**Source:** Drewry Maritime Research - Container Forecaster

These East-West routes handle the largest and most concentrated cargo volumes, providing the critical mass needed to justify deploying increasingly large vessels. Sharing ships enables carriers to spread operating costs, improve vessel utilisation and offer more comprehensive weekly networks without each having to operate an entirely independent service.

The economics differ on North-South routes. Trades connecting Asia, Europe and North America with Latin America, Africa and Oceania generally have smaller and more fragmented cargo flows. Vessel sizes are consequently more varied, while port infrastructure, cargo imbalances and operational requirements can make network coordination more complicated. Historically, cooperation has therefore tended to take the form of individual VSAs, slot exchanges and regional consortia rather than extensive alliance structures.

**Source:** Drewry Maritime Research - Container Forecaster

However, the strategic incentives for greater cooperation on North-South trades are becoming increasingly compelling. The average size of containerships deployed on these routes is rapidly increasing (see Table 2) due to the cascading effect from East-West trades, and with the industry facing substantial newbuilding deliveries over the next few years that trend is only going to continue.

Therefore, pooling capacity and rationalising overlapping North-South services could offer considerable efficiencies for carriers, particularly on routes where maintaining multiple competing networks is difficult to justify.

At present, this is all speculation with neither Maersk or ONE willing to comment. Ultimately, the significance would depend on the scope of any agreement, if one is reached at all. A collection of individual VSAs would represent an incremental development, whereas coordinated network planning across multiple North-South trades could mark a more fundamental shift in industry structure.

If a broad alliance does indeed materialise and delivers meaningful cost and service advantages, it could encourage other carriers to pursue similar arrangements, potentially accelerating consolidation of North-South service networks and increasing competitive pressure on smaller operators.

The key question for cargo owners surrounding any extension of alliances into new geographies will be whether it delivers a better-connected and more reliable North-South shipping network or simply a more concentrated one. Smaller shippers, particularly those reliant on secondary ports and less competitive trades, will worry that they could find themselves with fewer alternatives and less negotiating leverage.

From the carrier viewpoint, ONE, and even Maersk, must be looking at the huge North-South network of market leader MSC and conclude that they need to cooperate to provide a competitive network (see Figure 1). Hapag-Lloyd, who currently partners Maersk on East-West routes in the Gemini Cooperation, would then consider that it also needs to find itself a North-South partner.

The container shipping alliance model has traditionally been built around the economics of mega-ships on East-West routes. There is no longer any compelling reason why similar principles of scale, utilisation and network efficiency cannot be applied elsewhere.
