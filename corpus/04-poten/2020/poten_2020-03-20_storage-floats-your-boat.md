# Storage Floats Your Boat

### Floating storage can provide temporary boost to tankers

Two weeks ago, the OPEC+ deal fell apart and the major OPEC producers started to export "at will". The global spread of the coronavirus had already curtailed oil demand, so it was immediately clear that most of this additional oil would go into storage, either fixed on land or floating on the oceans. In this Weekly Opinion, we want to explain the economics of floating storage and discuss how the situation may be different this time from previous markets with excess crude supply.

Initially, inventories will build on land, primarily in locations where oil demand has been negatively impacted and refiners are forced to cut back runs. China was the first major country where this happened (independent of the OPEC+ collapse) and inventories have been rising sharply since February. According to data analytics company URSA, Chinese crude oil inventories have increased by 60.5 million barrels (8.6%) since the start of 2020 and are now at a record high. These increases in inventories are likely to happen in other global markets as well as refiners are cutting runs to deal with lower product demand due to COVID-19.

Oil market contango is another source of demand for inventories. This is a situation when oil is more expensive for future delivery than it is today. In that case, a buyer (usually a trader or an oil company) can buy physical barrels of crude oil now, immediately sell futures for later delivery and put the oil into storage. As long as the price spread between today's prices and the futures market is more than the storage cost, including financing and insurance, the trader can make a profit. When there is significant oversupply in the oil markets, contango is necessary to remove excess oil. Among the OECD countries, the U.S. has significant storage capacity. The most obvious candidate to receive more barrels is Cushing, Oklahoma, but President Trump has directed the Department of Energy to also look at filling up the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) to its maximum capacity, by purchasing 77 million barrels of American crude. Ultimately, as storage capacity is filling up prompt prices will go down to levels that are so low that producers will need to cut output.

In addition to land-based storage, traders like to utilize tankers to store crude oil, because of the flexibility they provide to move the crude around the globe and store it in that part of the world where it has the highest value. The economics for floating storage is essentially the same as they are for land- based storage. The trader must consider the price spread and see if that pays for the tanker, as well as the financing and insurance costs of the cargo. In the current market, the rates for floating storage, which typically utilize VLCCs, range between $45,000 per day for 6 months on an older vessel (>15 years) and $62,500 per day for 12 months on a modern, scrubber fitted, ECO ship. The highest reported fixture, $80,000 per day was for a short-term charter (minimum 80 days / maximum 120 days) in the U.S. Gulf. While these rates are healthy from a historical perspective, it does not compare to the $200,000/day (or more) that some vessels were able to command in the spot market.

As the chart above illustrates, floating storage of crude oil is not a new phenomenon. During the global financial crisis in 2008-2009, floating storage peaked at some 100 million barrels. During the period of international sanctions on Iran (2012-2016) floating storage peaked at more than 120 million barrels (a significant part of which was utilizing Iran's own tanker fleet). Iran's tanker fleet has again been utilized for floating storage since 2018/2019, but in recent weeks new vessels have been added. The expectation is that floating storage will increase further over the next few months and reach levels not seen before. This represents a significant capacity overhang for when global oil demand starts to recover.
