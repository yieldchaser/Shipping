# Libyas Rollercoaster Ride

### Oil exports have collapsed (again) this year

After several years of relative stability, the oil export situation in Libya deteriorated again in January of this year. The fighting between the warring factions in the west and the east of the country intensified and the leader of the Libyan National Army (LNA), Mr. Khalifa Haftar instructed the closure of Libya's eastern oil ports. Since then, oil exports from the country have effectively stopped, with only an occasional shipment making it out. The blockade of Libya's oil production and export infrastructure forced the National Oil Company (NOC) to invoke force majeure, which was only lifted last week. Expectations were high that a resumption of oil production and the opening of the ports would kickstart exports. However, this proved to be too optimistic. Only one Aframax tanker loaded a cargo of crude oil destined for Italy. Unfortunately, this has been a familiar story for Libya, which has experienced a number of false starts since its longtime strongman Muammar al-Gaddafi was ousted in 2011. Libya's problems have reduced it to a somewhat marginal player in the global oil markets. At the same time, the country continues to have significant potential. Libya has large reserves of high-quality crude oil and has a good geographical location in the Mediterranean, close to the European markets.

Estimated at 48 billion barrels, Libya has the largest proven oil reserves in Africa (ahead of Nigeria with 37 billion barrels), and it is in the top 10 globally. The quality of its crude oil is very high; most Libyan grades are light sweet with a very low sulphur content. Most of the country remains unexplored and the ongoing civil unrest has prevented a large-scale exploration program. As a result of the political instability and the volatile security environment, many of the international oil companies (IOCs) that traditionally were involved in Libya through exploration and production sharing agreements with the NOC, initially left the country or reduced their exposure. However, given Libya's potential, several IOCs, such as Total, BP, Repsol and ENI are keen to return once the dust settles.

Historically, European countries around the Mediterranean, in particular Italy, France, Spain and Greece have been the main buyers of Libyan crude. More recently, Turkey has become a more important customer. Turkey has become one of the most important backers of the UN-recognized, Tripoli-based Government of National Accord (GNA), the rival of Mr. Haftar's Libyan National Army. Outside the Mediterranean, Libyan crude is also exported to Northwest Europe (UKC/Baltic) and Asia. Over the period since 2014, China has become the second largest importer of Libyan crude oil (after Italy). This is quite remarkable, especially since this is a long- haul trade and Libya's ports currently cannot accommodate VLCCs. The trade to China is predominantly a Suezmax trade. The importance of China as a market also shows through in 2020 to date. While almost no crude left the country since January, due to the various closures and blockades, China was one of the few countries that continued to get some deliveries from Libya. Italy, Spain and Turkey were the others.

It is difficult to pinpoint the impact of the conflict in Libya on the tanker market. At first glance, the effect seems to have been fairly limited, because the collapse in Libyan production and exports coincided with the OPEC price war and the global oil demand destruction due to Covid-19. These factors have had a much bigger impact on the Aframax and Suezmax market in the Mediterranean. However, as global oil demand recovers, Libya's high-quality light sweet crude will be very desirable and if the country's warring factions can reach a compromise that allows production and exports to resume, there will be no shortage of takers. While the impact on the Aframax market may be limited due to the short-haul nature of the Libyan export trades to southern and northwest Europe, a resumption of the Suezmax trade to China to 2019 levels would give the Suezmax market a boost. Since Libya is exempt from the OPEC production cuts, it could potentially ramp up to the 2019 productions levels of between 1.0 and 1.2 Mb/d. Further growth would require major investment and a resolution of Libya's geopolitical quandary.
