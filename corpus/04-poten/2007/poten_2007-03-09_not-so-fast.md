# Not So Fast

After last week's stock market tumble, many individuals and businesses paused to think about the stability and autonomy of the US and Chinese economies. The drop that was precipitated by other stock market declines in the Far East, left many wondering whether or not recent strength in the market was based on fundamentals or was simply a result of momentum. While the market has recovered slightly over the last several trading days, it still remains slightly lower than beginning of the year levels. The chart below shows the S&P 500 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average since the start of 2005. Although the stock market may appear to be suffering slightly, there is a consensus that the recent activity was more of a correction than a cause for any long term concern.

With many quick to believe that long- running strength in the freight and equity markets are doomed to come to an end, there is reason to believe that growth will at least continue in the near-term. After all, it is said that "economists have correctly predicted nine of the last five recessions."

The tanker fleet is larger than ever, yet rates are still robust.

Many anticipated that 2006 would be the year that rates would decline significantly, but this was not the case. Some have suggested that high rates could not possibly sustain through 2007- something that is not apparent looking at today's levels. Pundit asserted that $100 oil would be seen before $50 oil, something else that has yet to happen. It is difficult to predict the future, but understanding fundamentals certainly helps with the anticipation. Sounds as if many tanker market analysts have fallen into the trap of economists and predicted nine of the last market drops as the "end of the boom." The market seems to be saying, "not so fast."
