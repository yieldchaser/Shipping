# A Winter of Discontent

One of the key drivers of demand for tankers is crude and clean product oil demand. One of the most relied upon crude oil and refined products outlooks is published by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). The EIA released an updated Short-Term Energy Outlook and Winter Fuels Outlook on October 12th and it contains some revisions worth noting. The key points are summarized below: Ø Oil prices (West Texas Intermediate) are forecast to remain above $63/bbl for the rest of 2005 and 2006.

Ø Petroleum demand growth has been reduced to 1.2 million bpd for 2005, down from the previous outlook of year-to-year growth of 1.7 million bpd.

Ø This revision reflects a reduction in petroleum demand growth to 1.8% per year for 2005 and 2006 compared to an actual growth of 3.2% in 2004.

Ø Gasoline prices are expected to average $2.56 per gallon between October 2005 and March 2006 consistent with higher crude prices.

Ø Natural gas heating oil prices are expected to rise 48%, or an average of $350 for the average household.

Ø Heating oil prices are expected to rise 32%, or $378 for the average household. The reasons are not surprising: hurricanes Katrina and Rita have knocked out a considerable portion of Gulf of Mexico production that must be compensated by imports in a world where supplies are already tight. The EIA raised its forecasts for diesel, heating oil, and gasoline prices. While the EIA forecast for full-year 2005 gasoline prices was increased from $2.32 per gallon in September to $2.39 in October, quite remarkably, the forecast for WTI for the full-year 2005 fell from September's $58.77 per barrel to October's $57.60. The widening spread between crude and refined products reflect the enormous growth in imports shown below. The increased transportation costs associated with these increased volumes clearly impacts the price for consumers.

Starting from a modest 500,000 tons per month of UKC clean exports to the U.S. Atlantic Coast at the start of 1996, clean cargo exports have been steadily growing reaching an estimated 2,300,000 tons in October. The total amount of cargo moved from northern Europe to the U.S. Atlantic coast from January- October 2004 was 14.4 million tons compared to 17.7 tons this year, up 3.3 million tons or 23%. Such large scale imports have affected U.S. wholesale prices that have to rise to cover the extra cost of importing clean cargoes from offshore markets. The chart on the next page tracks the cost in terms of $/bbl for moving 37,000 ton clean cargoes from northern Europe to the U.S. Atlantic Coast. The cost of moving clean products has increased 90% in September to $4.04 per barrel from a recent low of $2.13 per barrel in August.

### Conclusion

Sharply rising clean cargo imports coupled with sharply rising freight costs help to explain why U.S. gasoline prices reached $2.56 per gallon, $0.67 greater than the third quarter of 2004. Current estimates that full recovery of Gulf of Mexico oil production facilities and refineries will take a year means that the trend of higher import levels and higher shipping rates for clean cargoes will be sustained in the coming months. Higher prices for oil products will eventually lower demand, but commuting habits do not easily adjust in the short term.
