# Stranger Things

### U.S. crude oil imports are rebounding as the economy reopens

The United States started to import crude oil in meaningful quantities after World War II. In the 1970s, the growth in imports accelerated, increasing from about 1.5 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) in 1970 to close to 7.0 Mb/d in 1979. The Iranian revolution in 1979 sparked the world's second oil shock. Declining production in Iran caused oil prices to increase rapidly. In the aftermath, U.S. imports declined precipitously and bottomed out at 2.1 Mb/d in February 1985. In the 20 years from the mid-1980s until 2006, U.S. crude oil imports increased rapidly, reaching than 10.7 Mb/d in 2006. A combination of lower demand growth and higher domestic production caused imports to fall gradually until 2015. At the end of 2015, the ban on U.S. crude oil exports was lifted. While U.S. shale production also increased during this period, exports grew more quickly than production and this temporarily contributed to an increase in U.S. crude oil imports. As U.S. refiners adjusted to the new crude oil sources, domestic shale oil started to replace imports (in the U.S. Gulf) and imports declined again. When Covid hit, oil demand collapsed and both domestic production and imports took a hit. Now that the U.S. is in recovery mode, how is this impacting crude oil imports?

In the United States, it is important to differentiate between the two coasts, because they are subject to different supply-and demand dynamics. The Atlantic Coast, including the U.S. Gulf, has the most refining capacity. Refineries in this area also have ample access to domestic sources of crude oil, such as the Permian Basin and Eagle Ford as well as crude oil from the deepwater U.S. Gulf. The West Coast of the U.S. has less refining capacity and it does not have access to the shale oil resources. Instead, West Coast refiners have relied on Alaskan crude oil for a significant portion of their feedstock.

As mentioned previously, seaborne crude oil imports into the U.S. Gulf and the U.S. East Coast have been declining since mid- 2018 (chart 1). Increased access to domestic shale oil, sanctions on Venezuela and the closure of refining capacity on the U.S. East Coast all contributed to this. During their price war with Russia in March/April 2020, the Saudis sent large volumes of oil to the U.S. Gulf, but imports fell back to their previous levels after OPEC+ agreed to production cuts in the face of the Covid crisis. In recent months, imports have started to pick up as the U.S. economy is rebounding. U.S. crude oil imports into the U.S. East Coast and the U.S. Gulf are back to levels not seen since the first half of 2019. The reason for this increase is that while oil demand has increased post-Covid, production has not, so more imports are required. Shale oil producers have shown remarkable discipline in the face of higher oil prices. Rather than increasing production, they have focused on debt reduction and increased shareholder payouts. As long as this capital discipline is maintained, production growth is expected to remain limited. At the same time, U.S. exports remain steady as well.

The situation on the U.S. West Coast is different, as can be seen in chart 2. On the Pacific side of the U.S., about half of the seaborne imports are coming from Alaska and these movements are subject to the Jones Act, limiting the impact on the international tanker market. As the West Coast of the U.S. does not have easy pipeline access to shale oil, they rely more on imports. These come primarily from the Middle East and (to a lesser extent) from other sources, such as Latin America. Since the steady flow of Alaskan crude oil continued, the pandemic caused a significant drop in international crude oil imports on the U.S. West Coast. A recovery in demand has also led to a boost in imports.

Looking ahead, it appears that a continued recovery in U.S. oil demand will likely lead to more crude oil imports, both on the Atlantic as well as the Pacific side of the U.S. On the Atlantic side, this will mostly benefit the Aframax crude carriers, while VLCCs could be the beneficiaries on the Pacific Coast, at least in the next few quarters. Once the expanded Trans-Mountain pipeline in western Canada opens (late 2022/early 2023), we expect more Aframax traffic as well.
