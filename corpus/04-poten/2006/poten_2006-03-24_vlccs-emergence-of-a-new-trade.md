# VLCCs - Emergence of a New Trade

VLCCs have been the stallions of the ex-Arabian Gulf crude trade for nearly 40 years. They are finding a new backhaul requirement in an emerging dirty products trade- moving fuel oil from West to East. The driver is China. The following chart shows the growth of China's economy and consumption of fuel oil.

Fuel Oil Consumption It Isn't Consumption, It's Imports Domestic consumption in China has increased from 33.3 million tons to 47.4 million tons between 1998 and 2005 whereas domestic production has only risen from 18.3 million tons to 23.3 million tons. ### Exports

Imports China's growth in fuel oil imports, particularly since 2002, has mopped up the supply of fuel oil East of Suez. The key West of Suez exporter to meet China's demand is Russia/FSU. Smaller volumes of West of Suez fuel oil come from the US Gulf and Venezuela. The chart on the next page shows Black and Baltic Sea exports of fuel oil. Clearly much of the incremental growth in Russian fuel oil exports is from the Baltic Sea. Exports from the Black Sea are commonly carried in Aframax vessels as VLCCs are restricted from transiting the Bosporus. Hence the role of VLCCs is limited to exports from the Baltic Sea via ship-to-ship transfer in the North Sea or transshipment in Rotterdam.

### Baltic Sea

Black Sea VLCCs and Fuel Oil Transport Double hull tankers must be used for fuel oil movements in European waters. But double hull tankers also obviate the need for heating. Many fuel oil cargoes from Baltic Sea ports are carried to offshore locations in the North Sea for somewhat lengthy ship-to-ship transfers to load a VLCC. Alternatively, VLCCs can load fuel oil at a terminal in Rotterdam. Another terminal is under construction that will also be able to load VLCCs with full cargoes of fuel oil. Thus a VLCC discharging crude oil in one Rotterdam terminal can switch terminals to take on a full cargo of fuel oil. Delays associated with VLCC discharges of fuel oil cargoes were also once lengthy and costly affairs involving transshipment from Singapore and/or lightering in Southeast Asian waters. Two fuel oil terminals are being built in China that can directly accept VLCCs. A third terminal may follow. With these improvements in terminals, fuel oil couldn't be a better fit as a backhaul cargo for crude oil from the Arabian Gulf.

### VLCCs Won the Race

The average cost of moving fuel oil from the UKC to the Far East in 2005 was $30 per ton on Aframaxes, $22 per ton on Suezmaxes, and $16 per ton on VLCCs. Continuing improvements of ports and terminals to accommodate VLCC shipments of fuel oil could make the VLCC even more economically attractive for this trade. The following table shows that VLCCs have already gained the upper hand in this trade. Since 2004, VLCCs accounted for 60% of all spot fixtures for larger sized tankers on this trade.
