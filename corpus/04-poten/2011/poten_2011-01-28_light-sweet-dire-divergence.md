# Light Sweet Dire Divergence

Since the start of the New Year, a sharp divergence in the spread between Light Sweet West Texas Intermediate crude (better known as WTI) and Brent crude oil price has been a topic of heated conversation and debate in the trading community. With the Brent premium this morning hitting an all‐time high in excess of $12 per barrel to the often‐quoted WTI contract price, a host of questions are being raised from the physical relevance of these contracts to the potential impact on the world economy. A look back at the occurrence of this relatively new phenomenon, the factors, and what it means for the tanker markets, reveals some interesting trends. Readers beware, the results aren't pretty.

### WTI/Brent Spread

Looking at the historical divergence between the WTI and Brent contracts on the chart above, it is easy to see why the recent spike has much of the trading world abuzz with an array of explanations and analysis as to what's causing the disparity. The $12.27 per barrel spread recorded this morning represents the highest premium Brent has ever traded to WTI. Both crude oils have similar characteristics and represent major benchmarks off which the majority of all crude oils are priced. Due to its low sulfur content and lighter characteristics, WTI has historically traded at a slight premium to Brent. This relationship began to change in 2007 when, for the first time, the Brent contract traded at a premium to WTI for an extended period. 2008 Saw a return to 'normalcy' in the spread with WTI once again trading at a premium. The price spread first broke $10 per barrel in the last days of 2008 during the height of the financial crisis. Although the spreads narrowed once more, with WTI again trading at a premium to Brent through much of the 2009‐2010 timeframe, the frequency and consistency with which Brent traded at a premium had been unprecedented.

### Cushing's Cushion

Common factors cited for the price divergence most often include several of the following logical reasons. Increased Canadian oil sands exports are likely creating a supply glut and bottlenecks at the NYMEX delivery point in Cushing, Oklahoma for WTI, depressing prices. Another fundamental factor cited has been the waning supplies of physical North Sea crude oil. Recently, and perhaps most interestingly, certain news sources have cited "trading games" implying that specific Brent physical oil traders are taking strategic positions by purchasing up a large number of front‐month North Sea cargoes in an attempt to influence prices. What none of these explanations seems to address is why, if there is such a dramatic spread between the two contracts, haven't enterprising arbitrage‐minded traders utilized a depressed shipping market to capitalize on such an extreme price differential?

WTI/Brent Spread Looking at a comparison of the Poten Tanker Index' historical reaction to the price spreads in the above graph hardly leads one to conclude the spread has resulted in meaningful shipping activity in the form of sustained healthy freight rates. In fact, given the resulting collapse of shipping rates following the 2008‐2009 jump in the Brent‐WTI spread, and the current depressed tanker market levels in the face of the current spike, the picture again seems to be pointing to a bleak outlook if the arbitrage (or lack thereof) is any indicator of future tanker market health.

So why haven't these arbitrage opportunities created a more robust tanker market? Given the spikes in the spread, its possible traders haven't had the window of opportunity to take advantage of the price arbitrage. Another answer may lie in the arguments surrounding which benchmark is a more accurate barometer for the physical oil price. The Brent crude oil benchmark currently represents the pricing benchmark for over 65% of the world's traded physical crude oil. The WTI contract represents a pricing benchmark for about 30% of the world's traded physical crude oil, while physical supplies of WTI are quite scarce. It should be noted, most of the crude oil being priced off the WTI contract is already trading at a significant premium to the contract itself implying that the market has already compensated for WTI's lack of physical relevance. This could explain why shipping rates have remained depressed in the face of such a dramatic price discrepancy between the two contracts. It would also support the growing chorus of analysts, traders, and pundits calling for the Brent contract to be more indicative of fundamental demand for physical crude oil (versus speculative demand for paper WTI contracts). Lastly, if this dynamic exists, it certainly calls into question the relevance of the WTI price in the first place. $100 per barrel physical oil? We're already there. $86 per barrel oil? Maybe in the paper markets - but don't count on delivery.

### Conclusive Correlations?

Given the past correlations between the WTI/Brent spread and tanker rates, it makes it difficult to get excited about tanker rate prospects in the face of the dramatic spreads the markets are again seeing. Regardless of whether supply gluts at Cushing, supply declines in Brent, or a more conspiratorial trading scheme is at the heart of the divergence, it does not appear shipping rates are experiencing any revitalization in the face of a seemingly blatant arbitrage opportunity. Although the crystal ball is a little hazy these days, and a brewing Suez crisis is begging to destroy any bearish outlook on shipping rates, it's probably safe to say the current WTI/Brent spreads may not carry with them a meaningful recovery in tanker demand.
