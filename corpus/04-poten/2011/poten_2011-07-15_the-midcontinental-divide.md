# The Mid‐Continental Divide

A recent pipeline spill into Montana's Yellowstone River has added fuel to the fire surrounding the TransCanada Keystone pipeline expansion. Opponents to the project have been vocal on both sides of the American‐Canadian border for various reasons, environmental concerns chief among them. Record high crude oil inventories at Cushing, Oklahoma and slack demand from American consumers, however, have called into question the real need for the pipeline expansion and the underlying motives of those angling for its completion, especially on the American side. True, oil prices are still relatively high, but not from lack of supply. American markets are awash in expensive crude oil, but adding more to the trough will not likely mitigate the price pressure anytime soon.

The TransCanada Keystone pipeline, which has been planned in four phases as seen in the map below, was designed to move crude oil produced from Alberta's oil sands to markets in the United States.

In June 2010 TransCanada commenced commercial operation of the first phase of the Keystone Pipeline System, which brings crude oil directly from Hardisty, Alberta to market hubs in the U.S. Midwest. Phase II, a 36‐inch pipeline connecting Steele City, Nebraska to the storage and distribution facilities at Cushing, Oklahoma came online in February of this year. Phases III and IV, if completed, plan to ultimately connect this existing infrastructure to US Gulf coast refiners, expanding the market for Canadian oil sands production. The proposed expansions received Canadian regulatory approval in 2010, and prior to the spill at Yellowstone, it was anticipated that regulatory approval from the US Department of State would follow by the end of this year.

### Canadian Bacon Barrel Politics

Enhancement of American energy security has been cited as the paramount reason for expanding Canadian crude oil export capacity to the United States. Canada is already the single largest supplier of imported crude to the United States and the country's oil sands contain the world's second largest oil reserves, after Saudi Arabia. Waning crude oil production out of Mexico does create some cause for concern, but it is not clear that America actually requires more crude oil barrels delivered to inland markets, or moreover into storage tanks.

Crude oil stocks at Cushing, Oklahoma have averaged about 25 million barrels since January 2005. During the high crude oil price environment seen in 2008, stocks at Cushing were about 36% lower than this figure, averaging about 16 million barrels over the course of that year. As the rules of supply and demand would dictate, the price of West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil rose as stocks fell. In contradiction, the price of WTI climbed steadily over the first five months of 2011 even as stocks at Cushing grew to record highs in excess of 40 million barrels, as seen in the chart below.

In the Poten opinion Light Sweet Dire Divergence , the reversal in pricing structure among WTI and Brent was addressed as WTI's discount to Brent soared to $12 per barrel. Now at a $20 discount per barrel, the structure remains intact. It should be noted that the actual physical market for WTI is completely dependent on demand from adjacent refiners. Still, WTI seems to represent the "true" price of crude oil in the minds of the American public, and legislators are likely to "green light" projects that keep the masses at ease.

Cynics among us may see the hoarding of all types of barrels at Cushing as being driven by a desire to exercise control over the perception of global energy prices. As we delve deeper into the third quarter of 2011, continued downward pressure on WTI, artificial or otherwise, may be Washington's way of quelling anxious minds about inflation as we move towards a third round of quantitative easing (QE3) or its equivalent (see Inflation Everywhere But (in Tanker Earnings) Not a Drop to Drink ).

Should the Keystone pipeline expansion come to full fruition and extend to the US Gulf, shipping may be impacted as Canadian supplies displace those from farther afield. But as the events of this year have taught us (see OPEC Pickle ), not all crude oils are created equal. In theory, price and quality would dictate where crude oil flows. But given the Obama Administration's recent actions , it would be wise to anticipate erratic behavior. At least in the near term, neighborly back scratching between the United States and her neighbor to the north indicates politics as usual.
