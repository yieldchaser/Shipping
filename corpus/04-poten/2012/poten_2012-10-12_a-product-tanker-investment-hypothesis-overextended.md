# A Product Tanker Investment Hypothesis, Overextended

### IEA makes case for

Today's release of the long-awaited Medium-term Oil Market Report (MTOMR) by the International Energy Agency (IEA) featured a more relaxed oil market forecast for the next five years -- driven by weaker global demand growth, stronger North American supply and rising Iraqi production capacity. The agency went to great lengths to dramatise a "deeply transformed" global oil map, as a rebalancing of regional supply and demand would drive crude trade flows lower, but boost longer-haul product movements. Of course, the market has embraced this product tanker investment hypothesis for years, and the fundamentals remain still intact, so this is hardly new news. Still, the IEA's assertion that China would become a "new powerhouse in product exports" may be a fanciful overextension of this hypothesis. .

...but China as "export powerhouse" is a stretch The expected rapid expansion of export-oriented refining capacity in the Middle East and India should drive longer-haul product flows and product tanker demand, but this premise has its limitations. Simply, the refining industry faces too much capacity.

Current Poten forecasts for 2.3 mbpd of refinery shutdowns during 2013-17 provide a 1.3 mbpd decline in European capacity, or 9%, and a 0.9 mbpd, or 12%, cut in OECD Pacific capacity during 2013-17. Powerful forces are driving OECD refining rationalisation -- declining demand, tighter environmental regulations, aging infrastructure, and newer, more complex and competitive refineries in non-OECD locations -- but these have limitations. During the 2011-12 Atlantic Basin refining purge, the market discovered that refining capacity is difficult to kill. In addition to local government interference, new investors emerged when refinery prices collapsed to more popular price points, allowing refineries once left for dead to re-emerge under new ownership.

### Chinese as pragmatists?

So, the IEA arguing that China would become a "new powerhouse in product exports" was surprising. This assertion is based upon the agency's lower demand forecasts for China, which have sliced 2016 demand estimates by 1.14 mbpd from their June 2011 MTOMR. It also relies upon the assumption that the Chinese will continue to build refineries, regardless of the eroding global and domestic economics of additional refining capacity.

Build it and they will export Conversely, the Chinese are more likely to maintain the product equilibrium shown in the chart on the previous page, in which Chinese refiners have been modest net exporters of transportation fuel for the past four years. Chinese distillation and upgrading capacity additions should continue to pace strong domestic mid-distillate demand, providing a gradual rise in gasoline surpluses. While AG and Indian refining capacity should help meet a widening European structural deficit in mid-distillates, local gasoline demand growth in those regions limit should export potential. China has a growing role in meeting an expanding gasoline deficit in non-OECD Asia --supporting intra-regional MR demand -- but calls for an export powerhouse are pre-mature.

...Chinese gasoline exports welcome The demand fundamentals supporting the product tanker investment hypothesis remain in place, but overextending this premise to new geographies, when the refining industry faces severe threats to profitability, is unsettling. The product tanker investment case faces its own potential threat from renewed MR ordering and "eco-design" marketing-- it does not need an additional ordering rationale from an illusory export potential.
