# Tanker Stocks Running Aground

Investors around the world are collectively holding their breath watching the values of equities deteriorate daily. The credit crisis has transcended into a rapid slide in the broader equity indexes and stock prices are getting crushed by the minute. Shipping equities have been swept up in the tide, despite how these companies are performing in the freight markets. The chart below shows the closing equity prices for a few of the major publicly traded shipowners relative to their prices on September 10, 2008. Although each of these companies may adopt a different approach to using debt, charter coverage (spot versus term) and clientele (counter party risk), it seems that the stock market appears to be making little distinction.

Value is in the eye of the beholder At present, the market capitalization of many tanker stocks are at levels far less than what the physical value of the ships would suggest. Net asset value is defined as the price of the ships net of any debt. As such, net asset value is somewhat subjective and can be difficult to determine - particularly in a market that is seeing little or no transactions. The market price of anything can be defined as the level at which there is a willing buyer and a willing seller. Today, it seems the inactivity levels have led to bearish sentiments where asset prices are concerned, but these price levels remain untested to a large extent.

Many companies today are perceived as trading at or below net asset value. Add in the fact that it is unlikely many public companies will see an interruption in cash flow. Term coverage for the public shipping companies enables a visible and guaranteed flow of income for the duration of the charter period - a fact it would appear Wall Street has temporarily forgotten. For example, General Maritime has 23 tankers mixed between Suezmaxes and Aframaxes. Just looking at the Suezmax fleet alone, the 11 owned ships could have an average value of around $100 million per ship, or about one billion for just this part of the fleet. Subtract debt, and the implication is that the Aframax fleet is free! The stock market is completely discounting the term coverage strategy for these ships as General Maritime has over 70% of the fleet on charter. The next move Some shipowners for the time being appear to be waiting on the side lines, many with cash on-hand to act. Private shipowners may have the opportunity to bargain hunt if the public markets become even weaker.

Alternatively, we can expect to see more consolidation in the public companies. Share repurchases, special dividends or privatization of these companies could become even more common if the equity prices continue to slide. Companies that privatize or buy back stocks are effectively able to buy tankers (or tanker equivalents in stock shares) at cheaper prices than the assessed asset price levels.

Let us not forget, spot market rates have stayed relatively firm further helping the cash positions of companies with spot market exposure. Further still, the past quarter of freight rates could show some of the best earnings for shipping companies yet! Cash is still flowing, despite credit being on hold. The tanker stocks are getting caught in the general down-draft, but the cash flow and earnings outlook are still good. Seems like a good time to buy.
