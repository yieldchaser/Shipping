# Blindsided by Demand

Autumn in the northeastern US is marked by a symphony of swirling, multicolored leaves shed from their host trees. But lately, leaves are not the only things that are falling. Petroleum demand in the US, which has slowly withered since the end of last year, entered a slippery decline stage this past September - well after July's peak in crude oil's price. How long demand will languish at present levels or will it sling-shot back now that prices have fallen are questions perplexing the oil industry.

While there is some suggestion in the chart below that lately the nation's consumption - as represented by product supplied - may be reacting to lower prices by partially recovering, only after more time passes will the real impact of lower prices become known.

### Fill It Up Please!

US gasoline demand early in 2008 was typical of levels seen in recent years. Since this past May, however, demand has eroded to below or, at best, near 2005's levels. Lately there has been some sense of returning demand - likely spurred by the collapse of gasoline's price. The clouds of uncertainty hovering over the nation's economy, though, could be seen as a caution light by some motorists wishing to return to the carefree days of old.

### Send Us Your Exports, Please!

US imports of gasoline stocks anticipate seasonal rises and falls in its demand. Expectations of late winter/early spring price arbitrage between markets sow the seeds for added springtime gasoline movements into the US. But as the chart below suggests, there seems to exist a structural bottom to the level of the US' year-round imports. Gasoline's recent fall in demand was followed by a drop in imports. Whether or not imports continue to drop depends on the perceptions of recovery.

Even in times of more vibrant economic activity, US gasoline imports in the late fall and early winter typically are not strong. But they are not necessarily weak, either. The structural bottom to the level of these imports usually tends to keep clean tankers busy in this trade. If lower prices soon spur an unexpected uptick in US gasoline demand, tankers many be called on to supply some of this unforeseen volume if the price arbitrage between markets supports freight costs and also rewards risk. In these shifting times, it is prudent for both owners and charterers to be aware of the potential to be blindsided by either abruptly rising or falling demand.
