# Tankers Still Afloat

The shipping markets tend to ebb and flow with the health and cycles of the world economy and trade. While it is sometimes difficult to determine the root causes of the bursts in the market, a downward correction in freight rates can be felt quickly especially if there is a surplus of tonnage. Sudden changes in demand and consumption patterns can convert a well supplied market into one with a capacity glut to the detriment of shipowners. Various shipping sectors - tankers, containers, dry bulk, and gas - will experience peaks and troughs at different points for a variety of reasons and durations. One divergence present in today's market is the freight rate slide in the dry bulk market versus the relative steadiness of the tanker market. It appears that dry bulk shipping has fallen victim to a few of the classic symptoms of a market bubble. Demand for dry bulk commodities grew creating congestion at ports with insufficient infrastructure. Delays helped to further tighten supply in the market as ships were forced to wait longer to load and discharge cargoes. Add in the fact that freight rates escalated as charters were arranged back-to- back and ships were flipped among different charterers. As credit tightened, these pressures rapidly unwound to reveal lower charter rates combined with a dearth of cargoes in the market.

It stands to reason that the physical demand for commodities still remains in the dry bulk sector; however the pop in the bulker shipping bubble has created some unwarranted jitters rippling across the tanker market. The characteristics confronting the dry bulk market could potentially develop in other shipping sectors, however the tanker market seems to have different fundamentals to help insulate from insolvency.

### Key Driving Factors

Demand for oil continues to grow. Although demand figures have been revised downward recently due to the slowing global economy, analysts still expect growth in consumption to be between 300,000 to 600,000 barrels per day next year.

Hence the likelihood of a daisy chain of defaults is quite small. Major oil companies and reputable traders continue to provide stability to the tanker market.

General tightening in credit creates a unique opportunity for well- balanced companies to use cash on hand to buy assets from weaker competitors. Mergers, acquisitions and divestment of assets are on the horizon and will lead to further consolidation across the tanker industry.

While it is difficult to say what the exact results of the financial crisis will yield for the tanker market, there are sufficient fundamentals to suggest the market will remain relatively strong in the medium-term.
