# Are Russian Barrels Getting Out Of Favor

### Demand for Russian crude is cooling as sanctions tighten

When Russia invaded Ukraine a little more than two years ago, the reaction from many Western companies and governments was swift. They slapped a multitude of sanctions on the Kremlin regime and vowed to stop importing Russian crude oil and refined products. The G7 sanctions initiated a massive reshuffling of crude oil trade flows as Russia was forced to look for alternative customers for their oil. Initially, market observers expected that it would be challenging for Russia to find alternative clients for their oil given their massive dependence on Europe for both oil and refined products. This proved not to be the case. Russia offered significant discounts and price sensitive buyers such as India, China and Turkey were happy to take advantage of the situation and ramped up purchases. India in particular, went from a minor buyer of Russian crude prior to the invasion to the largest customer of the Kremlin in 2022 and 2023. Turkey also boosted its imports, mainly of Urals while China remained a large customer.

The G7 price cap, which started in December 2022 for crude oil, was meant to force Russia to sell its oil for a discount (below $60/barrel) or lose the ability to use Western shipping services for its exports. However, the G7 price cap was only loosely enforced, and many western owners continued to transport Russian barrels. At the same time, throughout 2022 and into 2023, Russia was able to build up a large dark fleet to circumvent Western sanctions and maintain its export potential.

In the fall of 2023, US and EU authorities started to clamp down on Western shipowners and service providers which were suspected of not complying with the price cap. This led to an exodus of Western owners from the trade, making Russia more reliant on the dark fleet. However, around the same time, G7 countries started scrutinizing (and sanctioning) an increasing number of vessels involved in the transportation of Russian crude oil. This enforcement clampdown has created increasing problems for both Russia and its key customers.

India in particular seems to be keen to reduce their reliance on Russian oil. Indian imports of Russia's Urals grade peaked in Mid-2023 and have gradually declined since then. Middle Eastern suppliers such as Iraq, Saudi Arabia and the UAE regained some of the market share they lost in 2022. India also reduced deliveries of ESPO barrels from Kozmino. However, the biggest relative decline has been in the shipments of Sokol crude to India. After the Sakhalin 1 project started up again in September/October 2022, India became the dominant buyer of this high quality (light-sweet) crude, buying the vast majority of its output. However, this all changed in November 2023, when the US slapped sanctions on several Russian tankers that were involved in these shipments. Since then, Russia has had a hard time delivering Sokol crude to Indian refiners, which have become increasingly wary of Russian crude because of increasing sanctions pressure. Indian refiners have also complained that Russian crudes have become less competitive.

The problems in the Red Sea, where Houthi rebels are targeting commercial ships have boosted tanker rates and shipping distances, since oil from the Atlantic Basin is increasingly being transported the long way around the Cape of Good Hope. This has also increased the delivered cost of Russian barrels from the Black Sea and the Baltic.

The changes in buying behaviour from Turkey and China are less dramatic, but even these buyers are more reluctant to go all-in on Russian crude. Several Turkish owners have left the trade and a Turkish Mediterranean terminal announced this week that they will stop handling Russian crude, reportedly as a result of increased sanctions pressure from the United States.

The Russians are trying to counter these developments by looking for alternative buyers. A VLCC recently delivered a cargo of Russian crude to Venezuela and the Kremlin has also delivered barrels to buyers in Pakistan, Ghana and Brunei. However, it will be a challenge to replace the volumes of India and Turkey if these countries decide to cut back their purchases. Much higher discounts may be needed or more Russian crude risks being stranded.
