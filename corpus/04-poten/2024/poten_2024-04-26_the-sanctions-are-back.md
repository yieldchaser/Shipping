# The Sanctions Are Back

### US reverses relief of sanctions on Venezuela

Back in October 2023, the U.S. Treasury Department announced that it would ease sanctions against companies that trade in Venezuelan oil or invest in the country for an initial period of six months. The main reason for the partial relief of sanctions was to encourage Venezuela's President Nicolas Maduro to enable democratic elections to take place in July 2024. Part of the agreement was that opposition candidate would be allowed to run for president and that political prisoners would be released. Unfortunately, Mr. Maduro did not follow through on these commitments and on April 18, sanctions relief was reversed. In this opinion we will discuss what happened during this brief period of sanctions relief and what will likely happen at the end of May, when the 45-day wind-down period expires.

When sanctions relief was announced six months ago, we did not forecast a significant increase in Venezuela's oil output. Rehabilitating the country's oil infrastructure would require a lot of work and money, much more than what could be achieved in a six-month period. However, we did expect a shift in the destinations of Venezuela's exports and - related to that - a change in the vessel segments that would be utilized. This is largely what happened. Venezuela's crude oil exports averaged about 580,000 b/d in the 12 months prior to the sanctions relief. In the 6 months since, exports were 540,000 b/d. From November 2023 through March 2024, Venezuelan exports to China declined, with India picking up most of these barrels. The United States continued to be a major destination due to the Chevron license. Spain and Italy also imported Venezuelan oil.

In terms of vessel classes, the changes were not so dramatic. The US trade continued to employ mostly Aframaxes, while the customers in Europe are using a combination of Aframax and Suezmax vessels. VLCCs are the vessel of choice for the long- haul trips to Asia, both China and India. Changes in destinations from China to India therefore had a limited impact on VLCC employment, although the sailing distance to India is shorter than to China, so there is a difference in ton-mile demand.

Prior to October 2023, the vast majority of Venezuelan exports went to China, with small volumes going to Cuba and Spain. In November 2022, the U.S. Treasury Department issued a license, authorizing Chevron to resume "limited natural resource extraction operations in Venezuela." As a result, Chevron started importing Venezuelan crude oil into the US again for the first time since 2019. US imports grew from 77,000 b/d in January 2023 to 185,000 b/d in September. However, when the sanctions were lifted in October 2023, many other customers were interested in buying crude from the state-owned oil company PDVSA as well. Customers from all over the world (except Russia) were free to do oil business in Venezuela.

Now that the sanctions have snapped back, some companies have been caught off guard. One example is the Polish refiner Orlen. According to a report from Reuters, Orlen has scrapped contracts to buy Venezuelan oil after losing millions of U.S. dollars in prepayments for cargoes that haven't been loaded. Orlen does not think it will receive the oil prior to the expiration of the 45-day wind-down period.

What will happen now that the sanctions are back? Two key developments are likely. First, more oil will flow to China as other buyers will disappear from the market and Venezuelan oil will be available for a discount. Customers in India will probably stop importing Venezuelan oil again, not wanting to run afoul of US sanctions. Another development will be a change in vessel employment. During the six months of sanctions relief, Venezuelan oil could be moved by mainstream owners, pushing the dark fleet out of the trades. However, since sanctions are back, clandestine tankers will again be the vessels of choice.

Some of the dark vessels that were employed in the Venezuelan trades transferred to Russian business, but going forward more dark fleet VLCCs will be needed to move Venezuelan oil to China. Unfortunately, this will reduce long-haul employment opportunities for the mainstream tanker fleet. However, the volumes from Venezuela are rather limited so the impact on the market will be muted.
