# Very Large Clean Carriers

### Unusual markets create unusual trades

The tanker industry has a reputation for being a very traditional business. Compared to other industries there has not been a lot of innovation in the way crude oil and petroleum products are moved. This is not a negative judgement. It is a testament to the fact that the global deep-sea shipping markets are already highly efficient and offer a very competitive product. Every now and then, however, there are innovations, sometimes technological, sometimes in business practices. One of those innovations is the use of large crude oil tankers to move refined products. This practice was regularly utilized for newbuilding tankers, transporting refined products on their maiden voyage out of the shipyard. However, now ship owners and operators have started to "clean up" tankers that traditionally traded crude oil and utilized them to transport refined products. What caused this development, what is involved and is this a temporary phenomenon, or is this operational innovation here to stay?

Why would VLCCs and Suezmax crude oil tankers, which are able to move 2 million and 1 million barrels of cargo be employed in the clean products trades? The reason is simple. Bigger ships deliver better economies of scale and the biggest ships would theoretically provide the lowest per barrel transportation costs. That's why our industry is always driven towards bigger ships. However, there are limitations to using these large tankers for clean petroleum products. Many product export and import ports are not equipped to handle these giant tankers. Furthermore, the size of a VLCC cargo is three to five times the size of a standard LR1 or LR2 parcel and many export and import terminals don't have the tankage to deal with such large cargoes. This means that Ship-To-Ship transfers may be required on one or both sides of the voyage, increasing the length and the cost of the voyage. However, despite these restrictions, VLCCs and Suezmaxes are increasingly being utilized for clean cargoes.

A contributing factor to the shift toward clean product transportation is the relative underperformance of crude oil tankers (in particular VLCCs) in the freight market relative to clean product tankers. The Russia - Ukraine war and the conflict in the Middle East, boosted ton-mile demand for product tankers. The same development as well as the cutbacks in Middle East OPEC production in combination with a slowdown in the Chinese economy caused the large crude tankers, in particular the VLCCs, to underperform. High freight rates for product tankers and lower earnings potential for VLCCs created the opportunity of using (some of) these large tankers in the clean product trades. The low level of newbuilding deliveries also deprived the market of the opportunity to utilize crude tankers for clean products on their maiden voyage out of the yard.

Enabling a VLCC that has been moving crude oil to switch to clean products is not straightforward. It is time consuming and expensive. The charterer will typically use a time-chartered ship for such a voyage. If the entire voyage, including about 30 days for clean-up, takes around 90 days, we estimate the hire at $4 Million (at $45,000/day). Adding to that the costs for fuel and chemicals, de-slopping charges, the additional manpower cost, as well as bunkers and lightering costs would bring the total cost of the voyage to $7.0 M.

However, a quick back-of-the-envelope calculation illustrates that the savings on freight are so high that (at least at the moment) it is worth it. In today's market, moving a 90,000 Metric Ton (MT) cargo of diesel on an LR2 from the Arabian Gulf to the UKC costs $4.4 million, or $49/ton. This is almost double the cost of the VLCC, which is $7 million for 280,000 tons, or $25/ton.

Obviously, switching from product tankers to (cleaned) crude tankers is not for everyone or every cargo. The vessels engaged in this trade tend to be modern VLCCs. The charterers involved are mostly traders who control the whole voyage and use their own tonnage. The products involved are mostly middle distillates, such as diesel and gasoil. The potential risk of contamination discourages the use of cleaned up VLCCs for gasoline or jet fuel.

This freight arbitrage will likely continue until the growing number of cleaned up crude tankers tips the balance back in favor of the dedicated product fleet and/or a pickup in oil movements pulls the crude fleet back in their core trades.
