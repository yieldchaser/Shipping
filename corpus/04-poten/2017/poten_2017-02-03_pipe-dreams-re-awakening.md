# Pipe Dreams Re Awakening

### What will these pipelines mean for the U.S. crude trade?

On January 24, in his first week in office, President Trump issued several memos and an executive order aimed at streamlining and expediting approvals for midstream infrastructure (like pipelines). Two of the projects that President Trump would like to see approved (fulfilling some of his election campaign promises) are the Keystone XL Pipeline ("KXL") and the Dakota Access Pipeline ("DAPL").

However, these documents are just a first step. In one of the memos, President Trump asks TransCanada to "promptly" reapply for the cross-border permit and directs the State Department to issue a decision within 60 days. TransCanada did respond quickly and two days later re-submitted a presidential permit application to the U.S. Department of State. This means that there could be a decision before the end of March. As a comparison, TransCanada initially asked the U.S. State Department for approval in 2008 and President Obama ultimately rejected the project in 2015, 7 years later.

The proposed Keystone XL Pipeline is a 1,664 mile crude oil pipeline, beginning in Hardisty, Alberta (Canada) and extending south to Steel City, Nebraska, where it will connect to the existing Keystone Pipeline System. It will have the capacity to transport 830,000 barrels per day.

Even if the State Department approves KXL, other hurdles remain for this pipeline. The project is still waiting for approval from Nebraska state regulators for its last proposed route (as of 2015) and many of the landowners in that state are vehemently opposed to KXL. President Trump's new regulations also mandate the use of American steel for new pipeline construction. The implications of this new mandate on the cost and/or construction time of the pipeline are not clear at this point. The CEO of TransCanada Russ Girling indicated that most of the materials for KXL have already been purchased and were manufactured in North America.

The latest developments regarding the DAPL are centered on the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers which is reviewing an easement that is necessary for the construction of the pipeline. President Trump's January 24 memo encouraged the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to expedite the review and approval process. If granted, the easement would allow the pipeline to cross under a federally controlled section of the Missouri River just north of the Standing Rock Sioux Reservation in North Dakota. This is the last approval needed to complete the construction of the project. Recent press reports seem to suggest that the approval is imminent.

The DAPL, which has a capacity of 570,000 b/d, is a 1,172-mile underground pipeline that extends from the Bakken/Three Forks production area in North Dakota to Patoka in Illinois. According to the proponents, the pipeline can eliminate 500-740 rails cars and 250+ trucks that are needed to transport crude oil every day. The DAPL is being built by Energy Transfer Partners.

The implications of the revival of these pipeline projects for North American oil balances and the crude oil tanker trades could be significant. By replacing expensive rail movements, DAPL will increase the physical and commercial availability of light sweet Bakken crude on the U.S. East Coast, potentially backing out foreign imports from the Atlantic Basin (especially if a " Border Adjustment Tax" will make imported oil more expensive*).

When completed, Keystone XL will significantly increase access to U.S. Gulf refiners for the producers of Canadian heavy crude oil. Canadian crude oil could also be exported to other countries via U.S. Gulf ports. This could become one of the most realistic export options for Canadian producers.

Seaborne export options from Canada are increasingly limited, especially for larger vessels. In 2015, the new Canadian government of Prime Minister Trudeau imposed a ban on oil tanker traffic on the North Coast of British Columbia, effectively killing the 525,000 barrels per day Enbridge Northern Gateway Pipeline, which was to run from Bruderheim, Alberta to the VLCC capable port of Kitimat, British Columbia. On November 29, 2016, the Canadian government officially rejected Northern Gateway. Recently, the potential export pipeline to Saint John (a VLCC port on Canada's East Coast), TransCanada's 1.1 million barrels per day Energy East Pipeline also hit a snag. Canada's energy regulator said that the review of this pipeline will need to start again from the beginning due to irregularities during the previous review process. This will push the project back at least two years. The revival of the Keystone XL pipeline has also diminished the importance of Energy East for TransCanada.
