# Are We Nearing a Turning Point?

China is not just driving the shipping market to historical highs. Its demand for raw material imports has driven up the prices of oil, steel, and copper to record levels. China has become the world's largest exporter of consumer goods. China is widely blamed for the loss of jobs in the United States through outsourcing of manufacturing activities. But the truth of the matter is that workers around the world involved with consumer goods have been affected by China including Mexico, Jamaica, and nations in Southeast Asia. On the other hand, workers employed in manufacturing capital goods (electricity generators, machinery and equipment) have benefited by China's economic explosion. Economic activity in Germany and Japan has been bolstered by greatly increased capital goods exports to China. U.S. exports to China of steel and equipment are also rising. China's economy is now expanding at its highest rate ever of 9.5% during the first 9 months of 2004, implying a record-shattering 12% plus annual growth rate. At that rate of compound growth, China's economy doubles in about six years, which means a doubling of energy (oil) demand, steel demand, and demand for every other industrial commodity.

Things Never Stay the Same Forever However, this doubling of demand assumes a straight line extrapolation of current trends. There is something in this world we can depend on: straight line extrapolations are the worst possible assumption one can make in a forecast.

Economies and industries move in cycles: what goes up eventually comes down and what comes down eventually goes up. The tanker industry is no stranger to cycles. Focusing on China, one cannot help but take a little romp down memory lane when the Asian economies, growing year after year, stumbled in 1997/1998, much to the astonishment by economists and businesspeople alike as a "bolt out of the blue." The following quote of a look back at the Southeast Asian economic crisis was published in The IMF's Response to the Asian Crisis of January 17, 1999.

The crisis unfolded against the backdrop of several decades of outstanding economic performance in Asia, and the difficulties that the East Asian countries face are not primarily the result of macroeconomic imbalances. Rather, they stemmed from weaknesses in financial systems and, to a lesser extent, governance. A combination of inadequate financial sector supervision, poor assessment and management of financial risk, and the maintenance of relatively fixed exchange rates led banks and corporations to borrow large amounts of international capital, much of it short-term, denominated in foreign currency, and unhedged. As time went on, this inflow of foreign capital tended to be used to finance poorer-quality investments.

Weakness in the financial system, poor governance and supervision, relatively fixed exchange rates that promoted exports, too much foreign currency borrowings invested in poorer-quality assets that did not provide an adequate return- does any of this apply to China? China has recently reported its negative trade balance turning positive by $10 billion. There are those who doubt this alleging that some Chinese exporters tend to exaggerate their exports to garner more tax credits to avoid taxation and some Chinese importers smuggle in goods to escape custom duties. Furthermore China is paying record high prices for its oil, copper, steel, iron ore, coal imports plus record high shipping costs. The principal source of income is consumer goods sold in the United States, ultimately a finite market- are the numbers adding up? Does anyone know?

### History Repeating Itself?

The first signal that the imposing financial façade of Asia had a weak foundation was the bankruptcy of the Hanbo Steel in Korea in January 1997. In February, Somprasong was the first Thai company to miss its foreign debt payments. This was proven wrong soon thereafter when the Malaysian central bank tried to restrict property and stock loans to stem a growing crisis. Fears of the Asian Contagion spreading to China, Japan, India, South America, Russia and perhaps North America and Europe emerged by the end of the year.

### Behind a Financial Collapse

Not mentioned in this chronology of events was the reason behind the financial collapse. Was the initial collapse of the Korean steel companies a reflection that too much steel capacity had been built for market demand? Unable to sell sufficient steel in volume and price to pay the debt requirements for building excess capacity would certainly be a cause of their demise. Could the collapse of other financial institutions in Southeast Asia have been loans going into default for building factories of all types whose volume of output could not be absorbed by the world's largest dumping ground for consumer goods, the United States?

### Implications for China

China has a fixed rate of exchange that encourages exports and induces outsourcing by the rest of the world. China's infrastructure to produce consumer goods is growing by leaps and bounds. Much of this is being financed by debt. Is it possible that China, in becoming the world's largest manufacturer of consumer goods, may saturate the market as its neighbors did in 1997? If the world does not consume what is being produced, what happens then? A repeat of the real estate and stock market debacles that occurred in Southeast Asia might be seen again if Chinese factory owners start dumping their real estate and stock holdings to generate the necessary cash to service their debts.

It is clear that China must be at the verge of hitting hard constraints in its emergence as the world's second largest industrial power. The nation is suffering from power shortages, its logistical system is overburdened, and there are signs of civil unrest. The recent interest rate hike, though modest, is an attempt to reign in excess investments in industrial assets and to cool domestic inflation and real estate speculation. It appears dubious that China can double its economy over the next six years without running its ship aground.
