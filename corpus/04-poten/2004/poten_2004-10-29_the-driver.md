# The Driver

Last week we concluded that shifting trade patterns and growth in ton-mile demand did not seem sufficient to explain the run-up in Aframax rates. This week we search for another driver behind tanker rates. The following chart shows the relationship between Arabian Gulf oil exports and VLCC rates.

### Chart: Vlcc Rates vs Middle East Exports

An R-square of 69% can be interpreted to mean that 69% of the variation in Vlcc rates can be explained by variation in Arabian Gulf exports. This relationship is not strong enough to predict Vlcc rates by knowing export volume. But as the above chart suggests, when Arabian Gulf exports increase, so do Vlcc rates and vice versa. An examination of the regression equation suggests that a million bpd change in AG exports will generate a 25 point change in Vlcc rates. Depending on what base rate is assumed for Vlccs, a relatively small change in AG exports has a rather dramatic impact on Vlcc rates. Similar analyses of comparing Suezmax rates with W. Africa exports and Aframax rates with total Caribbean, North Africa, and Indonesia exports shows that there is no relationship (R-square values near zero) between tanker rates and the respective export volumes.

### Vlccs and Suezmaxes

The following shows the relationship between Vlcc and Suezmax rates.

### Comparison of Vlcc & Suezmax WS Rates

75% of the variation in Suezmax rates can be explained by variation in Vlcc rates. While this relation is not strong enough to predict Suezmax rates by knowing Vlcc rates, as shown above, the two rise and fall more or less in unison. The primary reason for this is the sharing of West African cargoes shipped to the U.S. Gulf by Vlccs and Suezmaxes. Here the preferential vessel is Suezmaxes as they require relatively little lightering to enter U.S. Gulf ports. But if Suezmax rates become too high in relation to Vlccs, then there is an economic incentive for charterers to combine two Suezmax cargoes into one Vlcc. If the spread between the two vessel types narrows, then charterers prefer to use Suezmaxes. This switching between the two vessel types depending on their rate spread tends to tie their rate structures together.

### Suezmaxes and Aframaxes

Here the statistical relationship is not quite as significant as between Vlccs and Suezmaxes (R square of 67% versus 75%). But the general pattern is that Aframax rates fluctuate along the same lines as Suezmax rates.

### Comparison of Suezmax & Aframax WS Rates

The connection between Suezmaxes and Aframaxes is their competition for cargoes in the North Sea, Black Sea, and the Mediterranean. Wide rate disparities between Suezmaxes and Aframaxes induce charterers to change their preference for vessel type. Similar to the relationship between Vlccs and Suezmaxes for West African cargoes, this sharing of cargoes ties the Suezmax and Aframax markets together.

### Conclusion

Statistically speaking, we have shown that AG exports influence Vlcc rates, which, in turn, influence Suezmax rates. This cascading process continues with the tying together of the Suezmax and Aframax markets, and the Aframax and Panamax markets. Thus the major driver of the entire tanker industry is AG exports. With Vlcc rates sitting at WS275, we have to conclude that Saudi Arabia is pumping oil at its full capacity along with the other Arabian Gulf producers. This crude is primarily headed to Asia, China specifically. China just reported 9.5% economic growth over a nine-month period, or a mind-boggling annualized rate in excess of 12%. The Chinese government is trying to reign in economic growth lest the nation experiences a busting of the bubble like that which occurred in Southeast Asia in 1997/1998. How well it succeeds will have a major impact on the tanker market, never mind the crude, steel and copper markets. Tanker owners should keep a sharp eye on the driver, AG exports, but in today's world, this means keeping a sharp eye on China.
