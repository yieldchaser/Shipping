# Crazy Train

### The unprecedented gyrations in the freight market

In 1980, legendary rockstar Ozzy Osbourne wrote the heavy metal classic "Crazy Train". The lyrics, referencing the tense geopolitical climate during the cold war, include the phrase "going off the rails on a crazy train". In the current day and age, amid another geopolitical crisis, this might as well refer to the tanker freight market going off the rails. Ever since the start of the Iran war, tanker freight rates have moved in dramatic and unexpected ways, illustrating the impact that the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has had on the market. In this week's Tanker Opinion, we will try to illustrate some these movements, explain why they happened and give some thoughts on what may be next under different scenarios.

When the hostilities broke out on February 28, it took the tanker market by surprise. Iran responded to the bombing campaign of the U.S. and Israel by attacking vessels in and around the Strait of Hormuz. This effectively closed the narrow passageway linking the Persian Gulf with the Pacific Ocean to all commercial traffic, with the exception of Iranian vessels. As a result, a large number of crude oil and product tankers that were inside the Persian Gulf could no longer leave and became stranded. On the other side of the Strait of Hormuz, a sizeable fleet of tankers was anchored in the Gulf of Oman, waiting to enter the Persian Gulf to pick up their cargoes. A few ships have trickled out of the Persian Gulf and others, after being released from previous commitments, have left the area to pursue trading opportunities elsewhere. However, there is still a sizeable fleet of tankers waiting in the area. AIS spoofing has escalated in to a "digital fog of war", which makes it impossible to know how many oil tankers are impacted, but we estimated that at least 150-200 crude oil tankers and a similar number of product carriers are stationary. This has tightened the availability of the mainstream fleet and caused freight rates, which were already elevated prior to outbreak of hostilities (especially for VLCCs), to escalate further. There was one complication, however. The global benchmark VLCC route is the one from Ras Tanura (Saudi Arabia) to Ningbo (China). In a normal market, this is the most liquid and recognized route, and all brokers provide daily assessments. However, when the Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed, this became a largely "theoretical" assessment. With no actual fixtures to go on, rates started to vary significantly among brokers and rates are now assessed through a process of "price discovery", leading to wild swings from one day to the next.

As the conflict dragged on, the global oil markets have tightened considerably, both for crude oil and refined products. Asian countries in particular were very reliant on supplies from the Middle East and are now desperate to procure replacement barrels. Exports from the U.S. Gulf, Caribbean and Latin America are increasingly moving to the Pacific. After most available VLCCs were fixed out of the area, charterers started to target Suezmaxes and even Aframaxes for long- haul trips to Asia. This spiked freight rates in the Caribbean / U.S. Gulf area to eyewatering levels. Reports were circulating of an Aframax fixed for a 3-day U.S. Gulf lightering business for $950,000/day! Another report showed a rate of WS 1,000 for an Aframax loading in Venezuela. The shortage of oil and products has opened up massive arbitrage windows all over the world. However, there are not enough cargoes to meet demand and charterers in Asia are willing to pay whatever it takes to get their hands on available barrels and the tonnage to move it.

Shortages are starting to appear in many countries and governments are trying to reduce oil demand through emergency measures: implementing remote work, lowering speed limits, expanding public transport, and restricting fuel sales, etc. As the conflict continues, more countries will need to act, and the measures will become more draconian.

When the Strait of Hormuz reopens there will likely be significant catch-up demand for a period of time, as production restarts and inventories need to be replenished. This will again boost demand and support freight rates. However, trade flows may change in the future. Countries that have been hit by this oil supply crisis will reevaluate their fuel strategies, diversify their sources of supply and focus more on alternative sources of energy and renewables. Everybody now knows that the Strait of Hormuz can be closed and countries will want to reduce their exposure to that risk.
