# Redefining Refining

### Refining industry continues to change worldwide

The recent discussions about the OPEC and non-OPEC production cuts and their impact on oil prices and U.S. shale oil production are a reminder that the upstream side of the oil industry (exploration and production of crude oil) tends to receive more attention than the downstream side of the business (the process of refining and the distribution of refined oil products). However, the refining industry is going through a lot of changes and faces significant challenges and/or opportunities in the coming years, depending on whom you talk to and which region of the world they operate in. While developing countries in Asia and the Middle East will continue to add distillation capacity in the coming years, mature markets such as the U.S. & Canada and Europe are likely to see only limited capacity additions over the long run. As regional demand shifts, we will see capacity growth in certain parts of the world combined with refinery closures in other parts. All these developments have an impact on the overall health of the refining industry and will change global trade flows in refined petroleum products.

In the developed world, there is little - if any - need for capacity expansion and the activity in the U.S., Europe and Japan is focused on improving efficiencies and maximizing the processing capabilities of existing facilities. Uncertainty about the future of oil prices encouraged a new wave of consolidation, restructuring and divestments in these countries. In 2016, both integrated oil companies and independent refiners have been involved in a number of transactions. Some of the more notable deals in the U.S. include the proposed purchase of Western Refining by Tesoro in November and the acquisition of ExxonMobil's 155,000 b/d Torrance refinery by PBF earlier this year. In March, Saudi Aramco and Shell agreed to separate the assets of their Motiva joint venture. Shell has also been shedding downstream assets in other parts of the world (Japan, Denmark and Australia for example) as part of a strategic realignment. While most of the major integrated oil companies are reducing their global refining footprint, some of the large international trading companies are picking up assets. In February, Gunvor completed the takeover of an 88,000 b/d Kuwait Petroleum refinery in Rotterdam, while Trafigura recently participated in a transaction to buy Essar Oil, which operates a 405,000 b/d refinery in Vadinar, India.

The outlook for refining in 2017 is mixed. In most of the OECD countries, clean product inventories remain elevated. In Europe, both gasoline and distillate inventories are well above their 5-year averages, a result of high domestic refinery utilization and growing diesel imports from Russia, which is revamping its refining system. In the OECD Americas and OECD Asia, distillate stocks are also at the high end of their 5-year range. Global oil demand in the future is expected to be led by gasoline and Asia due to the continuing growth in the number of passenger cars in the region. U.S. product exports are expected to remain strong, in particular gasoline and diesel to Latin America. U.S. refiners benefit from a fall-off in Latin American refinery utilization, in particular in Mexico, Brazil and Venezuela.

In its recently published "World Oil Outlook", OPEC identifies four key trends that will impact the downstream industry. First, a slowdown in the pace of oil demand growth as a result of energy efficiency/conservation mandates which are driven by climate change initiatives. Secondly, demand continues to swing away from mature economies and toward developing Asia. Third, non-crude supplies, such as natural gas liquids (NGLs), gas-to-liquids, coal- to-liquids and biofuels, are expected to continue to grow, leading to a reduction in the proportion of new demand that will be satisfied by traditional refiners. The fourth and last trend identified by OPEC is the ongoing move towards higher quality products (i.e. fuels that meet low and ultra-low sulfur targets), like the recent adoption of the MARPOL Annex VI global sulphur cap mandate for marine fuels. This reinforces the long-term trend towards declining demand for residual fuels.

OPEC expects limited growth in product movements in the medium-term as some new refining capacity is projected to come online in demand centers, limiting requirements for imported barrels. However, in the long-term, product flows should recover in line with product demand growth.
