# More Gravy, Please!

As many Americans downshift their activities in anticipation of the US' observance of Thanksgiving, those more fortunate among them may also turn their thoughts to sharing a festive meal with friends and family. Platters piled high with meats and other foods dripping with gravy typify the celebration. Although a lull in state‐side office work may accompany the coming extended weekend, petroleum commerce and the vessels that facilitate it never rest. So far, though, this year's fourth quarter time charter equivalent (TCE) earnings have not brought tanker owners as much activity or revelry as they might like. While hire rates in some vessel sectors have occasionally jumped upwards in past years over the Thanksgiving holiday, burgeoning oil demand was the gravy that fed those rate spikes. Present conditions find this condiment lacking from the menu.

One tanker sector's prospects - medium‐range product tankers (MRs) - seem to not hinge exclusively on the revival and growth oil demand. Admittedly, while increasing demand would not hurt hire rates in this sector, their market rates mainly reflect the need for trade between regions with a surplus of refined products and those with a deficit. Of course, volume imbalances alone do not create product trade flows. The grease needed to turn the wheels of commerce is the price arbitrage between regions. MRs are typically considered a workhorse for short haul product voyages or for routes where port draft restrictions favor their size.

### Let's Trade

The East Coast of the US has traditionally been the dumping ground for gasoline stocks. During the first eight month's of this year, roughly 800 kBD of motor gasoline blending components and finished gasoline were imported. The ebbs and surges of this trade though are seasonal. Speculative movements are prompted by attractive price arbitrages between regions - sufficiently wide enough to more than pay for freight and other costs as well as for price offsets in product quality. A majority of these flows come from Europe. But since 2007, the reported spot fixtures from the UK‐Continent to the USA have decreased as shown in the chart below. ### UKC to USA

East Coast gasoline demand has not yet recovered to its levels prior to mid‐2008. Additionally, local supply of gasoline has dropped as two refineries seem to have closed permanently and another is shut pending refurbishing by its new owner. But even with the 400 kBD of refining capacity presently sidelined along the East Coast, the activity of MR fixtures has not responded. Greater use of vessels larger than this size range and possibly increased use of controlled tonnage are the likely drivers behind this trend. Movements of clean products from the USA to the UK‐Continent on MRs are best characterized as being erratic. These flows are usually low sulfur diesel stocks pulled out of the States by more attractive prices elsewhere.

In the short‐term, East Coast gasoline demand seems poised to slowly recover, but as the portion of higher fuel efficiency vehicles in the auto fleet grows, its revival may be relatively short‐lived. Weighing projections of future East Coast gasoline demand against its domestic supply as a means of gauging the potential for imports - especially those from Europe - appears to just be an exercise in speculation. Likewise, flows of diesel from the USA back to Europe are difficult to forecast because the price arbitrages that trigger those flows are even more difficult to predict. However, the prospects that Europe's net deficit in diesel will not only continue but also grow seem well founded in light of the region's recent refinery closures. Seeing this structural diesel deficit and any ensuing price arbitrage, US refiners may tweak, squeeze and cajole more diesel output to provide opportunistic exports. For tanker owners with vessels already in US waters, the opportunity to back‐haul clean products to Europe in a timely manner represents extra voyage revenue. Tanker owners with both flexible schedules and flexible rate expectations should be able to pour more of this back‐haul revenue gravy onto their plates.
