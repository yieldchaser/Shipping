**Operational review for the quarter ended 30 September 2026**

**Safety a key focus; strong price realisation with no change to production guidance.**

“The deaths of two colleagues at Escondida and Peak Downs are a profound loss. Our thoughts remain with their families and friends. No loss of life is acceptable. Investigations are underway and we will keep strengthening the controls that keep people safe in our pursuit of being a fatality free business.

Copper production was lower in the first quarter, reflecting planned grade declines and scheduled maintenance, alongside temporary disruptions from severe weather in Chile, stops for safety and the conveyor failure at Carrapateena. Our bulk commodities delivered a strong start to FY27 through a maintenance-intensive quarter. WAIO set records at both the mine and port, while BMA and NSW Energy Coal both delivered higher production than the same period last year. Stronger commodity prices provided a tailwind for earnings, particularly in copper where prices were around 50 per cent higher than a year ago.

We are building the next phase of growth in copper and potash. We continued to strengthen one of the world’s largest copper portfolios, from funding the Escondida New Concentrator in Chile to growing our resource base and long-term production potential at Copper South Australia, while also expanding our copper growth pipeline across the Americas. Jansen is rapidly taking shape, with Stage 1 now 87 per cent complete and on track for first production in mid-2027, adding a new and important commodity to our portfolio.

Global markets remain volatile, with geopolitical tensions, trade uncertainty and elevated energy and freight costs continuing to create headwinds across the industry. At the same time, demand for commodities remains resilient, supported by continued industrialisation and urbanisation in China and India, alongside unprecedented investment in data centres and enabling infrastructure globally, which is reinforcing the long-term outlook for copper. That demand plays to the strength of our portfolio, which touches almost every part of how the world builds, powers and feeds itself.”

**3 Geology**

The Olympic Dam deposit lies entirely within the Olympic Dam Breccia Complex (Figure 3), which is hosted by the 1,593.28 ± 0.26 Ma Roxby Downs Granite. The breccia complex contains the mineralised rock volume and covers approximately 50 km². It is about 6 km long and 3 km wide, typically extends to a depth of 900 m, and locally reaches more than 2,000 m below surface.

Approximately 350 m of unaltered, unmineralised, flat-lying Neoproterozoic to Cambrian sedimentary rocks unconformably overlie the breccia complex. The Roxby Downs Granite is the principal host rock. Other important components are bedded clastic facies; clasts of 1,594.63 ± 0.71 Ma Gawler Range Volcanic felsic feldspar-phyric lavas in hematite-rich breccias and dykes; and mafic-ultramafic lavas and dykes (MDY). The MDY are pre-, syn- and post-mineralisation, and form an integral part of the Olympic Dam ore-forming system. The breccia complex and the surrounding unbrecciated Roxby Downs Granite are intruded by the regionally extensive ca. 825 Ma Gairdner Dyke Swarm (dolerite).

Mineralisation is hosted by Roxby Downs Granite and other lithologies, including bedded clastic facies, felsic lavas, mafic-ultramafic lavas, sills and dykes, and subvolcanic quartz-phyric rhyolite. Tectonic, magmatic and hydrothermal processes have weakly to intensely brecciated these rocks. Iron oxide alteration has variably replaced them, producing a compositional continuum from recognisable lithologies to iron oxide breccias in which primary lithological textures have been obliterated. Faulting and brecciation commonly obscure lithological contacts.

More than 100 minerals occur in the deposit, although 15 account for more than 98% of the rock mass. The principal gangue minerals are hematite, quartz, muscovite, K-feldspar, chlorite, fluorite, siderite and barite. The dominant sulphide minerals are chalcocite/digenite/djurleite (collectively referred to here as chalcocite), bornite, chalcopyrite and pyrite. Sphalerite, galena, molybdenite and tennantite-tetrahedrite are less abundant, while native copper occurs only in trace amounts. Sulphides generally occur as disseminated grains within the breccia matrix and clasts, ranging in size from <20 µm to several millimetres, with an average grain size of approximately 100 µm. They only rarely occur as veins in granite-dominated breccias.

At the deposit scale, Cu ± Fe sulphides show a distinct upward and inward zonation: pyrite g chalcopyrite-pyrite g chalcopyrite exsolution lamellae in brown bornite g purple bornite-chalcocite symplectites g digenite lamellae in chalcocite.

**4 Data acquisition**

**4.1 Drilling and sampling**

Drilling comprises oriented and unoriented diamond drilling from surface and underground locations. The drilling includes parent holes and, where additional trajectories are required, wedge holes. A parent hole is the primary drill hole; a wedge hole is a subsidiary or “child” hole initiated from a parent hole at depth, allowing several trajectories to be drilled from a single parent-hole collar. Drill spacing varies across the deposit from approximately 20 m to >300 m, and drilling is generally oriented perpendicular to the interpreted orebody geometry.

Surface drilling comprised parent holes and, where required, wedge holes drilled from parent holes. Surface parent holes were collared in PQ3 diameter (83 mm) through the overburden and continued in HQ3 (61.1 mm) or HQ (63.5 mm) core. Wedge holes were generally drilled in NQ2 diameter (50.6 mm). Navigational drilling was used in selected surface and underground programs to support trajectory control, including parent-hole and wedge-hole drilling. Downhole survey methods and quality control are described in Section 4.3 *Surveys* .

Longer underground parent holes were collared in HQ diameter (63.5 mm) to approximately 500 m, continued in NQ2 diameter (50.6 mm) to approximately 1,700 m, and completed in BQ diameter (36.5 mm) where required. Underground infill and grade-control holes were drilled in BQ diameter (36.5 mm) and were generally not oriented. Longer holes were oriented using the REFLEX ACT III tool; at the end of each run, the drillers marked the low side of the core, which was then used as the reference for marking the core and recording structural measurements. Grade-control holes were generally not oriented and were therefore not used for oriented structural measurements.

The drilling data and distribution are considered sufficient to support interpretation of geological and grade continuity for the declared Mineral Resource for an underground bulk-mining scenario and the existing Mineral Resource declaration for the selective-mining method. The geology is supported by ongoing underground mapping and mining. Drilling orientations were selected to test the interpreted mineralisation and structures, and the scale and geometry of the mineralised system in relation to the drilling orientation is unlikely to have introduced material sampling bias.

Sample recovery was estimated during logging by reconciling the cumulative reconstructed core length with the drilled length for each core run. Estimated recoveries were high, generally exceeding 99%. Fresh core is relatively competent, and the sulphide minerals are generally not friable. There is no clear relationship between sample recovery and grade in Olympic Dam drill core; consequently, no material bias is expected from preferential loss or gain of fine or coarse material.

Drill holes were logged in qualitative detail below the post-mineralised cover. Logging included lithology, rock composition and texture, alteration minerals, sulphide distribution, mineralisation characteristics and geotechnical information for rock-mass qualification. Structural measurements were recorded from oriented core, and core was photographed dry. Geological, geotechnical, structural, sampling and assay data were managed in a SQL server-hosted database. Logging was completed to a standard appropriate for Mineral Resource estimation, mining studies and metallurgical studies.

Diamond core was split using an automatic core saw. One half was submitted for assay and the other half was retained in labelled trays at the Olympic Dam core storage facility. Retention of the half core provides material for geological review and verification sampling, while the generally competent nature of the fresh core and non-friable sulphide mineralisation supports preservation of representative material in storage.

No dedicated twinned holes have been drilled. Wedge drilling, proximal or cross-cutting drilling, close to existing mineralisation shows some short-scale variations in the tenor of mineralisation. However, this close-spaced drilling provides correlation and general support for the geological and grade modelling.

The majority (>91%) of mineralised diamond core was sampled at the standard interval appropriate to the core diameter: predominantly 2 m and 1 m for NQ2 core, and 2.5 m for BQ/BQTK core. These interval lengths were selected to provide representative sample masses of approximately 4-8 kg and are supported by Olympic Dam site-specific sampling testwork and sample nomograms, which demonstrated that the intervals provide an appropriate balance between sample precision and practical sample-preparation requirements for the respective core sizes. Minor (<9%) historical samples were collected at 1 m or 5 m intervals; these data are retained and used subject to the applicable data validation, compositing and estimation controls.

Samples were submitted to an analytical laboratory for final drying, staged crushing to 2 mm, splitting to an approximately 2-3 kg portion and pulverisation to produce a pulp with 90% passing 75 µm. Duplicate samples were collected at each preparation stage where a reduction in sample mass occurred. Bulk dry density was measured on all assayed samples using the water-immersion method on core samples at the Olympic Dam core-processing facilities.

Deterministic geological models (wireframes) of the sulphide species are built in Leapfrog modelling software, using a combination of implicit geological modelling combined with local updates driven by geological sectional interpretation. Extensive peer reviews of the geological models are undertaken prior to use in resource estimation.

The confidence of the geological interpretation of the Olympic Dam mineral deposit is supported by a long history of diamond drilling, geological logging, assay results, underground mapping, and reconciliation of mined material and metal production. Confidence in the mineralised model is sufficient in areas declared as Mineral Resource, where mineralisation orientations are adequately constrained by data spacing and supported by lithological, alteration and structural modelling. Areas outside the declared Mineral Resource generally have lower confidence due to wider drill spacing, reduced geological confidence, or insufficient demonstrated mineralisation continuity at a scale relevant to potential underground mining methods, including both bulk and selective mining scenarios. Future drilling and studies may refine these interpretations.

The Mineral Resource estimation process included:

• Raw assay data composited to 5 m lengths, due to consideration of sampling, mineralisation style and mining method choices, with the relevant domains flagged after compositing.

• Statistical analysis of the composites within key mineralisation domains.

• Variography modelling on mineralisation, sulphide and orientation domains.

• Identification of outliers and application of high yield thresholds to the composites, determined by geostatistical domain analysis and calculated using a metal-at-risk approach.

• For the mineralised domains being reported, estimation of grade and density via either a combination of multiple-indicator kriging with ordinary kriging (MIK-OK) or ordinary kriging (OK) methods within estimation domains constrained by mineralisation through sulphide domains and orientation domains.

Estimation was undertaken using Vulcan mining software (version 2023.4). Drill hole information is exported from the acQuire database as ASCII format text files and imported into a Vulcan format drill hole dataset. No top-cutting is applied; extreme or outlier values are instead managed using the high yield thresholds described above, which are customised to each stope area domain.

The block model uses estimation supports approximate to drilling density and resource confidence. Measured blocks are estimated at 5 m X by 10 m Y by 5 m Z support, while Indicated and Inferred blocks are estimated at 30 m X by 30 m Y by 15 m Z support. The model is sub celled to a minimum of 2.5 m in each dimension to represent geological domain boundaries accurately.

The classified elements reported in the Mineral Resource are Cu, U 3 O 8 , Au and Ag. All four are estimated using the same MIK-OK methodology and the same variography approach described below, with the exception that Au and Ag are subject to an additional Indicator Kriging (IK) domaining step to account for the portion of Au and Ag mineralisation not contained within the sulphide domains. Dry bulk density is also estimated using this approach (see Section 5.5 *Moisture and density* ). As Cu is the main economic element at Olympic Dam and MIK-OK is the predominant estimation method applied, the following discussion of variography modelling is framed in terms of Cu.

The MIK-OK methodology uses two estimation techniques: MIK and OK. The intent of the MIK technique is to divide the grade distribution into 20 domains (classes) using 19 strategic cut-offs. The spatial variability of grade within these 20 domains is modelled using three indicator variograms. The indicator cut-offs are calculated based on a mixture of metal abundance in each class, the number of sample composites within each class, and the overall shape of the grade distribution. Typically, the three cut-offs occur at 50%, 30% and 20%, respectively, of the available sample composites in the distribution, resulting in apportionment of the distribution into low, medium and high-grade variography components.

The OK component of the MIK-OK method uses the samples flagged against the MIK estimate to derive the final block grade. The spatial variability of the flagged samples is modelled using two variograms, which are based on a 50/50 split of the 20 MIK classes described above.

The resource estimation process has well established procedures, including peer reviews, that govern geological interpretation, resource estimation and reporting of Mineral Resources. Documentation is stored electronically in a secure centralised location. These documents contain information on deposit geology, geometry, detailed geological and geostatistical modelling, data preparation including compositing, and classification parameters.

The classified elements reported in deeper domains (known as Olympic Dam Deeps), which run along the base of the deposit, are estimated using OK.

A large number of other unclassified elements are estimated in various ways for use in assessment of economic elements, gangue elements, and gangue minerals that may impact processing.

**5.2 Mining factors**

The total Olympic Dam Mineral Resource assumes both selective SLOS method and a bulk underground mining method such as block caving. The UG Sulphide (SLOS) and UG Sulphide Block Cave (BC) Mineral Resources together account for the majority of the shallower mineralisation. The remaining mineralisation is insufficient to warrant ongoing separate reporting of an Open Cut (OC) Mineral Resource and has been removed.

It is assumed that the SLOS Mineral Resource would be extracted prior to block caving (with zero grade backfill material replacing mineralisation). Future mining studies are required and may determine that some areas of the deposit are more economically amenable to block caving without extraction of the selective SLOS Mineral Resource component, in which case, some of the SLOS Mineral Resource may convert to block cave Mineral Resource in future.

A maximum depth threshold of -1,500 m from surface level (~-1400 mRL) for block caving was applied to the BC Mineral Resource. This threshold was applied based on benchmarking of deposits of similar style and tenor to account for modifying factors, such as economic and geotechnical factors relating to the reasonable prospects for economic extraction of deep block cave mining. Beyond this depth there are uncertainties on management of geotechnical conditions, heat and ventilation. Future mining studies may refine these assumptions and considerations. No depth constraint exists for reporting of the SLOS Mineral Resource.

For the block caving Smallest Mining Unit (SMU) and potential feasibility, minimum mining widths and heights were considered. This included an assessment of grade continuity, relative to potential SMU sizes, to quantitatively demonstrate the degree of spatial consistency in mineral grade distribution. The assessment utilised a model regularised to 100 m X by 100 m Y by 200 m Z, where SMU selection required a minimum of three connected blocks in the XY plane, all of which needed to be above the nominal economic cutoff. This criterion was informed by technical studies, internal analysis, and consultation with mining engineers and caving specialists.

The presence of current or future backfill (such as rockfill, concrete aggregate fill or paste fill) in any future cave design could present a risk of mud rush (wet or dry), poor cave propagation, breakages or particle comminution. This material may act like other in-situ fine geological units (e.g. overburden) or may have variable properties dependent on backfill formulation. Backfill material could also act differently under varied seismic or stress conditions. The Competent Person assumes that prior to any future mining, sufficient mining controls relating to managing mud rush risks, geotechnical risks, grade variability (of economic and any potential deleterious elements) would be established to manage and maintain safe working and extraction practices.

There is no assumption related to potential concurrent caving, cave induced seismicity or seismic interaction, or distance buffers required for concurrent SLOS mining. The Competent Person assumes that any future caving would have optimisation on spatial or temporal mining sequences, with controls established prior to, during, or post-extraction of caved areas.

The Competent Person assumes that block caving, or other bulk underground mining methods, could occur in the presence of backfill material. No assumption has been made regarding the impact of non-homogeneous cave flow on the distribution or forecasting of economic or deleterious elements or materials. Prior to any consideration of conversion to Ore Reserves, future studies are required to understand practical constraints, or absolute limits, of either geotechnical, metallurgical, or other modifying factors. Due to the uncertainty of caving where there might be a significant portion of backfill, a nominal limit of ≥ 50% backfill, from current or future SLOS mining, is used to constrain resource classification to Inferred.

**5.5 Moisture and density**

Tonnages are estimated on a dry basis using dry bulk density data collected for every sampled interval of drill holes and domained in accordance with the copper mineralisation.

The majority of fresh Olympic Dam core has very low porosity and negligible moisture content; therefore, no moisture correction is applied. The estimation of density is performed using an approach similar to that used for Cu (MIK-OK or OK).

Determination of the classification category relating to geological confidence was undertaken using geological confidence shells (wireframes). These shells were created as follows:

• Regular grids, at spacings corresponding to desired confidence, were created using RMSP software (Python mining software). Grid spacings were increased according to confidence levels.

• At each grid point, a search was conducted using radii which correspond to twice the grid spacing to count the number of drill holes within that volume. Doubling the grid spacing ensures that the search volume around each grid centroid covers the entire area of influence for that cell and its immediate neighbours.

• Boundaries were generated where the drill hole density transitions between classification thresholds, creating 3D shells which reflect increasing classification confidence. These shells were manually calibrated in Leapfrog Geo (2023.3) with the current drilling database to resolve any artefacts and ensure that drilling confidence levels matched expectations. **9.1.4 Cut-off parameters**

Olympic dam applies a variable cut-off grade strategy that is informed by a study. Ore reserves are estimated at a cut-off grade of 1.0-1.7% Cu for UG Sulphide and ≥ 0.6% Cu for Low grade. UG Sulphide includes ore from stopes and development, while low-grade and waste is the material from development.

Mining block-specific cut-off grades exploit the unique geological properties of each block to produce a relatively consistent ore feed. This seeks to maximise throughput through a series of surface processing stages. This strategy enables high-grade material to be targeted in early years of the mine plan. Lower grade material is then exploited as higher-grade material is depleted.

Price and cost assumptions are used to support a variable cut-off. Operating costs are inclusive of mining, surface processing, general and administration, closure, marketing and royalties.

**9.1.5 Mining factors**

The mining method selected at Olympic Dam is underground SLOS with cemented aggregate backfill. Paste fill has also been identified as an alternative backfill strategy, with a paste plant currently under execution. The stopes are sequentially extracted and progressively backfilled to control maximum excavation spans and manage stresses in the rock mass.

The conversion of Mineral Resource to Ore Reserve is achieved by creating stope shapes using manual (engineered stopes) and automated techniques (Stope Optimiser tools). These stopes are incorporated into a mine schedule and sequenced considering geotechnical parameters and ventilation constraints, and operating parameters.

Detailed development and stope designs are generated with mine block specific cut-off grades and schedules are prepared considering respective mine block constraints.

The nominal stope mining dimension is 30 m by 30 m in plan, with stope heights varying according to geotechnical parameters and ground conditions. Stope widths range from 15 m to 30 m to accommodate irregular orebody geometries. The development dimensions and profiles vary for different applications but are typically in the ranges of 5 m by 5 m to 7 m by 7 m.

Overbreak (OB) and underbreak (UB) factors that inform dilution and recovery are reviewed annually and updated as necessary. Modifying factors are informed through reconciliation of site performance. They are applied by mine area to reflect localised geotechnical conditions. Typical planned factors are; UB tonnage factor: 5-11%, OB tonnage factor: 9-28%, OB grade factor: 47-77%. The wide range of factors reflects area-specific geotechnical and mining conditions across the operation. The use of these factors enhances the accuracy and confidence of the Ore Reserve estimate.

The mine plan includes a new material handling system with new underground crusher and conveyor to surface, supported by appropriate ventilation raises to deliver production profile.

Inferred Mineral Resources were considered in the Life of Mine schedule but are not included in the Ore Reserve estimate. **10.3 Market assessment**

Olympic Dam is a polymetallic deposit that produces copper cathode, uranium oxide concentrate, and gold and silver bullion. These products are sold to various domestic and international markets.

Future copper demand growth is expected to be underpinned by development in emerging economies as they electrify, industrialise, and urbanise. The global energy transition provides further upside, as copper is widely used in electric vehicles and renewable energy infrastructure.

Currently, the copper market is broadly balanced; however, demand is expected to exceed committed supply in the coming years, requiring new supply to come to market.

Nuclear power generation, and associated uranium demand, are expected to grow in the future, requiring new sources of supply.

Unlike most industrial metals, gold and silver prices are influenced not only by physical supply-demand balances but also by investment flows, monetary conditions, and safe-haven demand.
