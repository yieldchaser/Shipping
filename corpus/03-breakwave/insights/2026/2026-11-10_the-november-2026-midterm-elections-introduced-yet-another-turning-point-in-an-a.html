The November 2026 midterm elections introduced yet another turning point in an already turbulent political and economic landscape

November 10, 2026  |  November 10, 2026  |  https://www.breakwaveadvisors.com/insights/2026/11/9/the-november-2026-midterm-elections-introduced-yet-another-turning-point-in-an-already-turbulent-political-and-economic-landscape

ByMichalis Voutsinas

In November 2016, with the global economy still absorbing the aftershock of the Brexit referendum, the election of Donald Trump delivered a second political surprise. The initial reaction was one of alarm. As the count turned against expectations, US equity futures fell to their overnight limit, Tokyo suffered its sharpest session since the Brexit vote, Seoul followed it lower, and gold briefly rallied by almost 5 percent as investors sought refuge. Yet by the time European markets opened, the narrative had been rewritten. Investors chose to read the result as a promise of fiscal expansion, deregulation and a vast infrastructure programme rather than as a threat to the established order. European and American equities closed firmly higher, the dollar strengthened on expectations of reflation, and gold surrendered most of its overnight gains. Commodities divided along the same line. Iron ore surged to its highest level in almost two years on the prospect of infrastructureled demand and continued Chinese stimulus, while oil, still burdened by a persistent glut, drifted lower. For dry bulk, the timing was striking. Having touched a record low earlier that year, the Baltic Dry Index climbed from around 900 points on election day to a two-year high within ten days, and dry bulk equities enjoyed a speculative rally. The recovery, however, had begun before the vote, rooted in Chinese stimulus and restrictions on domestic coal output; the election amplified sentiment rather than creating demand. Beneath the optimism lay a more durable change. The threat of steep tariffs on Chinese goods introduced a new degree of policy unpredictability, prompting multinationals to reassess their supply chains and encouraging Beijing to insulate its economy, stimulate domestic demand and accelerate industrial upgrading. Those months set in motion a prolonged strategic recalibration, in which trade friction and technological decoupling became permanent features of policy on both sides of the Pacific.

Eight years later, history appeared to repeat itself, though with a revealing difference in emphasis. Trump's return to the White House in November 2024 produced one of the strongest sessions on record for Wall Street, with all three major indices closing at record highs. Beyond the United States, however, the mood was markedly more restrained. European shares reversed early gains to close lower as carmakers sold off on tariff concerns, Tokyo rallied on a weaker yen, Seoul slipped, and mainland Chinese equities edged down before recovering strongly the following session on robust export data and hopes of further stimulus. For China, the renewed protectionist agenda revived familiar pressures on an export-led growth model. Yet the resilience of Chinese manufacturing has so far blunted the impact of American restrictions. Exports have continued to grow, and what has changed most is not their scale but their direction. Shipments to the United States have fallen, while those to Southeast Asia, Africa and Europe have expanded at a robust pace. This should not be read merely as the rerouting of goods. It reflects a deeper restructuring of international commerce, as production networks, investment flows and commercial relationships adapt to a more fragmented geopolitical order. This time, markets priced tariffs rather than infrastructure. A surging dollar pushed gold to a three-week low, while oil, soybeans, copper and iron ore all retreated as investors weighed the prospect of renewed trade conflict with the world's largest commodity consumer. In the freight market, the reaction was barely perceptible, with the Baltic Dry Index edging from 1,405 points on election day to 1,427 the following session. Seasonal Capesize demand then lifted the index above 1,750 by mid-November. The gains proved short-lived, however, and the index retreated to 1,354 by the end of the month, its lowest level since January.

Bringing the story to the present, the November 2026 midterm elections introduced yet another turning point in an already turbulent political and economic landscape. Democrats secured control of both chambers of Congress, making substantial gains in traditionally Republican territory. The result reflected discontent over the cost of living and economic management, compounded by the war with Iran and the surge in energy prices. The market reaction this time followed a different script. During the Asian session, as the count tilted towards the Democrats, the AI-heavy markets of Tokyo and Seoul came under intense pressure, while mainland Chinese equities rose on hopes that a Democratic Congress would increase pressure to ease tariffs. Once the outcome was clear, European shares posted their strongest session in almost five months, led by banks and clean energy companies, and Wall Street recorded its best day of the year as investors embraced the prospect of divided government as a check on extreme policy outcomes. The relief proved short-lived: the following day, the Nasdaq slumped 5.5 percent, its worst session since April 2025, as fears that a Democratic Congress would slow the data-centre buildout triggered a rout in chipmakers, with Nvidia alone shedding almost $450 billion in market value. The reversal exposed how vulnerable the AI trade has become, already strained by high interest rates, heavy capital spending and lingering doubts over returns. The decisive move, however, came in commodities. Brent crude fell by 9 percent to below $95 a barrel, its steepest daily decline since April, as traders priced an end to the conflict. Gold, which had fallen to a one-year low ahead of the vote, climbed back above $4,000 an ounce on a weaker dollar and lower Treasury yields. Iron ore edged higher on short-covering and Chinese restocking expectations, although prices remain close to four-year lows. Fertiliser markets showed no immediate reaction, but lower oil and gas prices improve the cost outlook for nitrogen producers. In freight, the Baltic Dry Index rebounded by 4 percent, driven by a sharp rise in Capesize rates, while Supramax rates slipped to their lowest level since mid-October, underlining that iron ore restocking rather than politics was the principal driver.

Yet, while financial and commodity markets were quick to respond to the prospect of political change and geopolitical de-escalation, the implications for international trade and shipping are likely to unfold over a considerably longer horizon. A Democratic-controlled Congress will complicate Trump's legislative agenda, but its ability to reverse his protectionist policies remains limited by the extensive tariff-making powers delegated to the executive branch, the Senate filibuster and the presidential veto. Trade restrictions are therefore likely to remain an instrument of Washington's economic and geopolitical strategy. For dry bulk shipping, the immediate implications are likely to be felt first through energy markets rather than trade policy. A sustained easing of the Iran conflict would lower bunker costs and facilitate a return to more normal shipping operations across the Gulf, while potentially unwinding part of the geopolitical premium that has supported gas prices and, indirectly, coal demand. Beyond these near-term effects, however, the more significant implications lie in any reconfiguration of global industrial supply chains. Should trade barriers and tariffs remain in place or become more entrenched, companies could reassess the geographical allocation of their manufacturing operations, potentially accelerating the relocation of production capacity towards Southeast Asia. Such a shift could gradually reshape global supply chains and patterns of commodity sourcing, although its implications for seaborne trade and dry bulk demand would be difficult to quantify at this stage.

Taken together, the three elections tell a coherent story. In 2016, markets priced reflation and infrastructure, and iron ore led the commodity response. In 2024, they priced tariffs and a strong dollar, and commodities broadly retreated. In 2026, they priced geopolitics, and oil carried the adjustment. In each case, the freight market's response proved more muted and more dependent on underlying fundamentals than headlines, confirming that elections tend to amplify existing trends rather than create new ones.
