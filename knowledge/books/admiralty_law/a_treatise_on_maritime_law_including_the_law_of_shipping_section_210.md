It is a primary rule in the interpretation of a statute, that all the sections thereof are to be construed together, so that one section may often be explanatory of another.1 We shall, there- fore, first consider the effect of the fourth section relative to the right of abandonment The fiTst part of this section, we think, clearly does not apply to a case of collision. It is limited in express terms to the case of loss suffered by freighters or owners of goods or any property whatever on the s&me voyage ; and is intended to allow the pro- ceedings therein set forth only in a case of a breach of a contract of affreightment, where several claims are pending against, or apprehended by, the owners of the ship. The omission of the subject of collision seems to have arisen by the framers of the statute following the statutes of Massachusetts and Maine, which, like the early English statutes of 7 Geo. 2, a 15, and 26 Geo. 3, c. 86, limited the owner's liability to cases of affreight- ment and did not apply to collision. We infer this because the language of the 53 Geo. 3, c. 159, which applies to collision, is so clear and explicit on this point that it must have been fol- lowed, had it not been overlooked, or intentionally passed over.
