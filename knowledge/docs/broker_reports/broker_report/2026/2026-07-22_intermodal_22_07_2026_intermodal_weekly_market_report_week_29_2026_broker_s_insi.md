Market Insight
By Nikos Tagoulis, Senior Analyst
Following its successful first trial in 2025, China is set to take a
further step towards regular container operations via the North-
ern Sea Route (NSR). Last year, the 4,890 teu Istanbul Bridge,
operated by Chinese -backed Sea Legend Shipping, completed the
China–Europe haul in around 21 days. Building on that passage,
Sea Legend plans a seasonal service of eight roughly weekly sail-
ings from mid-August to October, deploying seven predominantly
feeder-sized vessels and targeting transit times of 20 to 22 days.
It marks the first serious attempt towards an Arctic liner contain-
er service.
The route's principal advantage lies in transit time. By shortening
selected Northeast Asia –Northern Europe voyages by 30–40%, it
offers a faster seasonal alternative to Suez routings and China –
Europe rail, with potential fuel and emissions savings against
conventional routes. That saving matters most for cargo that val-
ues speed over cost: higher -value, time -sensitive goods such as
electric vehicles, batteries and solar equipment, for which faster
delivery frees capital that would otherwise remain tied up in
transit. Ship size reflects the cargo volumes and the seasonal na-
ture of the trade. With neither the volumes nor the time to fill
larger ships, the service runs feeders of roughly 1,500 to 5,000
teu, sustaining near-weekly frequency through the season.
This initiative comes amid a gradual and steady rise in NSR activi-
ty. According to the Norwegian Centre for High North Logistics,
transit voyages rose from 97 in 2024 to 107 in 2025, roughly five
times the level of a decade earlier, while containership transits
increased from 11 to 15.
Several major economies are moving to expand their presence
along the NSR. Russia continues to move Arctic LNG, crude oil,
coal and other commodities to Europe and Asia via the route, sup-
ported by an expanding icebreaker fleet and state -backed plans
for ports, terminals and emergency -response infrastructure. Chi-
na is testing the route in Asia –Europe connectivity within its
broader Polar Silk Road context, while South Korea is likewise
building operational experience through government -supported
trial voyages. Together these point to more sustained interest in
Arctic shipping.
This interest is becoming more pertinent amid heightened geopo-
litical uncertainty and disruption to global energy and trade flows,
a period in which the value of alternative maritime routes can ex-
ceed their immediate commercial scale. The NSR is not a direct
response to events in the Strait of Hormuz, nor a broad alterna-
tive to the Suez Canal. Yet even with seasonal operations and
limited volumes, it offers a degree of transport optionality and
reduces dependence on a handful of maritime chokepoints.
The route holds potential, but for now remains constrained. Vol-
umes are still marginal against established corridors, and naviga-
tion depends on seasonal ice conditions, suitable tonnage, spe-
cialised crews and reliable navigational support. Infrastructure is
limited across much of the route, with few deepwater ports, safe
harbours, bunkering facilities or emergency -response capabilities.
Use of the NSR also requires engagement with Russia's admin-
istration and, where conditions demand, Russian icebreaker sup-
port, while sanctions on Russian counterparties add compliance
risk. Moreover, rising traffic would increase local emissions, in-
cluding black carbon, and the probability of an accident in a sensi-
tive ecosystem where harsh conditions could complicate any re-
sponse.
For now, the service's significance is largely demonstrative. The
reliability of Sea Legend's schedule offers a test of whether
scheduled Arctic operations can be repeated rather than remain-
ing one -off demonstrations. Successful execution would mark
another step after last year's Istanbul Bridge passage and, over
the medium term, could support a specialised seasonal trade,
generating demand for ice -capable feeder tonnage, navigational
expertise and associated marine services. Warming temperatures
and retreating ice may also extend the operational window. The
NSR remains presently a niche seasonal corridor, though on a
slow but steady upward trend.

At Alang, the market firmed last week on tightening steel sup-
ply, as the monsoon -driven slowdown in cutting activity limited
plate volumes. Even so, the rebound varies by product and could
prove fragile, given tepid downstream steel demand and the
absence of a clear catalyst in the segment. Meanwhile, India's
willingness to accept sanctioned vessels, in contrast with its
Subcontinent peers, partly offsets its weaker pricing competi-
tiveness. Sentiment is affected by last week ’s weakening of
local currency versus US dollar. On the macro front, June infla-
tion unexpectedly surged to its strongest reading since late
2024, as earlier oil disruptions transmitted into transport costs,
compounded by firmer food inflation.
Chattogram drifted through another quiet week, with the stand-
off between willing buyers and a hesitant market still unre-
solved. Enquiry for tonnage remains healthy, but appetite is
increasingly selective, favouring bulkers and units free of com-
pliance exposure after a tanker that had cleared all formalities
only to appear on a sanctions list before making it to the yard
sharpened caution among recyclers and cash buyers alike. Steel
trading is largely dormant, though activity picked up once flood-
ing subsided, and mills expect firmer demand after the mon-
soon. Gadani held steady, its fundamentals little altered from
the prior week: candidates remain scarce while purchasing ap-
petite stays firm, and an enduring scrap shortage continues to
underpin healthy buyer demand. Recyclers' offers were un-
moved. Local steel prices improved on firmer sentiment, though
actual consumption has yet to follow. Optimism rests partly on
fiscal measures, with a lower property withholding tax and sub-
sidised housing schemes expected to lift construction after the
monsoon. Meanwhile, Middle East geopolitical tensions are lift-
ing oil prices, placing a growing strain on Pakistan and much of
the region.
Turkey stayed subdued, with a tight pool of candidates and a
weak currency eroding purchasing power and dampening senti-
ment.

In China, the trade surplus increased further in June to
$125.62bn, up from $105.43bn in May and above the
$119.50bn forecast. Both exports and imports grew
faster than expected: exports rose 27% y-o-y, support-
ed by strong demand for AI data -center equipment
and hardware, while imports surged 36%, marking
their fastest growth in five years.
In Eurozone, the CPI rose by 2.8% y -o-y in June, in line
with estimations and down from 3.2% recorded in May.
In United States, the PPI decelerated to 5.5% y -o-y in
June, below market forecasts of 6.2% and May ’s print
of 6.5%.
In UK, GDP rose by 1.3% y -o-y in May, broadly in line
with market expectations of 1.4% and slightly above
April’s 1.2%.
