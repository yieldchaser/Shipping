## Main
By Michalis Voutsinas
Throughout history, wars have rarely transformed the global economy solely through the destruction they immediately inflict. Their more profound impact has stemmed both from the direct disruption of trade, production and supply chains, and from the expectations they create regarding the second- and third-round consequences that follow. Markets do not wait for supply to disappear before repricing risk, nor do businesses postpone investment only after disruption has become visible. Uncertainty itself becomes an economic force, influencing capital allocation, consumption, investment and trade long before the first measurable economic consequences emerge. Every major geopolitical crisis therefore unfolds along two parallel timelines: one on the battlefield, where events evolve unpredictably, and another in financial markets, where probabilities are continuously reassessed and translated into prices. The current conflict in the Middle East has become a compelling illustration of this phenomenon. While military developments have dominated global headlines, the more enduring story has been the gradual transmission of geopolitical risk through energy markets, inflation expectations, monetary policy and, ultimately, global economic growth. For the shipping industry, whose fortunes are intrinsically linked to the rhythm of international commerce, understanding this transmission mechanism is becoming increasingly important. Freight markets often react to the immediate logistical consequences of conflict, yet the more profound implications arise when geopolitical uncertainty begins to alter the trajectory of the world economy itself.
The evolution of oil prices since the outbreak of the conflict provides a compelling illustration of how markets progressively distinguish between fear and fundamentals. Brent crude initially surged as investors rapidly priced in the possibility of a broader regional confrontation capable of disrupting one of the world's most strategically important energyproducing regions. Memories of previous geopolitical crises prompted traders to attach a substantial risk premium well before any physical disruption to supply had materialised. As is often the case during periods of heightened geopolitical uncertainty, oil began trading less on prevailing supply-demand balances and more on the probability of future disruptions. As the conflict evolved, however, the initial rally gradually lost momentum. Gulf producers largely maintained export flows and global inventories remained sufficient to satisfy demand. Although insurance premiums, freight costs and security concerns all increased, the feared large-scale disruption to physical energy supplies failed to materialise, prompting markets to gradually unwind part of the geopolitical premium embedded in crude prices. This marked the first major turning point, as investors increasingly concluded that the global energy system had demonstrated a greater degree of resilience than initially anticipated. The second turning point has emerged more recently, as renewed attacks on commercial shipping and growing concerns over the security of critical maritime chokepoints have once again elevated geopolitical risk. The persistent uncertainty surrounding the Strait of Hormuz, the Bab el-Mandeb and the wider Red Sea has reminded markets that global energy security remains exceptionally fragile. Brent has consequently recovered towards the psychologically important threshold of $100 per barrel, reflecting not an actual shortage of crude but a renewed reassessment of the probability that supply could eventually be disrupted. In this respect, oil has once again become a geopolitical asset, with prices increasingly driven by changing perceptions of risk rather than any immediate deterioration in physical market fundamentals.
These concerns have been echoed by international institutions. In its June 2026 Global Economic Prospects report, the World Bank identified an escalation of geopolitical tensions and the resulting disruption to global energy markets as one of the principal downside risks facing the world economy. Under its baseline scenario, global GDP is projected to expand by 2.5 percent during 2026, representing another year of below-trend growth amid structurally weaker productivity, elevated interest rates and slowing investment. However, the institution also outlined a significantly more adverse scenario. Should military tensions materially disrupt energy supplies and trigger a sustained increase in oil prices, global economic growth could slow to as little as 1.3 percent, while inflationary pressures would once again intensify across both advanced and emerging economies. The significance of this assessment extends well beyond the oil market. Energy represents one of the economy's most powerful transmission mechanisms. Higher oil prices increase transportation costs, raise industrial input prices, reduce household purchasing power and complicate the task of central banks attempting to return inflation towards target. Businesses confronted with greater uncertainty postpone investment, financial conditions tighten and consumer confidence weakens. As economic activity slows, international trade inevitably loses momentum. The World Bank's warning therefore reflects not merely the possibility of temporarily higher energy prices but the broader risk that geopolitical instability gradually transforms into weaker global demand. For shipping markets, whose performance ultimately depends upon expanding commodity flows and industrial production, this distinction is particularly important. Temporary logistical disruptions may generate freight volatility and occasionally support earnings, yet they cannot permanently offset the consequences of a weaker global economy.
Against this increasingly uncertain backdrop, global trade is simultaneously confronting another structural challenge. This week, President Donald Trump announced a fresh series of tariff measures targeting several major trading partners, reinforcing a protectionist agenda that has steadily returned to the forefront of international economic policy. Although the immediate commercial impact remains difficult to quantify, the broader implications are already becoming apparent. Repeated tariff announcements increase uncertainty regarding future trading relationships, discourage long-term investment decisions and encourage companies to redesign supply chains around geopolitical rather than purely economic considerations. The result is a gradual fragmentation of global commerce, where resilience increasingly takes precedence over efficiency. History repeatedly demonstrates that international trade prospers under stability and predictability rather than confrontation. Businesses commit capital when future market access appears secure, while investment decisions become considerably more cautious whenever policy uncertainty increases. The renewed emphasis on tariffs therefore risks reinforcing many of the same economic pressures already generated by geopolitical tensions. While conflict threatens the physical movement of commodities through strategic maritime corridors, protectionism introduces uncertainty into the regulatory framework governing international trade itself. Together, these forces create an environment in which businesses face growing difficulty planning production, investment and logistics over the medium term.
For shipping, the convergence of geopolitical conflict, rising protectionism and a weakening macroeconomic outlook presents a paradoxical landscape. In the near term, disruptions to established trade routes, longer voyage distances, higher insurance costs and precautionary inventory building can all support tonne-mile demand and temporarily strengthen freight markets. Yet these gains should not be mistaken for evidence of healthier global trade. Shipping ultimately derives its prosperity not from disruption but from expanding industrial activity, rising commodity consumption and growing economic integration. The greater risk therefore lies in the gradual accumulation of uncertainties rather than in any single geopolitical event. Higher energy prices feed into inflation, inflation influences monetary policy, tighter financial conditions weigh on investment and industrial production, while protectionism further fragments global supply chains and restrains trade. Individually, each development may appear manageable; collectively, they have the potential to redefine the pace and direction of global commerce. Markets eventually learn to absorb geopolitical shocks as uncertainty gradually gives way to clearer expectations. The more difficult challenge, however, is adapting to an environment where successive geopolitical crises, trade fragmentation and structurally weaker growth reinforce one another. For the dry bulk sector, this distinction is critical. Freight markets may continue to benefit from temporary logistical dislocations, but the industry's long-term prosperity will ultimately depend on the resilience of global trade rather than the persistence of global uncertainty.
