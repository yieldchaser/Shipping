## Summary

## Main
By Michalis Voutsinas
China's economic trajectory continues to be shaped by the interplay between structural transformation and the gradual rebalancing of a growth model historically anchored in investment and industrial production. The prolonged adjustment in the property sector and a measured recovery in household expenditure coexist with increasingly sophisticated manufacturing capabilities, technological upgrading and a competitive export base. The latest economic indicators point to an economy retaining considerable growth capacity, albeit with marked differences in momentum across sectors and sources of demand. Gross domestic product expanded by 4.5 percent year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, accelerating from 4.3 percent in the preceding quarter and exceeding market expectations. Growth over the first nine months reached 4.6 percent, placing Beijing's annual target of 4.5-5.0 percent within reach. The improvement coincided with firmer industrial production and an acceleration in retail sales during September, indicating that economic activity gathered momentum towards the end of the quarter. While growth remains below the rates achieved during earlier phases of China's development, its relative performance compares favourably with that of many major economies. The latest figures therefore reinforce the view that China's economy retains meaningful underlying dynamism, while also highlighting the importance of assessing the sectoral composition and durability of that expansion.
External demand remains a significant contributor to this performance. Exports surged by 36.3 percent year-on-year in September in dollar terms, reflecting robust international demand for semiconductors, artificial intelligence-related hardware and other high-technology products. Industrial production increased by 5.5 percent during the month, while value added in high-technology manufacturing rose by 14.3 percent over the first nine months. Equipment manufacturing expanded by 10.2 percent over the same period. Taken together, these indicators reflect the continued evolution of China's industrial base, underpinned by technological investment, economies of scale and an established position within global manufacturing supply chains. The implications extend beyond the immediate contribution of exports to economic growth. China's growing competitiveness in higher-value manufacturing is broadening the sources of industrial expansion as traditional investment drivers undergo a prolonged adjustment. Capital expenditure in advanced manufacturing and technology-related industries is helping to reshape the composition of output, while creating scope for productivity gains and a gradual movement towards more technologically intensive production. The extent to which this transformation can underpin a more broadly based expansion will depend, in part, on its transmission to the wider economy through employment, household incomes and investment beyond the sectors directly exposed to strategic policy priorities and overseas demand.
Domestic consumption has also shown signs of improvement, although the pace of recovery remains measured. Retail sales increased by 1.1 percent year-on-year in September, exceeding market expectations. Communication equipment recorded particularly strong demand, reflecting purchases of new devices alongside the influence of consumer incentives. Over the first nine months, however, retail sales of consumer goods rose by 1.1 percent, while total retail sales of goods and services increased by 2.6 percent. The September acceleration is encouraging, but the cumulative figures suggest that the improvement in selected spending categories has yet to translate into a more pronounced expansion in household expenditure. This pattern reflects the broader transition underway within the Chinese economy. Household spending decisions remain sensitive to employment prospects, income expectations and property valuations, while the ongoing housing adjustment continues to influence consumer sentiment. At the same time, the expansion of modern services and technology-oriented industries is creating additional avenues for growth, with a different investment profile from that of the traditional property and infrastructure cycle.
The property sector remains at the centre of the economy's ongoing adjustment. Real estate development investment declined by 20.6 percent year-on-year during January-September, while the floor area of newly started buildings fell by 24.9 percent. Sales of newly built commercial properties contracted by 13.2 percent in floor-area terms, indicating that a decisive recovery in market confidence remains elusive. The sector's retrenchment continues to affect construction activity, household balance sheets and investment decisions across related industries. Nevertheless, the picture is not uniformly negative: some housing-price indicators have improved at the margin, while transactions in the second-hand market have increased. These developments point to differing conditions across locations and market segments, although the available indicators are not yet sufficient to establish a broad-based recovery. The investment landscape beyond real estate is similarly differentiated. Fixed-asset investment contracted by 7.2 percent year-onyear during the first nine months, while private investment declined by 10.4 percent. Excluding real estate development, fixed-asset investment fell by 4.2 percent, and infrastructure investment also recorded a decline. These aggregate figures indicate that capital formation has yet to regain broad momentum. At the same time, the distribution of investment reveals a more nuanced picture: expenditure in high-technology sectors increased by 4.9 percent, while investment in information services rose by 20.2 percent. Capital is therefore being allocated increasingly unevenly across the economy, with strategic and technology-intensive industries attracting investment even as other sectors contend with subdued returns.
For policymakers, the latest GDP figures provide greater latitude in calibrating the near-term policy response. With growth over the first three quarters already above the lower bound of the annual target, the immediate imperative for a large-scale stimulus package may be less acute. Authorities have instead pursued incremental measures intended to sustain activity, including additional financing capacity for local governments, infrastructure initiatives and mortgage-interest subsidies. The broader policy objective is increasingly to reconcile industrial competitiveness with a more balanced domestic demand profile. Continued investment in advanced manufacturing can strengthen China's position in international markets, but the durability of the growth model will also depend on the development of more substantial domestic demand. Improved household confidence, firmer income expectations and greater stability in the property market would help create a more favourable environment for consumption and private capital expenditure. External conditions will remain an important influence on this transition. Strong export performance has reinforced China's contribution to global trade, but the associated trade surplus has sharpened commercial tensions. Concerns over competitive pressures on domestic manufacturers and strategic dependencies have prompted discussion of additional safeguards and measures to diversify supply chains. Such developments introduce an element of uncertainty around market access and the future trajectory of trade, even if they do not necessarily interrupt China's export growth in the near term. Meanwhile, investment in artificial intelligence and digital infrastructure offers a significant opportunity for Chinese manufacturers, but the persistence of this demand will depend on the evolution of global capital expenditure, the actual returns on technology investment and the extent to which current spending translates into sustained end-market demand.
Against this backdrop, China enters the final quarter of 2026 with economic growth running broadly in line with its annual ambitions and a manufacturing sector that continues to demonstrate considerable adaptability. The strength of technology-related exports, the expansion of advanced industrial production and the September improvement in retail sales provide a constructive near-term outlook. At the same time, the property adjustment, the contraction in aggregate investment and the measured pace of household expenditure illustrate that the transition towards a more diversified growth model remains incomplete. The outlook will hinge on whether industrial upgrading can increasingly generate domestic economic spillovers, while policy measures help stabilise investment and encourage a firmer recovery in consumption. For the dry bulk market, the critical consideration is whether this evolving growth profile translates into sustained seaborne commodity demand, with its implications for cargo volumes, trade flows and tonne-mile demand ultimately determining the extent to which China's economic expansion can underpin freight-market performance.
