## Main
The three things business investors need to know about this week's artificial intelligence (AI) rout:
While some of the issues are idiosyncratic to Nvidia's governance, the outcome of the US election has opened the issue of the broader social costs of AI development.

By George Lagarias
Summary
AI's boom has met its first political test. Tech shares are down 10% since mid-October, and voters in the US and Europe are asking who carries the risks of data centres. Four years after ChatGPT first spoke to us in a way we could understand, we are beginning to confront the economic, social and political costs of a technology that could reshape life on the planet. The recent market rout reflects concerns about AI's sustainability, debt-financed investment and the possibility of slower development as voters demand greater safeguards. While some issues are company-specific, the broader AI theme remains intact, driven by political will and technological momentum. For businesses, potential delays are a welcome opportunity to catch up, strategise and adapt to the new reality.
Some conversations around adulthood need to happen. But it is quite often that for various reasons we are not ready for them. Sometimes, the children aren't ready. Much more often, it is the parents who dread the conversation. In the case of AI, part of the problem is that the conversation needs to happen all too soon.
Four years after ChatGPT spoke to us in a way we could understand, the conversation is nearly upon us, and we dread it, unsure whether we are the parents or the children in it. Humanity initially marvelled at AI; there was no celebration or unity in it. Initially dismissed as little more than a silver-tongued fabulist, it started becoming more apparent that our toolmaker species had invented a tool that can change the face of life on the planet as much as fire, the wheel, the spear, gunpowder or the internet.
It has certainly taken a bit of getting used to, but we have now adopted AI at a massive scale, and relatively quickly by historical standards. Within four years, almost 50 % of the internet articles are AI or AI-assisted-produced, as is 75 % of all new webpages. Half of internet traffic is now automated bots.
The speed at which AI is developing is exponential. We are already seeing growing evidence that it is affecting the job market, especially for younger people.
Image reference: image006.png | assets/2026-11-13_we-need-to-have-a-conversation-about-ai_img_image006_c5a0ffe2664f.png
With tech stocks falling 10% in the past few weeks, investors are increasingly worried that the AI theme driving markets may have reached a natural end. The Nvidia boss's personal misadventures aside, markets now worry that the AI theme, a lot of it financed by debt and fully endorsed by a now less powerful White House, may be running out of fuel, despite its popularity.
Some of the worries about sustainability have a basis.
A recent Bank for International Settlements (BIS) bulletin maps the web of circular relationships among AI firms, in which the same companies act as investors, suppliers and customers of one another. Between 2021 and 2025, 28.7% of AI firms' investment deals by value targeted another AI firm, and 55.2% of incoming investment in AI firms came from other AI firms. Among these AI-to-AI deals, 16.1% by count and 46.4% by value also involved a commercial supply relationship between investor and target. There is, of course, a modicum of economic logic to this: such arrangements h elp firms secure scarce inputs such as chips and compute , and bridge information gaps between investors and young companies. But they also ma ke the system more opaque and carry macroeconomic risks . Because the same capital can support both a firm's financing and its revenues, stress at one node can spread quickly across the network. The concern is more acute because AI capex is increasingly debt-funded. A follow-up paper puts hyperscaler capital expenditure at over $800bn this year, roughly 2.5% of US GDP , with gross bond issuance of $222bn by September.
And circular, debt-financed vehicles just run into a thick wall of (mostly blue) voters. Markets are naturally worried that the electorate, fearful of the environmental and social impact of AI, might slow the development of US-driven AI.
After all, 75 % of Americans oppose local AI data centres, across party lines, driven by power and water costs. The European Data Centre Monitor suggests that data centres consume one fourth of electricity in Ireland. Back in the US, 80 % favour strict AI safety rules and 68 % support legislation to pause AI development and ban superintelligence. In Europe, the numbers are similar. 84 % of Europeans demand careful workplace AI management.

Let's break down the questions markets are really asking:
Are fears that voters can block AI development and unravel what is, in essence, a leveraged financing system realistic?
According to J.P. Morgan, most of the 2027 projects are already funded, so a pipeline collapse would not be imminent.
Can voters press the issue?
Yes, at the edges. Voters have passed 45 ballot measures on data centres and most cap energy use or tighten approval requirements. Governors can slow projects, or stop them with state-level moratoria, but those powers have limits and courts can hear challenges.
Can delays in projects cause a big issue for markets?
Potentially, where covenants are too demanding, but there are three notable upsides.
A delay would push back the release of more capable models, which also need more computing power. That would take the pressure off AI companies to produce a breakthrough every few weeks and give businesses time to plan around the tools they already have. This would take the pressure off AI companies to come up with the next big thing in a few weeks, and buy time for the corporate world to adapt and plan around present tools.
They would also give a chance to the "slower money" to step in and fill the financing gap.
A slowdown in deployment could also mitigate some of the damage to the jobs market and allow society to adapt more seamlessly to the new technology.
An AI development slowdown has really one big snag: the geopolitical. A "Sputnik moment". If China continues to develop AI at the present pace, while the US and allies slow, then it could potentially reach some form of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) sooner than its competitor. Even then, if history teaches anything, "Sputnik moments" can cause voters to change their tack and demand that the US catch up. One can't but wonder: would Neil Armstrong have walked on the moon if Soviet Sputnik hadn't terrified America? Can a Chinese AI lead produce another "humanity to the moon" moment?
What should businesses make of the election and AI?
There are many twists and turns, but AI continues to be an intergenerational technological theme that has the potential to shape life on the planet. A delay buys time, and firms that use it should have [specific steps, for example retrained staff, redesigned processes, an agreed AI policy] in place before the next generation of models arrives.
What should investors know about the recent tech rout?
For one, Nvidia needs to be separated from the theme. Much of the company's woes (-22% since mid-October) are idiosyncratic, and not fundamental. Rather, in the grand scheme of things, they are of the lighter governance issues. With over 25 years in the market and a commanding market share in data centres, it is a company that may well survive the potential departure of its chief executive officer (CEO). Meanwhile, for the same period, the wider Magnificent 7 (which includes Nvidia) is down 6%, just double the wider general index. Second, the AI theme, even slowed down, stays very much intact. Driving it is not just speculative market sentiment but rather very strong political will, not to mention actual technological momentum. Having said that, investors may even welcome a market with lower levels of concentration and broader leadership. And remember that by and large, markets tend to do better after the midterms. In the 25 post-midterm 6-month periods, a small sample, arguably, the average annualised return for US large caps has been 24%, against a 9.3% return post-presidential elections.

# Building AI on credit: structures and spillovers

##### Key takeaways

- *Capital expenditure to build artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure is growing fast, and a rising share* *is financed with long-term debt and through complex off-balance sheet arrangements.*
- *Off-balance sheet commitments from a few large firms help to transform the credit rating of the debt,* *drawing in institutional capital. Banks often lend to both the vehicles and investors holding the debt.*
- *On AI-financing-related news days in 2026, spillovers to US Treasury and investment-grade bond* *markets were small and short-lived, but sustained issuance at the current pace could change this.* Artificial intelligence (AI) is a general purpose technology that promises large productivity gains across the global economy. As with other such innovations that have come before, most of those gains are expected to arrive only once the technology is embedded in the economy’s capital stock and production processes. Large US hyperscalers, a smaller set of newer cloud providers (the so-called neoclouds) and AI labs, support the capacity build-out. Capital expenditure by the five largest US hyperscalers is expected to reach more than $800 billion by the end of 2026 or around 2.5% of US GDP (Graph 1.A). And the build-out is expected to continue in the coming years.

These AI investments are increasingly built on credit. Gross bond issuance this year by the five largest hyperscalers reached $222 billion by September 2026 (Graph 1.B). In addition, debt is raised through off-balance sheet vehicles. 2 According to market sources, $75 to $100 billion of bonds and asset-backed securities linked to these vehicles have been placed so far this year. This Bulletin addresses two questions. The first concerns how emerging financing structures allocate credit risk within the AI ecosystem and who bears losses if returns fall short of expectations. We provide a mapping of the emerging structures through which these risks are allocated. Off-balance sheet vehicles can draw institutional capital into the build-out by combining asset backing with lease commitments, guarantees and vendor backstops. The second question involves broader spillovers from the borrowing. Much of the new debt is long- dated and placed with non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs) that also hold long-dated government and investment-grade corporate bonds. A growing supply could therefore affect the compensation these investors require for bearing duration risk, with consequences for benchmark yields and borrowing costs beyond the AI sector. This Bulletin finds that so far, such spillovers have been limited. On AI-financing- related news days in 2026, movements in Treasury term premia and investment-grade corporate spreads were small and short-lived. ## Emerging credit relationships and risks in the AI credit boom

The AI build-out is financed through a web of on- and off-balance sheet commitments supported by a handful of investment-grade firms. These structures benefit the firms building AI capacity. Neoclouds and AI labs gain access to long- dated investment-grade funding from a deep pool of investors. Hyperscalers gain capacity without adding debt directly to their balance sheets. The same structures might also carry risks to financial stability, especially if their adoption becomes more widespread.

First, exposures are concentrated. The lease commitments and guarantees rest on a small number of large firms, leaving investors dependent on the financial strength of these companies. While hyperscalers generate large cash flows from non-AI segments of their businesses, their aggregate off-balance sheet commitments already exceed their reported debt (Moody’s Ratings (2026)).

Second, there are large interconnections. For instance, a chip supplier may have several links to the same customer. It may sell hardware to the customer, hold an equity stake, purchase its capacity and guarantee some of its payments. If that customer encounters difficulties, the supplier could be affected through each of these channels. Banks may also be exposed directly, through construction loans to vehicles, and indirectly, through loans to the funds that hold vehicle debt.

Third, the contractual support protects investors only in part, leaving them exposed to falls in the value of data centres and chips. If a hyperscaler does not renew a lease or terminates it early, the vehicle may need to find a new tenant or realise the value of the assets, potentially at a time when similar capacity is also being offered to the market. In that event, the vehicle can fall back on residual value guarantees, but they are typically capped.

Finally, the structures are opaque, making risks hard to assess. Leases that have not yet started are disclosed but not recorded as debt. Guarantees are often recorded at little or no value until payments become likely. Such claims are harder to size than bonds. Without a clear view of the ultimate guarantor, lenders may hold larger exposures to a single firm than they realise. An insurer that holds a hyperscaler’s bonds and the debt of several vehicles leased to it, for example, could be more exposed to that hyperscaler than its bond holdings alone suggest. Opacity could also make the market for this debt freeze in stress, as investors struggle to value it. Since most of the debt is long term, a run on short-term funding like in the Great Financial Crisis appears less likely. But losses could still fall on the insurers, funds and other investors that hold the debt as well as on their bank lenders, and opacity could delay their recognition. Closer monitoring and fuller disclosure of these commitments would help investors, rating agencies and supervisors assess the risks.

After accounting for changes in the two-year yield and FOMC statement days, yields rose more at longer maturities on AI-financing-related news days (Graph 3.B). The 90% confidence band excludes zero at maturities from eight to 30 years. The pattern is consistent with higher compensation for duration risk but is not causal evidence.

Immediate spillovers to Treasury and investment-grade corporate bond markets therefore appear limited, but the potential pressure is growing. In 2026, hyperscaler bonds with maturities of 10 years or more accounted for about 24% of corporate issuance at those maturities and 13% of gross Treasury issuance, up from about 3% and 1.5%, respectively, in 2024 (Graph 3.C). Off-balance sheet vehicles add further long-dated claims. If supply expands at this rate, a concentrated group of duration investors will need to absorb a larger stock of claims with correlated exposures. This could exert greater pressure on long-term yields and, where leverage is shifted into opaque vehicles, add to financial stability risks.

# Years of Polling Shows Overwhelming Voter Support for a Crackdown on AI

*AnyAlawmakerAalignedAwithAAIAandABigATechAisAonAtheAwrongAsideAofApublicAopinion.A*

### Public Citizen | April 2026

American voters in both parties overwhelmingly oppose AI’s development and deployment, support aggressive regulation or prohibition of AI, and reject efforts to stop state-level regulation of AI, especially to protect kids online. These findings are consistent across <u>dozens of polls</u> conducted over the past decade – with public opinion turning even more sharply against AI and Big Tech in the last few years, as AI company recklessness and abuses have become more evident. The polls show strong and consistent demand among Democratic, Independent, and Republican voters for a crackdown against Big Tech companies generally and AI specifically.

## <u>Across Parties, the Public Agrees AI Must be Regulated</u>

### Almost all Americans 6NL17 Agree That AI Safety and Security Should Be Subject

***to Rules and Regulations*** (<u>Gallup / Special Competitive Studies Project: Sept. 2025</u>).

- 80% of U.S. adults believe the government should maintain rules for AI safety and data security, even if it means developing AI capabilities more slowly. This view is held by 88% of Democrats and 79% of Republicans and Independents.
- More than seven in 10 (72%) say independent experts should conduct safety tests and evaluations of AI products and services.
- Six in 10 Americans distrust AI somewhat (40%) or fully (20%).
- Only 2% of U.S. adults fully trust AI’s capability to make fair and unbiased decisions, a figure within the poll’s margin of error.
***AI is the Industry Americans Most Want to Regulate*** (<u>YouGov: Sept. 2024</u>).

- 72% of Americans want more regulation of the AI industry, a 15-point increase since a similar poll a year earlier. This includes 83% of Harris supporters and 66% of Trump supporters.
- Across the 40 industries asked about in the poll, AI is the industry that Americans in general and Trump supporters specifically are most eager to regulate.

***Two Thirds of Americans Want AI Regulation*** (<u>Center Square Voters’ Voice / Noble</u> <u>Predictive Insights: April 2025</u>).

- 64% of registered voters want the government to regulate AI.
### On a Bipartisan Basis, Voters Overwhelmingly Favor Candidates Who Support AI

***Regulation*** (<u>Artificial Intelligence Policy Institute: Jan. 2024</u>).

- 76% of voters, including 71% of Republicans, prefer a candidate running in their district who supports regulating AI because the risks it poses are so large.
- 77% of voters, including 75% of Republicans, say government should do more to regulate AI.
### By a 6-1 Margin, Americans Reject Tech Industry Self-Regulation of AI Risks

(<u>YouGov / Artificial Intelligence Policy Institute: Aug. 2023</u>).

- 72% percent of voters said they preferred slowing down the development of AI.
- 82% of voters said they don’t trust tech company executives to self-regulate, and 56% said they would support a federal agency to regulate AI.
## <u>Americans Losing Faith in AI’s Potential for the Economy</u>

***There is a Large Lack of Trust in AI*** (<u>IPSOS: March 2026</u>).

- Almost 75% of Americans believe there needs to be action taken by the government to prevent job loss from AI.
- 66% of Americans agreed that the development of AI needs to slow down, while 76% feel that people need to keep up with the advancement of AI.
- Only 11% of corporate communicators believe existing ethical policies are sufficient for widespread AI adoption.
***Voters Increasingly Think AI Is Likely to Hurt the Economy*** (<u>Data For Progress:</u> <u>February 2026</u>).

- 46% of voters think AI will likely hurt the economy, rising nine points from December 2025.
- Only 28% of voters think AI will likely help the economy, down eight points from December 2025.
### Advancements in AI Will Likely Lead to Decreased Job Opportunities According

***to 70% of Americans*** (<u>Quinnipiac: March 2026</u>).

- These views are shared among Americans regardless of work environment, with 71% of white-collar workers and 73% of blue-collar workers believe AI will lead to a decrease in job opportunities.
- Gen Z is the most concerned, with 81% thinking there will be a decrease.
- Americans are overwhelmingly unwilling (80%) to work in a job where their direct supervisor was an AI program.

### Americans Overwhelmingly Reject the Idea that Competition with China Justifies

***Leaving AI Unregulated*** (<u>Artificial Intelligence Policy Institute: July 2024</u>).

- Three out of four American voters are skeptical of the argument that the U.S. should race ahead to build ever more powerful artificial intelligence, unconstrained by domestic regulations, in an effort to compete with China.
- 75% of Democrats and 75% of Republicans believe that “taking a careful, controlled approach” to AI by preventing the release of tools that terrorists and foreign adversaries could use against the U.S. is preferable to “moving forward on AI as fast as possible to be the first country to get extremely powerful AI.”
## <u>Americans Reject Federal Preemption of State AI Regulation</u>

### Americans Overwhelmingly Oppose Federal Preemption of State AI Regulations

(<u>YouGov / Institute for Family Studies: Nov. 2025</u>).

- American voters oppose adding artificial intelligence preemption provisions to the National Defense Authorization Act by a decisive 3-to-1 margin. 57% of Americans oppose congressional efforts to block states from regulating AI through military spending legislation, while only 19% support such measures.
- Among Trump voters, 43% oppose preemption compared to just 25% who support it. Among Harris voters, 70% oppose the provision.
### By a Greater Than 2-1 Margin, Voters Overwhelmingly Reject Federal Preemption

***of State AI Regulation*** (<u>Echelon Insights / Common Sense Media: May 2025</u>).

- Nearly three out of five likely voters oppose a moratorium on state-level AI regulation, including half of Republicans. A majority (53%) of likely voters said they trusted state and local leaders more than politicians in Congress when it comes to regulating AI appropriately.
- By a more than three-to-one margin, voters trust states to regulate AI over the federal government.
- 81% of likely voters agree that Congress should not ban states from enacting or enforcing their own laws when it comes to protecting kids’ safety and privacy online. 87% agree that states should be able to enforce laws and regulations around social media, including requiring warning labels for children.

## <u>Americans Are Demanding Stronger AI Regulation</u>

***80% of Voters Say Go Slow on AI Development*** (<u>Fox News: Dec. 2025</u>)

- 8 in 10 voters favor a careful approach to developing AI to manage potential risks to the U.S.
- Only 1 in 10 voters think the president should regulate AI.
### Americans Overwhelming Support 6LH17 “SlowA Heavily Regulated” Development

***of AI*** (<u>Future of Life Institute: Oct. 2025</u>).

- 57% of adults reject the development of “expert-level AI” under current conditions; 17% feel it should never be developed; and 40% think it should be halted until proven controllable and safe. Shockingly, only 5% of adults agree that AI should be developed “as quickly as possible.”
- Almost three-in-four Americans support regulation “like pharmaceuticals with extensive testing before deployment.” Two-thirds (64%) of adults would like to see an “immediate pause” on the development of advanced AI until the technology is proven to be safe.
***Public Worries About Inadequate AI Regulation*** (<u>Pew Research Center: April 2025</u>).

- Both the public and experts worry the U.S. government will not go far enough in regulating AI. About six in ten U.S. adults and 56% of experts surveyed say they’re more concerned about this than about the government going too far.
- Majorities in both parties – 64% of Democrats and 55% of Republicans – are concerned about insufficient AI regulation.
- 59% of the public and 55% of surveyed experts have little or no confidence in
U.S. companies to develop and use AI responsibly.
***Americans Agree the Country Needs More AI Regulation*** (<u>Quinnipiac: April 2025</u>).

- 73% of Americans think that businesses are not doing enough to be transparent about their use of AI.
- 69% of Americans think that the government is not doing enough to regulate AI.
- 86% of Americans are either very or somewhat concerned about political leaders using AI to distribute fake or misleading information.
***Americans Are Worried About AI Risks*** (<u>Pew Research Center: Sept. 2025</u>)

- A majority of Americans (57%) rate the risks of AI for society as high. Far fewer (25%) see high benefits.

## <u>Americans Support a Wide Range of Specific AI Regulations</u>

***Americans Support Required Pre-Approval of New AI Technologies*** (<u>AI Policy</u> <u>Institute for Transformer: Jan. 2025</u>).

- 73% of Americans support requiring companies to implement safety measures and security standards for advanced AI models, and mandatory pre-deployment approval from a government board that certified companies have properly accounted for extreme risks. Democrats and Republicans gave this proposal identical levels of support.
- Only 7% of Americans want no regulation of AI.
### Americans Overwhelmingly Favor Requiring Disclosure of Deepfake Political Ads

(<u>Tech Policy Press / YouGov: Aug. 2024</u>).

- 87% of voters back a disclosure requirement for generative AI in political ads.
- 78% of voters want a ban on deceptive AI-generated content on social media that targets a candidate for federal office with the intent of influencing an election or soliciting funds.
- 77% of voters want candidates for federal office to be able to sue to take down content when their voice or likeness is used in deceptive AI-generated content.
### Voters Overwhelmingly Believe in Regulating Deepfakes and the Use of Artificial

***Intelligence*** (<u>Data for Progress / Accountable Tech: Feb. 2024</u>)

- 85% of Democrats, 84% of Independents, and 81% of Republicans believe AI companies should be required to label AI-generated content when it’s used to influence an election.
***Americans Favor Regulation to Protect Creators from AI Training*** (<u>Artificial</u> <u>Intelligence Policy Institute: May 2024</u>).

- 60% of respondents said AI companies should not be able to train freely on public data, and nearly three out of four respondents said companies should be required to compensate the creators of that data.
- 78% said there should be regulations on the use of public data to train AI models.
- 61% favored taxing AI companies’ high energy use to support and upgrade the electrical grid infrastructure.

***Parents Want More Guardrails on AI for Their Children*** (<u>Echelon Insights: February</u>

<u>2026</u>).
- 79% of parents want to be involved in decisions relating to AI tools at their children’s schools.
- Parents overwhelmingly (86%) believe AI chatbots should be required by law to provide warnings to minors before displaying text or content related to violence, self-harm, or abuse.
- 85% of parents think it should be required by law to alert a minor’s parents if their child discusses something harmful or illegal with a chatbot.
- 84% of parents would prefer having parental controls over AI chatbots, allowing them to limit what topics their children can discuss with chatbots.
- 79% of parents feel minors need permission from a parent before using chatbots.
### There is Massive Public Concern About Impact of AI Chatbots and Companions

***on Kids*** (<u>Echelon Insights / Common Sense Media: May 2025</u>).

- 85% of registered voters are concerned about the effect of AI chatbots and AI companions on kids and teenagers.
- 92% of registered voters are concerned about the impact of deepfakes.
- 93% of registered voters are concerned about kids being exposed to AI-generated sexualized content.
### Americans Worried About AI Deployment in Transportation, Job Decisions,

***Lending Decisions*** (<u>Axios / Illinois Institute of Technology: Dec. 2021</u>).

- More than 70% of respondents distrust the use of AI for self-driving buses and airplane autopilot systems.
- 69% percent said they would limit the use of AI for hiring decisions.
- Nearly 60% distrust AI for loan applications and mortgage rates.
- More than seven in ten liberals and conservatives overwhelmingly agree that there should be public or government oversight of the use of algorithms.
- Three-quarters of those polled said tech companies are too big (80% of liberals and 83% of conservatives).
- 81% think the government should be doing more to protect online privacy.
### About 79% of Americans Say a Human Being Should Always Make The Final

***Decision Before Any Use of Lethal Force*** (<u>Information Technology and Innovation</u> <u>Foundation: Feb. 2026</u>).

- 81% of Republicans and Democrats believe humans should always make the final decision before any use of lethal force.
- About 75% feel that AI technology isn’t reliable enough to be trusted with life-or-death military decisions.
- A little over half (54%) of adults are very concerned about AI-controlled weapons that select and fire on targets without human involvement.
- Only 21% of Americans support developing and deploying AI-controlled weapons.
