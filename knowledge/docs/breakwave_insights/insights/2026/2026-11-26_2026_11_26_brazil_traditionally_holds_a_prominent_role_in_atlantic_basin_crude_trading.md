## Main
By Nikolaos Tagoulis
Brazil traditionally holds a prominent role in Atlantic Basin crude trading, with the expansion of its offshore production further underpinning its influence in global oil flows. Rising output is increasing export availability and providing greater flexibility in directing cargoes between European and Asian markets, while offering buyers a source of crude supply largely insulated from geopolitical disruptions. Moreover, on the policy side, favourable trade terms under the EU-Mercosur agreement provide an additional commercial incentive for crude shipments to Europe.
The driving force behind Brazil's production growth is its vast pre-salt fields, where substantial oil and gas reservoirs lie beneath thick salt formations deep below the Atlantic seabed. According to the country's National Agency of Petroleum, Natural Gas and Biofuels (ANP), crude output reached a record 4.71 million b/d in September 2026, up 20.3% y-o-y, with nearly 84% originating from pre-salt fields. At the heart of this expansion lies the Santos Basin, off Brazil's southeastern coast, which accounts for around 80% of national crude production and hosts some of the country's largest offshore oil fields, notably Búzios and Mero. The scale of the Búzios oil field is particularly striking, with production reaching 1.19 million b/d, equivalent to approximately one-quarter of Brazil's total crude output. With additional FPSOs scheduled to commence operations at the Búzios field in 2027, Brazil is well positioned to consolidate its role as a major crude exporter outside OPEC.
The rise in output is also reflected in seaborne volumes, with Europe gaining ground as a destination without displacing Brazil's established Asian markets. According to LSEG data, Brazilian crude exports rose 21.9% y-o-y to 719 million barrels during January-October 2026. EU-bound shipments increased 19.3% to 137.8 million barrels, while China received 15% more and India's intake almost tripled. The sharper change in European trade came after April. Following a decline of 12.9% yo-y during January-April, EU-bound volumes rebounded by 41% over May-October, with October shipments alone more than doubling y-o-y.
This increase may partly be attributed to the EU-Mercosur agreement. Signed in January and provisionally applied from 1 May, the agreement aims to deepen bilateral trade relations. However, Brazilian government's decision to impose a 12% crude export tax in March gave Mercosur's preferential provisions greater commercial importance. Under the agreement, crude exports to the EU are subject to a reduced 6% rate, compared with the full 12% levy imposed on non-EU destinations, providing EU buyers an advantage in securing Brazilian barrels. The timing is particularly relevant for the EU, given its efforts to diversify away from Russian energy and the geopolitical uncertainty affecting oil supplies and tanker movements through the Arabian Gulf.
For the crude tanker market, the impact of Brazil's expanding crude exports largely depends on the geographical distribution of trade flows. Increased shipments to Europe support employment for VLCCs and Suezmaxes, which account for the bulk of these flows. However, while additional cargoes to Europe generate greater transatlantic activity, any diversion of shipments previously destined for Asian longer-haul markets, would shorten average voyage distances, potentially offsetting some of the gains in ton-miles. At the same time, India's neartripling of Brazilian crude imports lends support to long-haul tanker demand, while rising Brazilian production creates scope for further growth in export volumes. Moreover, Brazil's rising offshore production also supports demand for shuttle tankers able to operate in open-sea conditions, equipped with dynamic positioning (DP) systems, which transport crude from FPSOs to coastal terminals or STS transfer locations.
Aside from tankers, the impact for the offshore segment is clearly positive. Brazil represents a principal employment region for such vessels, with sustained investment in deepwater field development underpinning demand for PSVs, AHTSs and specialised subsea units. Further expansion of offshore activity is expected to strengthen demand for offshore vessel capacity, creating additional employment opportunities.
Whether EU retains its preferential tax advantage beyond 2026 depends on Brazil's crude export levy. The tax has been extended through three successive government resolutions, most recently in November, until 31 December 2026. If extended, EU-bound shipments would continue to benefit from the lower rate. If the levy expires, exports to all destinations would become exempt, removing Europe's relative advantage and potentially intensifying competition for Brazilian barrels. More broadly, Brazil's growing offshore oil output is establishing its position as a major global supplier, with both the tanker and offshore segments poised to benefit.
