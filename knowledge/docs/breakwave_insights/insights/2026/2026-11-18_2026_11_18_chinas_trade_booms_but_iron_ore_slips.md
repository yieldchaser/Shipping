## Main
By Ulf Bergman
Last week's Chinese trade data confirmed that the country's trade surplus will top one trillion dollars for a second consecutive year, highlighting the continued challenges that many countries face in their efforts to reduce dependence on imports from the world's second-largest economy. Additionally, compared with last year, the one trillion mark was reached earlier, and a new record looks likely.
Still, according to data released earlier this week, the Chinese economy is facing renewed headwinds. Industrial production and retail sales growth slowed last month and missed market expectations.

## Stronger Trade, but Softer Economy
In recent years, Chinese economic data releases have often contradicted each other. The past ten days have provided yet another such example. Chinese exports and imports exceeded market expectations, with the latter beating estimates comfortably. Last month, exports grew a solid 35.2 per cent year-on-year, beating the consensus forecast of 33.5 per cent. However, growth fell somewhat short of the 36.3 per cent recorded in September. The export numbers were underpinned by strong overseas demand for chips and AI-related equipment, as well as a low base for shipments to the US following last year's tariff escalation.
For imports, October's reading surpassed already lofty expectations. At 41.6 per cent, it cleared the consensus projection of 32 per cent by a considerable margin. It was also a significant increase from September's 31.0 per cent. A strong rebound in Chinese purchases of crude oil contributed significantly to the growth in imports.
In contrast to the bullish trade data, last month's industrial output and domestic consumption data proved underwhelming. While both gauges remained in growth territory, they fell short of both consensus and the previous month's readings. After reaching a six-month high in September, industrial production slowed to 4.8 per cent in October, the weakest since July. Market expectations had suggested that the strong momentum would be broadly maintained.
Growth in retail sales in the world's second-largest economy fell back to a meagre 0.4 per cent after a relatively strong 1.1 per cent in September. Analysts had expected some loss of momentum, but not to half the forecast rate. The decelerating growth in consumption spending highlights the continued challenges facing Beijing in its quest to reduce reliance on export-driven economic growth.

## A Mixed Picture for Dry Bulk Shipments
According to data from Signal Ocean, just over 208 million tonnes of dry bulk cargoes were discharged in Chinese ports last month. The aggregate was 0.7 per cent higher than in October last year. The total volume edged up despite a 5.2 per cent year-on-year decline in iron ore imports. Over the past four years, the steelmaking ingredient has accounted for approximately 55 per cent of the seaborne dry bulk flow bound for China. However, the decline in iron ore volumes was more than offset by robust bauxite discharges, which surged by 55.4 per cent.
Among the other major dry bulk seaborne imports, coal remained broadly in line with volumes recorded a year ago but rose by 14.3 per cent compared with September. For grains and oilseeds, discharged volumes were depressed, with a 10.5 per cent year-on-year decline and nearly a quarter lower than in September.
Given the time lags involved in the seaborne trade, a focus on exports bound for China rather than imported volumes could provide a better indication of how the flow of commodities responds to rising economic headwinds. Dry bulk commodities loaded on vessels bound for China during the past month declined by 1.5 per cent compared with October last year. While October's aggregate was higher than in the same month in 2023 and 2024, the retreat from last year's level may reflect the softness observed in this week's economic data.
A closer look at Signal Ocean's data for major commodities shows mixed fortunes. As with imports, iron ore faced headwinds, with exported volumes 2.5 per cent lower than a year ago. Still, there was a silver lining for the steelmaking ingredient, as October's month-on-month growth of 1.8 per cent was higher than in the same period in 2023, 2024 and 2025. In contrast, growth in bauxite shipments bound for China remained robust last month. Year-on-year and month-on-month expansion reached 13.6 per cent and 16.2 per cent, respectively. The continued rise in bauxite exports to China broadly offset the year-on-year decline for iron ore.
Coal export shipments destined for Chinese ports saw a continued seasonal rebound in October. However, despite volumes rising by 14.3 per cent from September, October's aggregate was 1.6 per cent lower than a year ago and 22.4 per cent lower than in 2024. Grain and oilseed exports for China delivered a robust October, with year-on-year growth at a healthy 19 per cent. Chinese purchases of US agricultural commodities accounted for much of the growth. Despite the strong rebound, total volumes bound for China were nearly 40 per cent lower than in 2023.

## Soft Economic Data Could Weigh on Seaborne Volumes
As a previous Insights article noted, dry bulk trade flow data is often a poor leading indicator of the Chinese economy. The reverse can still hold, however, and this time around softer economic data may signal headwinds for seaborne dry bulk imports. While early indications from Signal Ocean for the current month suggest that bauxite and agricultural commodities will continue to enjoy robust volumes, developments for iron ore and coal look set to be negative, with more year-on-year declines.
Beyond soft economic data, iron ore imports may also suffer as Chinese steel rebar prices have fallen to the lowest levels since June 2025 amid weak demand. Continued downward pressure on steel mills' profit margins could push crude steel output lower, following October's 1.6 per cent decline year-on-year.
Given the traditional dominance of iron ore and coal in China's seaborne dry bulk trade, continued weakness in both could put pressure on Chinese demand for seaborne transportation for the remainder of the year and weigh on freight rates, particularly in the capesize segment. Robust bauxite shipments from Guinea, a long-haul trade, should provide some offset in tonne-mile terms.
