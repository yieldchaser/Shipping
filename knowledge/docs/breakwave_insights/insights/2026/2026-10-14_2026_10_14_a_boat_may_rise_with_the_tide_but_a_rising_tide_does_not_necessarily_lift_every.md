## Main
By Michalis Voutsinas
A boat may rise with the tide, but a rising tide does not necessarily lift every boat. The same asymmetry is increasingly apparent in global trade, where the expansion of international commerce is being driven by forces whose implications for the underlying industrial economy remain far from uniform. Artificial intelligence is catalysing an unprecedented investment cycle in digital infrastructure, reshaping trade flows and lifting headline growth projections, while geopolitical conflict is disrupting the supply of essential resources and raising production costs. The World Trade Organization's October outlook captures this divergence: world merchandise trade volume is now projected to expand by 3.9 percent in 2026, more than twice the 1.9 percent forecast published in March. Yet this upward revision reflects, to a considerable extent, the exceptional momentum of technology-related trade rather than a commensurate acceleration across commodity-intensive industries. For dry bulk shipping, the composition of trade growth is therefore as important as its headline rate. The critical question is how much of the expansion in international commerce translates into incremental industrial production, physical resource consumption and seaborne commodity demand.
According to the WTO, trade in AI-enabling goods increased by 67 percent year-on-year in value terms during the first half of 2026. Accounting for 14.8 percent of global merchandise trade value, these products generated 47 percent of its growth over the period. Semiconductor shipments, servers, data-centre equipment and associated digital infrastructure have consequently emerged as disproportionately important contributors to international commerce. Against this backdrop, the WTO has raised its merchandise trade growth projections to 3.9 percent for 2026 and 4.1 percent for 2027, signalling considerable near-term momentum despite a challenging geopolitical environment.
The composition of this expansion, however, warrants closer examination. Trade statistics aggregate transactions with markedly different economic characteristics, from high-value, low-weight electronic equipment to the bulk commodities underpinning steelmaking, electricity generation, construction and food production. Their contributions to measured trade growth are not commensurate with their implications for physical transportation. A relatively compact shipment of advanced technology equipment can generate substantial trade value, whereas a cargo of iron ore represents a much greater physical volume but a different contribution to international trade value. Consequently, faster merchandise trade growth does not necessarily imply a proportional increase in industrial throughput, raw-material consumption or demand for dry bulk transportation.
This divergence reflects a structural shift in the composition of global investment. Expenditure on artificial intelligence is increasingly concentrated in computing capacity, semiconductor manufacturing, data centres and the supporting digital ecosystem. Such investment generates substantial cross-border flows of specialised equipment, but its broader commodity implications depend on the extent to which it stimulates additional physical investment elsewhere in the economy. Data centres require electricity, cooling systems, grid connections and construction materials, potentially generating further demand for power generation, transmission infrastructure, metals and building materials. The scale of these spillovers will depend on the geographical distribution of new facilities, their energy requirements, the technologies used to supply electricity and whether associated expenditure creates genuinely additional capacity or merely relocates existing activity.
The relationship between digital capital expenditure and bulk commodity demand is therefore neither immediate nor linear. Imports of servers and semiconductor equipment register directly in trade statistics, whereas the effects on steel consumption, electricity generation and infrastructure development emerge through successive stages of investment and production. The economic significance of the AI boom for dry bulk shipping will ultimately depend on whether its initial concentration in digital capital formation evolves into a broader expansion of the physical economy. Such a transition would establish a more durable transmission mechanism from technology expenditure to industrial output and, ultimately, commodity transportation. Until then, the exceptional performance of technology-related trade should not be interpreted as evidence of an equally strong recovery across traditional commodity markets.
The supply-side environment introduces a further complication. The Middle East conflict has disrupted the availability and pricing of essential resources, including energy and fertilisers, exposing the vulnerability of global production networks to geopolitical shocks. The consequences extend beyond the direct cost of energy imports. Higher input prices can compress industrial margins, weaken household purchasing power, alter production decisions and defer capital expenditure. In agriculture, fertiliser affordability and availability influence planting economics and input intensity; in manufacturing, energy costs affect production competitiveness and the viability of energy-intensive processes. These pressures can therefore constrain activity across sectors with markedly different exposure to international trade and commodity markets.
The interaction between technology-led investment and resource-market disruption creates an uneven macroeconomic backdrop. While digital infrastructure spending supports cross-border shipments of manufactured goods, elevated energy and input costs threaten to constrain activity in industries that generate substantial demand for bulk commodities. Aggregate trade can consequently remain buoyant even as the underlying conditions for steel production, industrial coal consumption or agricultural input demand deteriorate. Nor should these forces be assumed to offset one another mechanically. Growth in highvalue technology imports does not necessarily compensate for weaker industrial output, just as stronger merchandise trade does not establish that the physical consumption of raw materials is accelerating at a comparable pace. The result is an economy in which headline growth indicators may obscure materially different trajectories across individual sectors.
The outlook for 2027 merits particular scrutiny. The WTO's projected 4.1 percent expansion in merchandise trade suggests that international commerce should retain considerable momentum. Nevertheless, the exceptional contribution of AI-related goods in 2026 creates a higher comparison base, while continued geopolitical uncertainty leaves the outlook exposed to renewed energy-market volatility and its effects on production costs. The durability of trade growth will depend partly on whether investment broadens into power generation, grid development, construction and additional industrial capacity. If these second-round effects materialise, the technology cycle could generate meaningful incremental demand for steel, energy and other physical inputs. Conversely, if expenditure remains concentrated in high-value equipment and specialised digital assets, with more limited spillovers into commodity-intensive industries, its contribution to traditional dry bulk trades may prove less pronounced than headline trade projections suggest.
For dry bulk shipping, the implications of the current trade cycle ultimately depend on commodity-specific fundamentals and the extent to which aggregate growth translates into physical production and seaborne transportation. Iron ore demand remains closely linked to steel production, mill profitability and construction activity, while coal flows reflect electricity demand, industrial consumption and the competitiveness of alternative energy sources; agricultural trades, meanwhile, are shaped by harvest outcomes, feed demand, fertiliser economics and trade policy. Vessel employment is further influenced by sourcing patterns, voyage distances and the geographical redistribution of trade, with geopolitical disruption capable of redirecting cargoes and extending tonne-miles even where underlying consumption remains unchanged. The WTO's upward revision provides a constructive macroeconomic backdrop, but the durability of its implications for dry bulk demand will depend on whether technology-led investment generates broader gains in industrial production and resource consumption, rather than remaining concentrated in high-value manufactured goods. Ultimately, the pace of global trade growth matters less in isolation than its composition, material intensity and geographical reach, which together determine how effectively expanding international commerce translates into sustained cargo volumes and tonne-mile demand.
