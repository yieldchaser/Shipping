## Summary
Transportation Fuels Prospects 2009‐2015,
The global economic crisis has slowed the forces that drove crude oil prices to record highs in the summer of 2008, but economists generally concede it has not stopped them. ## Transportation Fuels Prospects 2009‐2015,
The global economic crisis has slowed the forces that drove crude oil prices to record highs in the summer of 2008, but economists generally concede it has not stopped them. Coming years will force the energy industry to satiate developing world demand growth with an uncertain refining capacity footprint while under environmental constraints and persistent geopolitical risk. The period until 2015 will be most influential in forming a foundation from which the industry will face a future loaded with enormous challenges.
These and other fundamental issues are considered in Poten & Partner's upcoming multi‐ client study, Transportation Fuels Prospects 2009‐2015, scheduled for July 2009 publication.

## Desperately Seeking Demand
In its most recent report, the IEA estimates that worldwide oil demand this year will fall to an average of 83.2 million barrels per day; a 3 percent drop from 2008. The global slowdown has had a chilling effect on the petroleum industry: Crude oil prices and product demand have both fallen and their decline has slowed some major refinery projects which could significantly impact the timing of future supply.

## A Shifting Landscape
Upon the eventual recovery of oil demand, participants will encounter a market where established supply roles are evolving quickly and new pricing relationships are being forged.
The petroleum industry's center of activity has shifted eastward to the developing nations of Asia‐ led by China and India‐ where new refining capacity amid stagnant worldwide demand has chipped away at the "Asian premium" that previously added cost to barrels headed eastward. In the United States, a national conversation about the economy, national security and the environment explicitly aims to reduce America's dependence on foreign oil. European markets' preference for diesel over gasoline and environmental protection initiatives are changing trade flows in that region. The Middle East and Africa both strive to enhance their respective roles as suppliers while also fostering their own demand growth. In South America, Brazil provides an example of how to harness biofuels on a scale that impacts petroleum demand. Across the Former Soviet Union, countries are attempting to navigate a complicated position between their former benefactors in Moscow and customers in Europe.

## Known Unknowns
While supply and demand fundamentals along with price arbitrage will determine its basic direction, the international transportation fuels market will confront an increasingly complex environment in the years to come. In attempting to project that future environment, technology and policy are the biggest unknowns.
An evolution of environmental and tax policy as well as technology are necessary before biofuels can significantly impact transportation fuels consumption; biofuels accounted for just one percent of total road transportation fuel supply in 2008. It is now clear that while policies have succeeded in ensuring that biofuels will play a role in the future transportation fuels mix, a host of economic, infrastructural and political constraints also suggest that their role is not likely to be fully defined for years to come. Focus has sharpened on the development of "second‐generation" non‐grain feedstocks that should assuage food security concerns, but experts say this new generation is 10 to 15 years from wide‐scale adoption with many hurdles to yet overcome.
The expanding use of electronic and hybrid vehicles is unlikely to moderate transportation fuels demand within the medium term. Billions in research funding at home and abroad is bound to produce significant technological breakthroughs, but rapid mass‐scale adoption of these new technologies would take years even under typical fleet replacement trends. The challenges facing new vehicle technologies were made evident recently when the Energy Department announced an end to its research into hydrogen fuel‐cells, a technology previously hailed as key to a carbon‐neutral economy, because they will not be commercially practical over the next 10 to 20 years.
The economic crisis has inspired a lively international debate about the appropriate role of government at a time when many nations, including the United States, face challenges only governments have the resources to address. The blurring line between public and private interests will have tangible impacts on the energy industry as governments adjust policies such as price controls and subsidies that directly impact supply and demand and stringent environmental regulations that pose significant challenges to industry.

## Asian Recovery Leads the Way
The economic crisis will continue to dampen demand in the near‐term, but the fundamental assumption that links economic development with transportation sector growth remains essentially unchanged. Middle‐class aspiration was the driving force behind the expansion of the automobile in the United States and is not an exclusively American phenomenon. As the automobile continues to expand its place within the cultures of the rapidly‐developing demand centers of the East, supply of transportation fuels should provide opportunities for refiners and ship‐owners for decades to come.
