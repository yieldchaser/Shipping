## Restraining Order
After duking it out with the economy in the second half of 2008, the tanker and dry bulk market took a hard fought 12 th round tumble. Rumors have circulated that 20‐60% of the global orderbook may be cancelled or delayed. Shipyards have scrambled to enforce contracts with their clients and generate some semblance of a cash flow. On top of this uncertainty, outlooks from the International Energy Agency (IEA) seem continually revised to reflect falling global oil demand. This plight shared by the involved yards and owners is only going to get more painful.
Since the third quarter of 2008, this sentiment has been increasingly pervasive in articles and press releases-but it may be fundamentally flawed. The worldwide recession has undoubtedly affected the tanker and dry bulk markets, but it has not unleashed a demon. Instead, it has checked the exuberance of a market which contains a 50% unfinanced orderbook and yards that suffer from 15% to 35% orderbook slippage. New tanker supply has been curtailed in the wake of delays and the phase‐out of single hulls has slowed for tankers in low rate environments.
As can be seen below, the tanker orderbook is experiencing an imbalance in the ratio of deliveries to vessels left on order for this year. While uncovering the extent of these delays will not be known until the year is through, the carry over of fourth quarter 2008 deliveries into the beginning of this year hints at what may lie on the horizon.

## Look Familiar?
The $250 billion shortfall within a $500 billion orderbook resembles the VLCC ordering frenzy that took pace in the mid 1970's: in both cases, the number of unfinanced newbuildings presented a substantial problem. Currently, banks lack the capacity to fix this gap and with more rigorous credit requirements since the downturn began, they are reluctant to throw money at anyone, let alone in the shipping industry. This lack of financing appears grim, but it is useful in exposing the flimsy nature of the newbuilding orderbook.
For example, if a newbuilding contract never receives the final stamp of approval or adequate funding in the first place then its legitimacy as taking part in the orderbook is tentative at best. By removing these 'newbuildings' from an analysis of cancelled orders, the outlook no longer curdles the blood. The tanker orderbook would see approximately a 5% decrease and the dry bulk market-arguably the hardest hit sector-would lose roughly 10‐15% of its orderbook.
Throughout this ordering glut and global recession, both yards and owners have tried to maintain their delicate relationship, making cancelling a difficult endeavor. One notable exception to this is Frontline, who cancelled two VLCCs and four Suezmaxes; a move that reduced their orderbook by about a third, equivalent to $556 million. However, larger owners like Frontline, are generally granted more wiggle room than owners with only a few ships on order-and it is these owners who make up the majority of the orderbook.
In fact, the yards possess a remarkable amount of clout; despite being plagued by orderbook backlog, they have been highly resistant to price cuts or additional delays demanded by owners. The financial weight of the new IMO standards, which will be required in all vessels delivered after January 1, 2012, will include more efficient engines, water‐treatment systems and ballast tank coating. The new standards of coating alone are predicted to add roughly a 2% increase to newbuilding costs. The yards appear to be protecting their orderbooks from cancellations-a situation at odds with the reality that most tankers face a delay of 6 months or more.
A solution to this puzzle is government backing: by stimulating the yards and seeking to prevent ship abandonment, incentives are provided to both owners and yards to maintain a stable and constant relationship. With the financing and influence that a government's support can supply, both the tanker and dry bulk newbuilding markets will revive. They may not ricochet back to 2008 levels, but they could escape drastic decimation.
