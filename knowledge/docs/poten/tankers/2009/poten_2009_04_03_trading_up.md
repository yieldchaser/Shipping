## Summary
The G‐20 met this week in London to address key topics facing the global economic structure maintaining the theme of "Stability, Growth and Jobs." Over the two day period world leaders put their heads together to strike $1.1 trillion deal in an effort to pull the world out of its recession. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) was a major focal point of the summit and has been earmarked $500 billion to deepen its pockets and given a call to increase regulatory policy. On international trade finance, the G‐20 produced a $250 billion package that will likely have a direct impact on shipping.

## Trading Up?
The G‐20 met this week in London to address key topics facing the global economic structure maintaining the theme of "Stability, Growth and Jobs." Over the two day period world leaders put their heads together to strike $1.1 trillion deal in an effort to pull the world out of its recession. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) was a major focal point of the summit and has been earmarked $500 billion to deepen its pockets and given a call to increase regulatory policy.
On international trade finance, the G‐20 produced a $250 billion package that will likely have a direct impact on shipping.

## World Real GDP Growth
Ton‐Miles Since 1995, world year‐on‐year GDP percent change has peaked and troughed with VLCC ton‐miles suggesting a correlation between the two. According to the IMF growth in GDP is still expected albeit at a much lower rate than the past several years. While lower demand for oil and products seems to have had a negative impact on tanker demand recently, the bigger challenge on the horizon for tanker owners could be governmental policies that will ultimately impeded trade.

## Heal the World
With the conclusion of the summit in London on Thursday, "The Global Plan for Recovery and Reform" was published on the London Summit website (londonsummit.gov) highlighting six major topics agreed upon for righting the global economy. Below are listed the major goals.
• Restore confidence, growth and jobs • Repair the financial system to restore lending • Strengthen financial regulation to rebuild trust • Fund and reform our international financial institutions to overcome this crisis and prevent future ones • Promote global trade and investment and reject protectionism, to underpin prosperity • Build an inclusive, green, sustainable recovery While the meeting of the G‐20 touched on many global economic topics, trade was a major focus. Discussions of anti‐protectionism and maintaining open trade lanes were widespread and the sentiment was reflected in the opening lines of this section of the plan.
"Reinvigorating world trade and investment is essential for restoring global growth...we reaffirm the commitment made in Washington: to refrain from raising new barriers to investment or trade in goods and services, imposing new export restrictions, or implementing World Trade Organization (WTO) in consistent measures to stimulate exports." The G‐20 have made clear their intention to coddle world trade through this recession but in the end actions will speak louder than words - especially when it comes to the allocation of the $250 billion budgeted for supporting trade financing. US In the Mix In the chart below, the value of US imports and exports over the past 45+ years shows how growth in imports has outpaced growth in exports. Jobs have moved to where they are done better or cheaper and foreign products have become more popular, but over the years foreign capital became more abundant increasing the trade deficit as outside investors were drawn to US based investments.

## We Have Come So Far
In the afterglow of this summit, the G‐20 will return to their respective nations with ideals set forth in the plan for keeping the doors open on trade. Unfortunately, the global recession has already pushed many nations to more isolationist economical measures. For example, US government has created special incentives through monumental industry bail‐ outs and other modes of financial relief to save domestic businesses seemingly in vain. Simultaneously, cap and trade initiatives to be put in place will hurt manufacturing jobs and further impact the competitiveness of the US. The G‐20 may have the power to move and shift the sands of trade and with the careful application of the ideals set forth in their new plan, isolationism will be avoided and the natural flow of world commerce will help refloat the economies. The prospect of this happening, however, is seeming more and more bleak.
