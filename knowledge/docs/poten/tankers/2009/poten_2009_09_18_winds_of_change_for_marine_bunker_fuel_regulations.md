Winds of Change for Marine Bunker Fuel Regulations

Governments worldwide are working both separately and together to develop limits on sulfur and carbon emissions from industry, improvements in mileage standards for automobiles and better oversight of derivatives trading. ## Winds of Change for Marine Bunker Fuel Regulations
At the moment, the specter of tightening regulation is a fact of life across nearly every niche of the energy industry. Governments worldwide are working both separately and together to develop limits on sulfur and carbon emissions from industry, improvements in mileage standards for automobiles and better oversight of derivatives trading. While all of these regulatory efforts will eventually impact the tanker industry, the effort to reduce sulfur content of marine bunker fuels is of immediate concern for both the bunkering and shipping industries.
The International Maritime Organization (IMO) adopted regulations last October calling for the sulfur content of marine bunker fuel within existing Emissions Control Areas (ECAs) to drop from existing limits of 1.5%S to 0.1%S by 2015. The group also approved a decrease in the maximum permissible sulfur content outside of the designated areas from the 4.5%S today to 3.5%S in 2012, and proposed a 0.5%S cap by 2020.
Particularly significant for US shipowners will be the introduction of a North American ECA as early as August 2012, the area of which is related in the map below.
The proposed area of the ECA includes U.S. and Canadian waters adjacent to the Pacific coast, the Atlantic/Gulf coast and the eight main Hawaiian Islands. The proposed ECA would extend 200 nautical miles from the coastal baseline. France has joined the ECA proposal on behalf of its island territories off the coast of Newfoundland. Mexico is also considering joining the N.A. ECA initiative.
Restrictions on the sulfur content of heavy marine bunker fuel burned in the Baltic Sea as well as in sections of the North Sea are not new. IMO regulations limiting the fuel sulfur content burned within Emission Control Areas (ECAs) were announced earlier this decade. Since May 19, 2006, the sulfur content of heavy bunkers used within defined regions of the Baltic Sea was capped at no more than 1.5%S. About 18 months later - on November 22, 2007 - sections of the North Sea and the English Channel also imposed this level sulfur cap on heavy bunkers burned within its defined area. Local bunker markets have since coped with the evolving trade patterns of this grade of fuel. But come July 2010, the rhythm of the bunker market within the ECA is mandated to dance to the beat imposed by an even lower cap - heavy bunker fuel burned within these ECAs must contain no more than 1.0%S. The N.A. ECA will be subject to this more stringent limit when implemented in 2012.

Demand for low sulfur heavy residual bunkers is rendered inelastic at a given level of traffic by the ECAs' mandates. On the supply side, how much of these bunkers come from local refineries and how much come from areas outside the ECAs ultimately depends on its price. There is no magic way for local refineries to either easily or cheaply reduce bunker sulfur content. Refineries in neighboring regions seem a likely source from where any shortfall of 1% sulfur bunker fuel will be pulled. Finding enough stocks to blend, however, will certainly be more daunting than blending the current sulfur requirement. A restrictive limit on the concentrations of aluminum plus silicon will also crimp refiners' blending options.
The bunker fuel sulfur limit in the ECAs will be further reduced from 1.0%S to 0.1%S starting January 1, 2015. Vessels operating in the designated areas of northwest Europe and North America must burn compliant marine fuel oil subject to the IMO "fuel availability" provision. This step‐function change in fuel quality will require vessels to consume marine distillates in place of low sulfur residual fuel oil. This further tightening of marine fuel sulfur requirements will impact distillate balances in both North America and Europe and test refiners' ability to meet this incremental demand. Regional pricing arbitrages and trade routes will no doubt be impacted.
On a global basis, the change from maximum 4.5%S content and 3.5%S effective January 1, 2012, should not present any insurmountable problems for the Industry since the current average sulfur level supplied is approximately 2.7%S as measured by current IMO methodology.
Longer term, however, the drastic reduction in the marine fuels sulfur cap from 3.5%S to 0.5%S in 2020 (subject to an IMO feasibility study in 2018), will present significant challenges for the Industry. Given current technology, refiners do not have the capability to produce residual fuel oil at this level of sulfur; neither does the refining industry have the economic incentive to add this type of capability. Meeting the paradigm shift in demand and more stringent emissions requirements could evolve via several scenarios. Marine fuel blending to this lower level will no doubt increase and involve both low sulfur residual fuel oil and marine distillates. Commercialization of exhaust gas scrubbers and other abatement technologies could also play an important role.
In summary, the impending marine fuel regulations pose distinct issues for vessel owners and operators, marine fuel suppliers, and refiners. These and other questions will be explored further in the upcoming Poten & Partners publication, "Marine Fuel Regulations 2010‐2025 ‐ What the Future Holds for International Shipping Industry" to be released in November.
