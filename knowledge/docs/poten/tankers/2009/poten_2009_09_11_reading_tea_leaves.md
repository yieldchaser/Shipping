## Reading Tea Leaves
After last year's sudden and dramatic wrecking of the world's economies, the sterling perception of economic theory has become severely tarnished. Despite this fall from grace, some economists have lately been talking of the signs of a nascent recovery. Admittedly, reading the tea leaves to make prognostications like these is not a simple task. While the notion of an economic bottom either being here or being near is growing more prevalent in the media, the questions of whether or not any bottom will hold or if a new, even lower one replace it remain unanswered. Hence, moderate skepticism of statements that a recovery is beginning seems reasonable especially when the outlook comes from a discipline that did not see last year's disaster coming.
Until a sweeping recovery is unquestionably in place, tanker rates seem destined to remain a hostage of the world's meager economic activity. To give perspective of just how steep tanker demand's falloff has been since last year, a year‐to‐year comparison in Japanese crude oil imports is highlighted below.

There is no question that markets endure peaks and troughs from time to time. Historically, oil markets have followed this pattern, too. Anticipating the next uptick in activity is a key ingredient in the success of tanker market participants. But when and where will tanker activity pick up?
At first glance, the gathering consensus that oil demand will rise next year might seem encouraging to tanker owners seeking higher earnings. But as the chart below highlights, the progression of yearly oil demand outlooks issued monthly by the International Energy Agency (IEA) has not been without revisions.

The direction of increased oil flow pulled into consuming regions from oil supplying ones governs how ton‐mile demand growth will perk up.
Differentiating crude oil demand growth from OECD and non‐OECD countries reveals that the latter is anticipated to generate all of 2010's anticipated increase. OECD countries are expected to maintain steady demand between 2009 and 2010. Non‐OECD oil demand, however, is projected to reach 40.1 mbd by 2010 - or 3.4 percent more than 2009. Overall, the world could see demand reach 85.3 mbd in 2010, which represents an increase of 1.4 mbd or 1.7 percent more than 2009's level. There is no doubt that many of these barrels will likely sail East with many of them originating in the Arabian Gulf. That scenario portends an incremental increase in tanker demand for the coming year.
Drilling into the IEA's information reveals that between 2007 and 2009, China's apparent oil demand, (including oil for strategic storage), rose from 7.6 to 8.1 mbd or about 6.6 percent. But this increase, while impressive, comprised less than 40 percent of the total non‐OECD rise of 1.5 mbd over the period. Nearly half of this period's non‐OECD rise - some 0.7 mbd - is attributable to the seemingly relentless increases in Middle East demand. Much of this region's new oil demand probably never saw the inside of a tanker for very long as it was consumed near where it was produced. From 2009 to 2010, the IEA's outlook is that China's and the Middle East's oil demand each will lift by 0.3 mbd.
While some practitioners of the dismal science - more commonly referred to as economists - see the buds of a recovery, caution is still wise in acting on their view. Assessing tanker demand in the cloudy, shifting times surrounding any economic recovery seems bound to yield erratic indications. Careful pinpointing of emerging and perhaps even fading trade routes is essential to understanding how the recovery of ton‐mile demand will progress. Some oil demand stalwarts of last cycle's economic expansion may not have quite the same vigor in the next one, while others might easily show as much or even more demand as in the past. Agile tanker market participants should position themselves to seize asset opportunities when and where they appear to mesh with expectations of ton‐miles demand recovery.
