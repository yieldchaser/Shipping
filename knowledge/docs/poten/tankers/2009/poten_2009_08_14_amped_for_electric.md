## Amped for Electric
Amped for Electric Pulling the plug on conventional autos?
A smiley‐faced electrical socket stood in for the zero on a banner announcing General Motors' expectation of 230 miles per gallon rating for the Chevy Volt, its long‐awaited electric car introduced this week. This visible break from the company's gas‐guzzling past earned enough praise from the media to justify the smile on GM's face, but the enthusiasm with which some promote electric cars as a panacea for petroleum's side‐ effects has raised significant questions.
Assuming GM has done the arithmetic correctly, the important question becomes how this technology is more efficient and more environmentally friendly? GM's 230 mpg (such that the analogy even makes sense), claim is based on their application of Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) draft guidelines for city driving in electric vehicles with a full charge; a limited and specific driving profile. The company warns further that actual gasoline‐free mileage will also be influenced by distance travelled, accessories used, driving style and a variety of other factors.
As seen in the chart below, passenger miles definitely set the pace for vehicle demand, but the 98% growth in the vans, trucks, SUVs, pickup trucks since 1990 suggests the American preference is for heavier duty vehicles.

## Buses
GM has yet to release a highway mileage estimate for the Volt, a number which a GM spokesman was quoted as acknowledging would be "fairly significantly lower" than the headline‐grabbing 230 mpg announced this week. Although GM and the EPA came to an agreement in September about a testing method that would guarantee a mpg rating above 100, the EPA has not tested the Volt and did not endorse GM's expected rating.

## Green Eggs
The environmental advantages electric cars offer over internal combustion engines are far from clear when taking a holistic appraisal of a vehicle's carbon footprint.
GM says that plugging into the electricity grid once a day is the key to high‐end performance and that charging can be done at home overnight when demand and rates are lower. Because the electricity necessary to charge the lithium‐ion battery propelling the Volt is most likely to be produced with coal or other hydrocarbons such as natural gas, the green‐ness of the technology remains questionable. Not to mention the finite lifespan of the battery and vehicle will mean heaps of hazardous waste that could pose environmental threats down the road.
While some impracticalities such as changes to infrastructure will naturally follow if the technology does bring efficiencies, let us not underestimate the start‐up subsidies (tax credits) that will be required for a paradigm shift to take place; especially when it is not clear that we are replacing one technology with a better one.

Aside from the coal factor, the batteries such as those inside the Volt require vanadium, zinc, bromine, nickel, cobalt, manganese and lithium, among other metals. Ensuring supply of these strategic metals sufficient to support a wide‐scale adoption of the electric vehicle would present its own set of diplomatic challenges. Although the materials procurement is at present a small part of the equation, unless the technology is actually efficient from cradle to grave, it is tough to see how these inventions are categorically an improvement.
