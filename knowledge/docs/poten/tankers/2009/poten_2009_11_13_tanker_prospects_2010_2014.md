## A Medium Term Outlook
Tanker Prospects 2010 - 2014 A Medium Term Outlook Any attempt to project the course of the tanker market between now and 2014 must confront its position at the crossroads of so many of the challenges currently facing the worldwide economy. Changing trade and consumption patterns, ever‐tightening environmental regulation and the pace of global economic recovery must all be considered alongside the other fundamentals long assumed to propel crude oil and tanker markets. Tanker Prospects, soon to be published by Poten & Partners for the 13 th consecutive year, includes such analysis and can be a valuable tool in commercial strategy development.
The past year has seen rapidly contracting demand for crude oil and refined products impact spot tanker markets while the credit crunch not only limited sale and purchase activity, but also strained those invested in the looming orderbook. As shown in the chart below, 2009 spot market earnings failed to reach the levels set in recent years and marked the end of one of the strongest earnings periods in the history of the tanker market. There have been encouraging signs regarding oil demand and the wider economy of late, but few would doubt that it has been a very challenging past year for the tanker market and considerable uncertainty remains about its future.

## VLCC Spot Market Earnings
Foremost among the uncertainties currently facing the tanker market is the pace of orderbook deliveries. As in past downturns, the delivery of vessels ordered at the height of the market continues to hold down freight rates still struggling to recover. Shipowners and shipyards are currently negotiating delays and deferments, but the timing of these deliveries is subject to many influences. It remains to be seen how the market will absorb this tonnage.
While delivery of the orderbook will be the key metric over the medium term, the near‐term prospects for the tanker industry remain tied to recovery in the international economy. China's infrastructure spending and Saudi Arabian power generation were cited as the main drivers for the International Energy Agency's (IEA) 220 kbd upward revision of its 2009 oil demand projection released yesterday. IEA also reported evidence of stronger‐than‐expected fuel consumption in the US and in October the Department of Commerce reported that the American economy grew at a 3.5 percent annualized rate in the third quarter of 2009. While modest, the fourth quarter demand growth expected by the IEA would mark the first year‐on‐year quarterly increase since the second quarter of 2008 as well as a milestone on the long road to recovery.
The central role of developing nations in shaping near term expectations for growth should come as no surprise. It has been widely acknowledged that the emerging nations of the East, especially China and India, will be the likely sources for the majority of crude oil and refined product demand growth in the coming years. Less often acknowledged is the fact that expanding oil demand in these markets will translate into increased ton‐mile demand in unpredictable ways. In order to benefit from opportunities in those markets it is necessary to first build a clear understanding of these countries' current role in the petroleum industry and expectations of how those roles transform global tanker trade routes.
