## Dollars and Sense
The slight rally in the crude tanker freight rates has left many wondering if the crude tanker market is actually resilient enough to emerge from the financial crisis relatively unscathed. Over the past two weeks the market has gained momentum, with the VLCCs out of the Arabian Gulf bound for the Far East breaching $40,000 per day, spurring increased bullishness on the part of shipowners and oil companies. Only last month nearly all sectors of the market were bottom feeding, earning charter rates sufficiently below break‐even levels.
Although it is tough to say that a recovery is imminent, the sentiment has certainly improved ‐ even if only temporarily. The challenge now becomes navigating a rate environment that still seems to carry an element of surprise. While there is a tendency to paint all shipping sectors with one brush, it is important to remember a few key characteristics in the tanker market. Trade demand is essentially driven by fundamental economic strength. While other shipping sectors are dependent on slightly more fickle drivers such as discretionary consumer spending or construction demand, energy remains a necessity for survival.
Slight positive movements in a broader economic recovery will yield immediate results in the physical oil markets. For example, the number of Arabian Gulf spot market VLCC liftings has seen a very slight increase month‐on‐month. Additionally, the highly liquid and fairly consolidated spot market complicates and obscures the available tanker supply. ## Beyond Fundamentals
A purely fundamental approach, meaning a comparison of the expected increase in oil demand to the forward supply of tankers, is far from sufficient for determining a view on the future of freight rates and asset prices.
Within the ten‐year historical range the fundamentals suggest that the closer ratio of demand to supply yields greater volatility, but does not necessarily translate into higher average rates. Even in a seemingly over‐tonnaged environment, regional imbalances and the incremental ship out of position can still create a significant ripple effect. ## Heart in the Game
In addition to supply and demand, tankers can be an emotional business; psychology cannot be understated. On the asset side, it is difficult to quantify and foresee the role and impact the instincts of individual actors will play. History is laden with stories of shipowners that have waffled between feast and famine by trusting their gut. Owning tankers is not for the faint of heart and it seems that the key to success is an underlying belief in the market itself ‐ the growth of trade, the participants and the power that the free‐market can and will react instantly to information.
Remember the bottom of a market is a moving target. When enough sentiment builds that the market is approaching its bottom it is already too late. The real skill is getting in near enough to the bottom to capture the bounce.
