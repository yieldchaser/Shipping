## Another production increase suggests a change in strategy
About one month ago, we wrote our tanker opinion about the surprise decision of OPEC+ to increase production well above the monthly increase previously indicated (" OPEC Springs A Surprise " ). On April 3, the eight OPEC+ countries that committed to voluntary production adjustments in 2023, decided to bring back 411,000 b/d in May, three times the April 2025 increase of 137,000 b/d. Most analysts expected this to be a one-off production boost. However, one month later, on May 3, the OPEC-8 group (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, UAE, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman) decided to repeat the same 411,000 b/d increase for June. While this OPEC group can change their mind any time, it appears that these accelerated increases are the new normal. At this rate, the OPEC-8 group will return the 2.2 Mb/d of voluntary cuts to the market by November 2025, rather than September 2026, as per the original schedule. In this week's tanker opinion, we will discuss the potential implications for the tanker market if OPEC-8 stays the course.
There is little historical precedent for the current actions of OPEC. Obviously, OPEC has restricted and boosted production and exports many times since the organization was founded in 1960. However, when OPEC accelerated the unwinding of production cuts, it was usually during periods of a tightening market, i.e oil demand was higher than expected or there were unanticipated supply disruptions. A recent analysis from Morgan Stanley shows that the only historical period that shows any similarity to what is happening at the moment is what happened during late 1997, when OPEC decided at their meeting in Jakarta to increase production. Unfortunately, this coincided with the start of the Asian financial crisis. The combination of increased supply and the Asian demand slump resulted in a sharp decline in oil prices. Something similar is happening now, albeit not on the same scale. There are a number of possible reasons why the OPEC-8 group is boosting its production. Maybe certain members (Saudi Arabia) want to signal to the countries that produce beyond their quota that they need to fall in line. Maybe OPEC wants to put pressure on Non-OPEC producers (U.S. shale in particular) to restrict investment and maybe regain some market share. Maybe they want to please President Trump, who has advocated for higher OPEC production to bring oil prices down. We can only speculate.
What are the implications for the tanker market of the accelerated production increases by OPEC-8? Are they also exporting more? If so, where will the oil be going if there is only limited demand growth? Which segments will be impacted most?
One of the key challenges is to determine if these increases that OPEC has announced will translate into more barrels on the water or if these increases are being used to satisfy the so-called "compensation plans". Except for Algeria, all the other member in the OPEC-8 group exceeded their production targets during the period between January 2024 and March 2025. On April 16, these countries submitted an updated compensation plan. Iraq, Kazakhstan and (to a lesser extend) Russia overproduced the most. Kazakhstan has shown no real intention to cut back (they boosted exports this year), while Iraq has promised much, but delivered little. The OPEC quotas and compensation plans have become increasingly detailed and complex, but appear to have limited practical impact.
Since the production increases have been announced, we have seen some more exports from the OPEC-8 group, but a detailed look at the seaborne exports of the individual countries does not show a clear pattern (see Chart 1) yet. Crude oil exports in April did average 230,000 b/d higher than those in March, but it is probably too early to call May, although the first week does show an increase. There are also significant month-to-month variations. However, continued increases in OPEC-8 production will show up in the seaborne export numbers and create additional employment opportunities for tankers, in particular VLCCs, which would be the main beneficiaries. Russia and Kazakhstan already produce at or close to capacity, so the increase will likely come from the countries with spare capacity, such as Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iraq and Kuwait.
