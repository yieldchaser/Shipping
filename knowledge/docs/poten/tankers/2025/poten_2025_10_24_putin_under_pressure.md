## What will additional sanctions do to tanker rates?
Last week, our Tanker Opinion discussed the potential implications of a growing oversupply of crude oil as most pundits expect that a combination of non-OPEC production growth and OPEC+ quota increases will overwhelm anemic global demand growth in 2026. Now, we have to potentially worry about the opposite. The Trump administration, citing frustration with "Russia's lack of serious commitment to a peace process to end the war in Ukraine", announced on Wednesday sanctions on Russia's largest oil producers, including Rosneft and Lukoil. Combined, these companies represent between 50 and 60% of total Russian crude oil production. This is the first time that the current U.S. administration has directly targeted Russia. One day later, the EU rolled out its 19 th sanctions package directed towards Russian energy exports, including adding 117 ships to the list of sanctioned vessels. It is noteworthy that the EU also took aim at two Chinese independent oil refineries and a Chinese trading firm. These new sanctions, in particular the U.S. blacklisting of Rosneft and Lukoil send shockwaves through the oil and tanker market, which were already in turmoil because of previous OFAC sanctions on Chinese terminals and the tit-for-tat port fees that the U.S. and China rolled out last week.
It is important to note that it is not entirely clear if, when and how these new U.S. sanctions will be implemented and enforced. However, from the initial reactions, it seems that they are already having an impact. Combined, China and India buy approximately 90% of Russian crude oil, with most of the remainder going to Turkey.
A report from Reuters, citing unnamed sources, suggested that state-owned refiners in China will stop seaborne imports of Russian crude oil, at least temporarily. Chinese pipeline imports of Russian crude oil will probably not be affected. These flows, which are estimated to be around 900,000 b/d, are harder to track. It is unknown if Chinese independent refiners will stop buying Russian crude or whether they will look for ways to circumvent the sanctions. Some of these so-called "teapot" refiners and the terminals handling their crude oil imports have already been targeted by the U.S. Treasury Department for their imports of sanctioned Iranian oil. The situation in the tanker market has been further complicated by the introduction of the Chinese port fees, which target U.S. controlled tonnage. This has (temporarily) restricted the availability of suitable tonnage and made the market more inefficient. Once factor that could mitigate the immediate impact of these new sanctions is that Chinese refiners have spent the better part of this year building up inventories. They can afford a wait and see attitude.
The situation for the refiners in India is different. They have responded in a similar way, taking their cues from the Indian government. Reliance Industries, India's largest refiner said that it would "recalibrate" its imports in line with government guidelines. The situation for Reliance is particularly tricky, because they have a long-term crude supply contract with Rosneft for 500,000 b/d. Indian refiners already started to reduce imports in recent months under pressure of the U.S. government. President Trump imposed additional tariffs on Indian products because of their purchases of Russian crude oil. In addition to the pressure from the U.S., India was also singled out by the E.U. for their - indirect - support of Russia. They sanctioned Nayara Energy, one of India's largest oil refiners, which is partly owned by Rosneft. They also announced that the E.U. would not import any refined products from India that are derived from Russian crude.
The actions from the E.U. and the U.S. have boosted oil prices and reopened the arbitrage from the Atlantic to the Pacific. This will drive additional ton-mile demand, which will push freight rates higher, in particular for VLCCs. An analysis from Vortexa showed that sanctioned oil from Russia and Iran are the main reason for the record levels of oil on the water. The additional sanctions will only make this situation "worse", with the dark fleet increasingly becoming a source of floating storage. This is music to the ears of the mainstream tanker owners, who are already benefitting from increasing market inefficiencies.
