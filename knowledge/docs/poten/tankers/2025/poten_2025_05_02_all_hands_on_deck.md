## Building the U.S. into a Maritime Powerhouse.
The domestic maritime industry never received so much interest in the United States as in the last few months. It seems that a new piece of legislation is being introduced every week. Last week, in our Opinion piece about the USTR (" Make America Build Ships Again? "), we referenced a new piece of bipartisan (i.e. supported by both Republicans and Democrats) legislation that was expected to be reintroduced in Congress this week. This did indeed happen on Wednesday, April 30 th . The act is called the Shipbuilding and Harbor Infrastructure for Prosperity and Security (SHIPS) for America Act. The sponsors of the bill want to enhance U.S. competitiveness and substantially increase investments in the maritime workforce. One of their aims is to expand the U.S. built, U.S. Flag fleet by 250 vessels in 10 years. This is a very ambitious goal. At the moment, only about 80 oceangoing ships fly the American flag in international commerce. In this Tanker Opinion, we will discuss some of the individual components of the proposed legislation, how it interacts with existing laws, such as the Jones Act and previously announced initiatives, such as the USTR measures proposed on April 17 and President Trump's Executive Order of April 9 ("Restoring America's Maritime Dominance").
The SHIPS Act (the Act) has different components, and its goals closely mirror President Trump's Executive Order. Please note that the Act contains a lot of different individual provisions, too many to list here. We will describe some of those that we think are the most relevant or impactful for the oil tanker market, but this is not meant to be comprehensive or complete.
To coordinate all the efforts related to U.S. maritime policy, the Act proposes to establish a new Office of the Maritime Security Advisor within the White House to advise the president on the National Maritime Strategy.
The Act also set up a Maritime Security Trust Fund (similar to the Highway Trust Fund and the Aviation Trust Fund), to create a dedicated source of funding for "critical maritime security programs". This is one of the key components of the Act, since it re-establishes the U.S Tonnage Tax regime (Table 1) and, in addition, creates penalties for Chinese-owned vessels and other vessel owners who conduct significant amounts of business with the CCP-owned China State Shipbuilding Corporation (CSSC) or other shipyards owned and controlled by a "foreign entity of concern". Foreign countries of concern are defined as China, Russia, Iran and North Korea. These penalties will be in addition to the USTR fees discussed last week. These penalties are as follows. (1) The Act will impose a fee of US$ 5.00 per net ton of capacity for any vessel owned by a foreign country of concern or a vessel belonging to a fleet of vessels where 50% or more of new vessels ordered will be built in China, or any other shipyard owned and controlled by a foreign entity of concern. (2) If 25-50% of the fleet will be built by such shipyard, the fee will be a reduced US$3.50 per net ton. (3) A US$1.25 per net ton is imposed on existing vessels which are part of a fleet where 25% or more of vessels were constructed or repaired by shipyards owned and controlled by a foreign entity of concern. Exceptions are provided for U.S.-flag and other neighboring shipping companies. Table 2 above summarizes these fees for the most common tanker segments.
In addition to the tonnage taxes and penalty fees that will accrue into the Maritime Security Trust Fund, there are several other initiatives included in the Act. It proposes that MARAD starts a Strategic Commercial Fleet Program with the previously mentioned goal of establishing a fleet of 250 U.S.-flagged vessels in international commerce. While eligible participants are allowed to initially reflag foreign built vessels into this program (to increase the fleet more rapidly), this option will no longer be available after fiscal year 2030. The Act recognizes that there is an imminent need for tankers, and that MARAD should prioritize these vessels. The Act also tightens the various cargo preference rules; including a new rule requiring that within 15 years, 10% of all cargo imported into the U.S. from China be imported on U.S.-flagged vessels. Finally, to energize American shipbuilding there is a requirement that by 2035 10% of total seaborne crude oil exports is transported by vessels built in the U.S.
