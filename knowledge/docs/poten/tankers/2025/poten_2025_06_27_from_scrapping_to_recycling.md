## Ship recycling enters a new era
Yesterday, on 26 June 2025, the Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships (HKC) officially went into effect. The aim of this piece of regulation from the International Maritime Organization (IMO) is to improve the safety and environmental performance of ship recycling yards worldwide. The HKC has been a long time coming. The convention was approved by the IMO back in 2009, but it required a lengthy ratification process by member states before it met the conditions to enter into force. So, what happens next? What will change in the international ship recycling industry?
The HKC applies to all ships over 500 Gross Tons. Under the new regulations, ships destined for recycling must carry an Inventory of Hazardous Materials (IHM) onboard. Shipowners are required to ensure that every vessel has an International Ready to Recycle Certificate (IRRC), issued by the vessel's flag state, while authorized recycling facilities must provide a specific Ship Recycling Plan (SRP) for each vessel. Governments are responsible for ensuring that facilities within their jurisdiction comply with the Convention's regulations.
Since these new regulations have been in the works for 16 years, shipowners and recycling yards have had time to prepare and become compliant. Many yards in countries such as Bangladesh, India and Turkey are compliant and have accredited facilities, while others (like in Pakistan) are working towards accreditation. There are some potential complications. At this point only 24 flag states representing approximately 60% of the world's merchant fleet by gross tonnage has ratified the HKC. Flag states that have not (yet) signed up, such as China, the United States, Greece and Singapore, may not follow the same procedures. There is also the issue of alternative and competing regulations. There are two other related international efforts with respect to ship recycling, the EU Ship Recycling Regulation (EU SRR) and the Basel Convention (BC) in 1998. The EU passed the EU SRR in 2013, and it is basically the HKC plus additional requirements for vessels from EU member states. The Basel Convention was designed to control the movement of hazardous waste generally between countries and, in particular, from more developed countries to less developed countries. Vessels are not considered hazardous waste by the Basel Convention, but vessels being transported for recycling are. In the context of this opinion, it goes too far to explain these regulations in detail. However, there are differences, which means that end-of-life ships that are in full compliance with HKC, risk being detained in ports for violating the BC. It is important that these rules are aligned and streamlined so the HKC can reach its full potential.
Clear and consistent rules and regulations are important since the global merchant fleet is growing. The tanker fleet in particular is also ageing rapidly. Limited newbuilding deliveries in recent years, in combination with a lack of recycling. Since the sanctions on Russia were enacted, older tankers (>15 years old) are sold into the dark fleet for prices well in excess of their demolition values. As can be seen in chart 1 above, tanker recycling virtually stopped after 2022.
The key question is: What will happen if, at some point in the future, sanctions on Russia and/or Iran and Venezuela are lifted? The general expectation is that most tankers from the dark fleet (especially the sanctioned ones) will no longer be tradeable and be sailing to the beaches of India, Bangladesh and Pakistan. However, if history is any guide, it all depends on the rate environment. If rates are healthy and the vessels are in class, chances are the owner will keep them trading. If there is a significant market correction, that will change. There could be a mass exodus. Will there be enough demolition capacity at accredited yards? Probably. Anecdotal evidence suggests that there is a lot of idled recycling capacity that can be brought back online within a matter of months. Recycling prices will probably go down when vessel supply picks up, which will make recycling more profitable. India, the second-largest steel producer in the world behind China, uses only 20% of ferrous scrap in its crude steel production relative to countries in Europe (60%), the U.S. (70%) and Turkey, so there is significant potential for additional demand.
