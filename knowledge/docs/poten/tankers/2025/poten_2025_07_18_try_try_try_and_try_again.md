## Will the latest EU sanctions package make the difference?
Today, on Friday, July 18, the EU adopted its 18 th sanctions package against Russia since the invasion of Ukraine. The EU issued a press release this morning describing the new measures. They are targeting Russia's energy, banking and military sectors. Most of the measures are building on previous sanctions, but there are new elements that could make a difference if properly implemented and enforced. In this opinion we will take a closer look at these measures.
On the energy side there are three key elements that are worth looking at in more detail. First, the EU announced that they will lower the price cap for crude oil from $60/barrel to $47.60/barrel, the first change to the price cap since it was implemented by the EU, the US, the other G7 countries and Australia on December 5, 2022. In addition to lowering the price cap, the EU also announced an "automatic and dynamic mechanism to modify the oil price cap" to ensure its effectiveness. The U.K. announced that it will also reduce the price cap to match the EU's level, but it did not commit to the dynamic pricing model. Other countries have not agreed to the revised price cap. Previously, the U.S. objected to a reduction. The EU statement did not mention changes to the price cap on oil products. The impact of the price cap in Russian export volumes has been limited since Russia has created a large "dark fleet" that can service its exports. This has significantly reduced their need to use vessels mainstream owners.
The EU also added 105 tankers to their list of sanctioned vessels, bringing the total number of listed vessels to 444. These are part of the dark fleet that Russia uses to circumvent the oil price cap mechanism. Unfortunately, the EU sanctions targeting the dark fleet have had limited impact on their usage. We don't expect the additions to the EU sanctioned fleet to change that.
The sanctions measure that could potentially be the most impactful is the EU import ban on refined petroleum products made from Russian crude oil. The EU refers to this as "preventing Russia's crude oil from reaching the EU market through the back door." This measure seems to target primarily the Indian refining industry. Since the start of the war, India has ramped up crude oil imports from Russia (see Chart 1), attracted by the favorable prices. Indian refiners imported some 1.5 Mb/d of crude oil from Russia, making it the second largest importer of Russian crude after China. Unlike China, however, India also exports large volumes of refined product to the European Union (Chart 2). Ironically, these exports have increased since the start of the war in Ukraine. India's independent refiners are the most active on the export market, since the state-owned refineries typically focus on supplying the domestic market. The EU blacklisted the Nayara Energy refinery in Vadinar, Gujarat. This Indian refinery is 49% owned by Russia's state-run oil company Rosneft. The EU's move effectively blocks Nayara from exporting any refined products to European countries. The immediate impact on the other Indian refiners is not clear. Until the full text of the new sanctions regime is published, it is unknown exactly how the EU will measure and enforce this new rule. Does it apply to any refiner who use any Russian oil or does it only apply to refiners that predominantly or exclusive process Russian barrels? How do you measure this? Ironically, the lowering of the price cap by the EU could make Russian crude even more attractive (i.e. cheaper) for Indian refiners. Not surprisingly, the Indian government took a dim view of the EU measures. India's Ministry of External Affairs issued a statement stressing that India does not recognize unilateral sanctions and urged the EU to avoid "double standards" - especially in energy trade.
It is too early to draw any major conclusion from these new measures. The expansion to the product trades makes it potentially more powerful, but enforcement could be challenging. Ultimately, the U.S. position will determine the outcome. The Trump administration has threatened to tighten the screws on Moscow with 100% tariffs and secondary sanctions on buyers of Russian crude if Mr. Putin refuses to commit to a ceasefire. Under these circumstances the EU measures could be effective. If not, their impact will likely be limited.
