## Five New Trains Boost Global Liquefaction Capacity
Suppliers in Oman, Nigeria, Trinidad and Australia have commissioned no less than five trains totaling an astounding 20.6 MMt/y of new liquefaction capacity in the past several months. Australia's Darwin LNG exported its first cargo just a few days ago while the fifth train at Nigeria LNG was handed over for startup last month and has yet to ship out a single cargo. Others including Atlantic LNG's fourth train and the new Nigerian facilities have feedgas issues to contend with and may take longer than expected to ramp up to plateau. A case in point is Nigeria LNG, who has factored in a conservative 12-month build up profile for its two-train NLNGPlus project. Despite these drawbacks, the amount of new capacity associated with these new production units is still very impressive (see table below). Several of the facilities, including NLNGPlus and Atlantic LNG's train 4, had been scheduled for startup last year and their availability would have helped ease this winter's tight spot market. But operations were delayed by a variety of glitches ranging from the usual mechanical problems associated with commissioning to difficulties in synchronizing development of the upstream gas reserves nominated to supply the new trains.

## Projects
Both NLNGPlus trains were supposed to begin export operations in 2005, train 4 in June or July followed by train 5 in November. This timetable slipped when mandated supply projects fell behind schedule, and suffered another setback in August when a pipeline leak and subsequent fire shut down one of the producer's main feedgas transmission systems (see LNGWM, Sep '05). Train 4 shipped out its first commercial cargo on January 7 followed by the second two weeks later. The first cargo went into Louisiana's Lake Charles terminal for BG's account while Total received the second shipment in Spain. Once the fifth train is up and running, NLNG will have a production capacity of 17 MMt/y. While it is unclear how quickly all five trains will be producing at full capacity, operations at the Nigerian plant appear to be running smoothly and the venture could even have some excess availability towards the end of the first quarter or shortly thereafter (see related story above).

## NL
Train 4 at Atlantic LNG faced similar feedgas delays. In the middle of last year, operators were planning to start producing LNG to the tank in September 2005, implying first exports one month later in October (see LNGWM, May '05). But this was postponed several months due to ongoing upstream constraints coupled with a minor incident involving mercury contamination in the refrigerants. Even when the plant was ready to start producing, contractual issues delayed loading operations after the shareholders balked at lifting cargoes while the necessary agreements with the government of Trinidad & Tobago were still outstanding. "Train 4 was ready to produce in early November. By December it had been through the loading and commissioning exercises. But the offtakers refused to lift cargoes until the government agreements were in place," comments one insider, who points to this contractual impasse rather than the train's gas supply problems as the biggest impediment to early startup. The first cargo from train 4 finally left Trinidad on January 8 and the upstream situation has improved to the point where operators now expect to get something close to the full feedgas allotment of 800 MMcf/d by the end of February or early March. Train 4 has added another 5.2 MMt/y of production capacity, boosting total design capacity at the Point Fortin plant site to 15.1 MMt/y.
In contrast to these other projects, Oman's Qalhat LNG exported its first cargo more or less on schedule on December 12 while Darwin LNG shipped out its inaugural cargo a full month ahead of plan on February 14 (see related article below). The venture had been hoping to begin loading operations even earlier and Tokyo Electric Power Co moved the Pacific Notus into position off Australia's Wickham Point in January to lift the first cargo. When the shipment failed to materialize after five days, the Japanese company dispatched the vessel to other business. It was eventually replaced by the Kotowaka Maru , which set sail for Japan with the venture's inaugural cargo earlier this month. Another tanker is expected to arrive within a couple of weeks to lift a second cargo for Tokyo Gas, the Australian project's other major customer. Once it is up and running, Darwin LNG expects to load a cargo every ten days. This could enable the venture to sell several pre-contract cargoes to the two Japanese utilities prior to March 31 when its term contract commitments are due to begin.

## Projects
Last year was a particularly bad one for technical glitches and industry players are hoping the setbacks won't be repeated in 2006. These were particularly severe at Egypt's Damietta plant, which tripped off-line repeatedly due to electrical problems that have now been resolved. But it was by no means the only producer with technical issues: Australia's North West Shelf experienced compressor shaft vibrations at train 4 in August and had to shut the facility down for 35 days while Atlantic LNG was dogged by recurrent mechanical troubles including freezing problems at its existing trains. And the difficulties extended to Qatar, where RasGas II labored to resolve startup issues with train 4 and Qatargas identified pinhole leaks in the scrub column on train 1. Indeed, the problems at Qatargas may entail the replacement of scrub columns on all three of its trains during scheduled maintenance later this year. On the shipping side, the problems with some vessels equipped with GTT membrane tanks have been a matter of concern. "From a performance perspective the industry is showing strain across the board," comments one company official. Others argue that the setbacks reflect the construction sector's seriously overstretched resources and the introduction of new companies into the business. Only time will tell if the problems experienced in 2005 are a one-off or a harbinger of more to come.
