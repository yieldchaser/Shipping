## Greeks and Norwegians Talk Ships
The Twelfth Annual Shipping Conference of the Hellenic and the Norwegian-American Chambers of Commerce was held in New York on February 7 th . This year's theme was "The Image of Shipping: Perception and Reality." As always, the event draws together one of the best cross-sections of industry leaders. This year's conference was no different. Image of an Industry The first session was on the "Image of Shipping" moderated by Michael Grey of Lloyd's List. As has been true for years, Mr. Grey pointed out that there is always room for improvement on the public image of shipping. The popular perception as portrayed by the media is that of vessels running aground or spilling oil. The reality of shipping's excellent record with regard to performance and safety and its role as the lifeblood of world commerce is generally unrecognized. Mr. Grey suggested a number of ways to improve the popular perception of shipping including stressing the industry's benefits to society, showcasing role models, and confronting issues that contribute to giving a bad impression. Hamish Norton of Bear Sterns talked about Wall Street's perception of shipping as an industry known for glamour and quick fortunes, but also one that has experienced low historical returns and is subject to severe revenue cycles. Wall Street investors have little understanding of customary practices in shipping; for instance relying on flags of convenience and operating in a way that reduces tax liabilities. This has created an aura of an industry with a low sense of responsibility. Another practice that turns investors away from shipping is the separation of operations and ownership. Investors instinctively distrust managements contracting ship operations to third parties. Investors simply don't believe that the traditional structure of shipping works for the long term.
The cure for this is for more shipping companies to go public. Investors make or lose money in deeply cyclical industries, but most cyclical industries are owned by the public. Public companies have management structures and behavior that reflect generally accepted standards, especially those reflecting potential conflicts between management and shareholder interests. Compliance with regulations in terms of audited financial reports, transparency of operations via annual and quarterly reports, and oversight by a board of directors have made shipping companies that have gone public in recent years an attractive alternative for investors, particularly those shipping companies with assets in the multi-billion dollar range. Paul Slater of First International Corporation talked about the formation of the Maritime Industry Foundation. The aim of the Foundation, founded in 2005, is to "enrich human knowledge and understanding of the vital roles of maritime transport and commercial shipping."

## Jones Act Resurrection
Stephen Van Dyck , Chairman of Intertanko, moderated the portion of the conference on the Jones Act fleet. The featured speaker was Morten Arntzen , President and CEO of Overseas Shipholding Group. In Mr. Arntzen's view, Jones Act has a long life ahead of it with 60% of existing vessels expected to be phased out by 2015. He believed that the volume of cargo trading between U.S. ports will remain more or less predictable. Thus there are opportunities for Jones Act shipping companies with a professional approach and high quality operation to provide tonnage to charterers. In this regard, OSG has been a leader by bareboating from Aker Philadelphia Shipyard ten 46,000 dwt product carrier newbuildings. Five of these are under bareboat charters for seven years and five under bareboat charters for five years with options for two more vessels. Two vessels are already fixed to Shell and Mr. Arntzen took the opportunity at the conference to announce that another two have been fixed to BP. The "gamble" in this ten-ship, option 2, $1 billion order appears to be paying off.

## Forward Freight Agreements
The last part of the conference was on the FFA market and whether the market was informative, liquid, and transparent. A key speaker was Per Heilmann of Heidenreich Marine. He advised that the components of the Baltic Index, determined by a panel of 41 members, accurately reflect the actual shipping market. The Baltic Index serves as a fairly reliable barometer of future market movements. Owners, who are natural sellers, make up 45% of the counterparties, yet account for only 15% of the traded volumes. Trading companies and financial institutions make up 40% of the counterparties, but trade 70% of the volume. Others spoke about the new clearing ventures, FFA trading strategies, and an increase in FFA participants despite a decline in overall trading volume in 2005 from that of 2004.
