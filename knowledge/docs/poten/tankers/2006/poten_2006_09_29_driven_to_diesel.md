## Driven to Diesel?
In a recent interview with the Wall Street Journal, President Bush reiterated his position on decreasing the United States' reliance on foreign oil and changing driving habits to curb gasoline consumption. Homegrown ethanol technology was offered as a solution. He remarked, "It's interesting, this [energy] diversification has required federal involvement-federal involvement through tax credits for ethanol, as well as research dollars." In a market economy, price should be the ultimate determinant of widespread interest in an alternative fuel source in the United States. The market has been clear: today's gasoline prices simply are not high enough to be a catalyst toward an appreciable switch to ethanol or another feasible substitute, like diesel. Perhaps the need for federally funded research is not as surprising after all.

## Diesel more valuable
The graph on the previous page shows the price spread between the spot US Gulf Coast gasoline and No. 2 low-sulfur diesel. When the line is above the x-axis it indicates that gasoline is more expensive and thus more valuable to refiners than diesel. When the line is below the axis, diesel is more expensive. What is interesting is that in the last two years, diesel has been more valuable to refiners even during peak driving season. If this trend continues, the supposed savings to consumers for switching to diesel vehicles may not be realized. Picking A Pump Automakers have been publicizing the advantages of driving diesel cars in the United States, and have particularly focused on fuel economy. While diesel engines generally get better mileage, the price of diesel relative to gasoline may jeopardize any savings.

## Changing the Mix
The largest hurdle for the adoption of diesel or other alternatives in the United States is the secure and ample supply of these alternative products. Refineries in the US are currently geared to maximize production of gasoline at the expense of heavier products. As the production of gasoline cuts into the production of diesel, simultaneously increasing domestic diesel production while maintaining domestic gasoline production is not feasible. For now, any major changes to the gasoline-fed fleet unlikely in the near to medium term. Current market conditions today are such that Europe consumes essentially all the diesel it produces and imports incremental supply from abroad. For the most part, the world refining system is at capacity for high quality diesel until new refining capacity comes online. If diesel demand increases in the United States, expect competition for high quality diesel to arise with traditional consumers such as Europe effectively pushing diesel prices up and further incentivizing gasoline consumption.
