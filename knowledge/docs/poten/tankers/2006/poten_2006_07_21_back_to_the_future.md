## Back to the Future
In the past six months, crude oil prices have fluctuated between $50 and $80 per barrel. This price volatility serves as a demonstration of the impact geopolitical tensions can have on oil prices. At present, demand for oil is high: thus demand for tonnage is high and VLCC rates in the past week have been on the rise. With the Israeli-Lebanese conflict quickly escalating, the question today is whether we are again on the brink of a Middle East crisis, and what the implications might be for the tanker market.

## Iran-Iraq War
The chart on the previous page shows historical Worldscale rates for VLCC voyages from the Arabian Gulf to the United States. Since Iraq's invasion of Kuwait and Operation Desert Storm that followed, in the early 1990s, rate volatility has remained high. With a tighter supply-demand picture in global oil markets, driven primarily by India, China and the United States, the impact of global conflicts on oil prices and rates has become even more apparent over the last several years. Poised for a Collapse? In past conflicts, escalation has led oil companies to bolster their crude inventories to counteract supply impasses. Tankers were hired for storage and oil hoarded for anticipated supply interruptions. Oil prices increased based on actual physical tightness and speculation of inconsistent future supplies. When conflicts subsided this surplus oil was released from storage tankers and into the global market. Excess crude in the market negatively impacted oil prices and increased tanker tonnage availability creating a decline in rates. The difference in today's market is that demand for crude is much higher than it has been in the past.

## Iraq War
Operation Desert Storm The graph above shows the inflation-adjusted price (based on the CPI) for Arab Light/Brent crude over the past 35 years. The spike in prices resulted from the compounded effects of the Iran hostage situation and the Iran-Iraq war. By 1980, these events interrupted reduce worldwide crude production by four million barrels per day. Charterers took on tonnage as a means of storage and filled up shore-side facilities. In addition, high demand countries like the U.S. undertook projects and legislation reform designed to curb excessive dependence on oil. Conservation measures took hold as consumption began to decline in consuming nations putting downward pressure on tanker rates. This decline was, however, not as significant as the decline in oil prices from $85 per barrel to $25 that would occur over the next 5 years. Could we be facing the same situation? Historically, the natural progression of strong tanker markets, which resulted partly from crude supply constraints, has been towards a decline in rates. Going forward in today's environment there are three potential scenarios: a conflict is resolved peacefully, a conflict is resolved violently, or there is a decline in crude oil demand. In the short term, if a conflict is resolved peacefully with no major supply interruption, rates and oil prices should most likely stabilize. If conflicts are not resolved and supply disruptions occur, rates and oil prices will remain relatively strong until conflicts are resolved. If demand declines, expect rates and prices to decline more quickly.
For now, we are certain that the demand side of the equation is not set to change in the near term and the escalating conflict in the Middle East in recent weeks has added increased fears of supply interruption. Under these conditions, expect tanker demand to remain high, along with rates for the balance of the year.
