## Will pipelines cloud the outlook for the tanker market?
A number of years ago, there was much discussion about new pipeline construction and expansion projects and their potential impact on tanker demand. Recently it has become quiet, in particular with respect to pipelines outside of North America. What is happening to some of these high profile projects? The Burma to China pipeline appears to be completed or near completion. Earlier this week several news outlets reported that the 2009 built, 297,000 dwt VLCC Xin Run Yang discharged a cargo of crude at the Maday terminal in Myanmar for the new Myanmar to China pipeline. This pipeline provides China with an alternative (and shorter) oil import route which bypasses the congested Strait of Malacca. The pipeline, with a capacity of 440,000 barrels per day (b/d) is meant to supply a new refinery under construction in Anning, west of Kunming in South-Western China. However, the construction of the 200,000 b/d refinery is delayed and recent reports suggest that it may not be operational until 2016. The pipeline will probably not be utilized until then. Another significant pipeline project was the expansion of the Eastern Siberia Pacific Ocean (ESPO) pipeline, which runs from the oil fields in Eastern Siberia to the East Coast of Russia (Kozmino) and Daqing in China. This pipeline currently has a capacity of 600,000 b/d. Based on rapidly growing demand in China, Transneft, the Russian pipeline company, was planning to increase the capacity of ESPO to a least 1 million b/d. However, although reportedly approved, construction on this expansion has not yet started and the future of this project appears uncertain.
In the Middle East, discussions have been ongoing about an ambitious 1,000 mile crude oil and gas pipeline from Basra in Iraq to Aqaba, a Jordanian port city in the Red Sea. The planned capacity of this pipeline is one million b/d. Geopolitical unrest, in combination with current low oil prices has cast serious doubts on the viability of this US$18 billion project. Most of the global oil pipeline activity is currently centred in North America where oil and transportation companies have been scrambling to develop infrastructure projects to bring growing shale oil, oil sands and conventional crude to markets and replace more expensive rail transportation. Several of these projects could have a positive impact on the international tanker markets as they would facilitate exports of Canadian crude. These projects include the 525,000 b/d Northern Gateway pipeline which would bring crude to Kitimat, a planned VLCC port on the West Coast of Canada, and the 1.1 million b/d Energy East project which would transport crude to Canada's east coast. However, none of the major pipelines In North America have been approved yet and further delays are likely as all of these projects face significant domestic opposition. While there are other pipelines on the drawing board, most of these are just in the planning stage. Therefore, it is unlikely that new pipelines will have a significant impact on the tanker market in the short to medium term.
