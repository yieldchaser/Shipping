## South of the Border Swap
The Potential Impact of a US - Mexican Oil Exchange As geographical neighbors, the United States and Mexico have a long and mutually beneficial energy relationship. For many years, Mexico has been one of the top suppliers of crude oil to the US, which has reciprocated by sending significant volumes of refined products to Mexico. Mexico is also the largest net importer of US natural gas. Both countries are now exploring ways to further increase their ties. PEMEX, Mexico's state oil company, has applied for a swap transaction that would involve importing heavy Mexican oil into the US in exchange for equivalent volumes of light US crude oil. What is the background of this proposal and how would it impact the crude oil and petroleum shipping markets in the Caribbean and beyond if it was approved? Why have such exchanges not been discussed or even implemented previously? Simply put, they were not allowed. Until the recent deregulation of their oil industry, Mexico prohibited crude oil imports, while US laws (still) prevent the exports of crude. Also, and maybe even more importantly, there was no commercial rationale for an exchange, given the fact that the US was previously a large net importer of crude oil with limited or no crude available for export. Now, the situation is entirely different. The shale oil boom has created a surplus of light sweet crude oil in the US, which cannot be exported. The exception, of course, is Canada (exempt from the export ban since 1986) and exports to this country have soared to 375,000 b/d recently. In Mexico, crude oil production and exports have been declining in recent years, forcing the country to import increasing volumes of refined petroleum products, in particular from the United States. Mexican refiners are producing a surplus of low-value high-sulphur fuel oil, which is increasingly difficult to sell. Domestic demand for fuel oil is declining as power plants are switching to gas and the international market for high-sulphur fuel oil is shrinking. Running more light sweet crude oil from the US would allow Mexican refiners to increase the yield of gasoline and middle distillates and reduce the output of fuel oil. If we assume that the proposal for a 100,000 b/d crude oil swap will be approved, what will be the impact on the tanker markets? Since there are no crude oil pipelines that run between the US and Mexico, the crude oil (most likely Eagle Ford Shale) will have to be shipped by sea, probably on Aframaxes from Corpus Christi and other US Gulf Coast ports to be discharged at Pajaritos and/or Dos Bocas. A volume of 100,000 b/d is equivalent to approximately one Aframax lifting per week. On a round-trip basis, Including loading and discharging this may employ 1-2 vessels in the region. The impact on the product tanker market is more difficult to predict, as it depends partly on how much additional gasoline the lighter crude oil slate will yield and how much less fuel oil. Since most of the Mexican gasoline and diesel come from the US (i.e. short-haul), the impact of this change on the product tanker market is likely limited. However, the excess fuel oil is increasingly being exported to long-haul destinations in Asia, so the negative impact of these reduced volumes on the dirty tanker market may be more significant.
