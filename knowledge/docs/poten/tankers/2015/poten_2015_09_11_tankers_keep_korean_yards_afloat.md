## Tanker orders help recoup some of the offshore losses
So far in 2015, Korea's largest shipyards have mostly made the headlines not because of their shipbuilding prowess, but because of their disappointing financial results. In the second quarter of this year, South Korea's Big Three, Hyundai Heavy Industries Co., Daewoo Shipbuilding & Marine Engineering Co. and Samsung Heavy Industries, racked up a combined operating loss of 4.75 trillion won (~US$4 billion). This was the worst quarter in the history of the Korean shipbuilding industry. These disappointing results came mostly from losses in the building of offshore structures. At the same time, the Korean won strengthened against the Japanese Yen and the Chinese Yuan, further raising the pressure on South Korean builders. However, amidst all this carnage, resurging tanker orders have provided a lifeline.
South Korean companies emerged on the global shipbuilding scene in the 1970's, following in the footsteps of Japan. After World War II, Japan emerged as a major force in this sector. From the late 1960's through the early 1980's, Japan had about a 50 percent market share with yards in Continental Europe and Scandinavia (previously the dominant shipbuilding regions) struggling to compete.
Similar to that of Japan, the growth of Korean shipbuilding was the result of a carefully planned industrial program predominantly focused on the export market. By 2003, Korea surpassed Japan as the world's largest shipbuilding nation.
Over the last 10 years, China, the new economic powerhouse in Asia, has been catching up fast. About fifteen years ago, even the output of German yards was higher than that of China. Since then, the situation has changed rapidly. China's output surpassed that of Germany in 1998 and Japan in 2009.
Acknowledging the competitiveness of their Chinese rivals, Korean yards started to focus on building higher-valued units such as LNG and offshore, but the sharp reduction in oil prices and resulting decline in capital expenditures by international oil companies upended this strategy. Delays and cost overruns wreaked havoc on the results of Korea's Big Three.
In recent years, the Korean yards have rediscovered the tanker market. Among the main shipping segments, it is the only one where the owners are doing reasonably well and have been willing to ink new contracts. Korea's track record of quality products and reliable delivery times has helped them capture a significant part of these orders. As Figure 1 shows, Korean yards have maintained their market share, while the Chinese captured market share from their Japanese rivals.
An interesting aspect of the resurgence of tanker orders is that, even though contract prices (quoted in US$) have barely budged (if anything, they have trended lower in recent months), the profitability of tanker orders for the Korean yards has improved. This is the result of the confluence of two factors (see Figure 2): on the one hand, the Korean won has depreciated relative to a strong US$. On the other hand, steel prices, which make up 15-25% of a ships' contract price, have been reduced by up to 40% over the last 2 years.
As a result of the strong US$, tanker newbuilding prices in Korean won have actually reached their highest level since 2010. In combination with lower steel prices, which reduced production costs, tanker orders are helping the Korean yards offset some of the operating losses incurred in other sectors.
