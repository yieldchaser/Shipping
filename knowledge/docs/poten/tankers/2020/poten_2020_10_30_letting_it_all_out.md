## Libyan oil is hitting an oversupplied market
We last discussed Libya in our Weekly Opinion a little over two months ago (July 17, 2020 - "Libya's Rollercoaster Ride"). At the time the country's production and exports were reduced to almost zero because of the continued fighting between the two factions that controlled the west and the east of the country, respectively. Although the situation was bad for Libya, the country's production and export struggles were quite helpful for the other members of OPEC, as they were trying to reduce production in the midst of the Covid-19 pandemic. Now, barely two months later, the situation has changed 180 degrees. Libyan oil is flowing again. What is its impact on the oil and tanker markets?
By the end of September, Libya's National Oil Company (NOC) lifted force majeure and started operations at several and ports and fields in the country. Early on, Hariga, Brega and Zueitina (all in the eastern part of the country), were classified as "safe ports" by the NOC. During October, more export terminals opened. In the span of several weeks, Libya's exports increased from less than 100,000 barrels per day (b/d) to more than 500,000 b/d by the middle of October. If the truce sticks, Libya's oil production and exports could reach 1 million b/d in Q1 of 2021. As a reminder, this is still lower than the 1.2 million b/d that Libya exported January of this year.
Libya's export boost comes at an inopportune time for the OPEC+ producer group, which has been struggling to balance global supply and demand for oil in the face of the Covid-19 pandemic. In May of this year, OPEC+ cut production by an unprecedented 9.7 million b/d. This production cut was scaled back to 7.7 million b/d in July and a further reduction to 5.8 million b/d was planned to commence in January 2021. This schedule of gradually lower production cuts is under pressure now. On the one hand, as we discussed above, Libya is raising output by up to 1 million b/d. On the other hand, Europe is suffering from a second wave of Covid-19 infections, which has led to reinstatement of partial lockdowns. This will likely have a negative impact on oil demand in Europe. The United States also continues to have high Covid-19 cases. This may halt or at least slow down the recovery in oil demand in the Western OECD countries. OPEC will meet again at the end of November to discuss plans for the new year, but, unless the global outlook improves dramatically between now and then, we expect that OPEC+ will decide to keep the current quota in place into 2021. OPEC+ will want to avoid a repeat of the 2 nd quarter of this year when prices collapsed due to a massive oversupply of crude. Tanker owners have fond memories of this time and won't mind a repeat, but this scenario is unlikely.
The direct impact on the tanker market of the rapidly rising Libyan exports is probably more subdued and regional. As we have discussed in previous Opinions, Libya's exports are predominantly transported on Aframax and Suezmax tankers and most of the volumes stay in the Mediterranean. Italy, in particular, is one of the largest importers of Libyan crude. When production and exports from Libya were disrupted early this year, the impact on Italy was significant. In 2019, 22% of Italy's crude oil was sourced from North Africa (primarily Libya). In 2020 to date, the share of North Africa was down to 9%. Mediterranean exporters that increased their market share in Italy during this period include the Black Sea (+2%-points) and Sidi Kerir (+3%-points). Italy also increased imports from the U.S. Gulf in 2020 (up from 4% to 7%).
Since the tanker market is significantly oversupplied and the additional barrels from Libya primarily benefit short-haul Aframax trades, the impact on the freight market has been negligible so far. After an extremely high second quarter, the freight market softened in the summer and remained in the doldrums so far. A seasonal uptick could still be in the works and in that respect, more barrels from Libya could help, especially if they are exported outside of the Mediterranean. To the extent that the U.S. crude, that is no longer going into the Med, will be exported elsewhere over longer distances, that could help the market to some extent. However, the demand side of the equation will have to pick up for a meaningful freight rebound to happen and with Covid cases rising in many parts of the world, that seems unlikely in the short term.
