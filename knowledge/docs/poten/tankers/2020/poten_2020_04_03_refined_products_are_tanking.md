## Floating storage on product tankers is on the rise
In the last few weeks, since the collapse of the OPEC+ deal and the various announcements of oil production growth by Saudi Arabia and other producers, the focus of the oil markets has been squarely on the crude oil tanker market and VLCCs in particular. Record spot rates and rapidly rising floating storage levels dominated the headlines. However, on the oil demand side of the equation, it is not the crude oil producers that are on the frontlines. It is the refiners. They are the ones that are directly and immediately exposed to global demand destruction as a result of Covid-19 and the isolation measures that are put in place all over the world. The struggles of the refining industry and the direct impact that will have on product tanker markets will be the topic of this week's Poten Tanker Opinion.
As global oil demand has declined rapidly, refiners worldwide need to adjust their output. Refining margins have come down fast, although there are differences between the various products. Refining margins for gasoline have turned negative in many parts of the world. Jet fuel is another product that is no longer profitable to make for refiners. Jet fuel demand has fallen quickly as airlines have suspended flights all over the world. Diesel, on the other hand, is still in demand (relatively speaking). Prices for diesel, in particular in Europe, have rallied in recent weeks relative to crude oil. This has led to a number of cargoes that are moving from Asia and the Middle East to Europe, primarily on LR1 tankers.
We need to consider another factor for the refining industry. While it is a global industry, the demand mix is different in different countries and this has created regular tradeflows between regions with a relative surplus for a certain product and regions with a shortage of the same product. That's why gasoline flows into the United States and Europe typically imports diesel. The Covid-19 pandemic has upended many of these trades, because demand is down in most of the world, causing product surpluses everywhere. Even in Asia, where demand is gradually starting to come back, there is still too much diesel and jet fuel.
Ultimately, the global demand destruction will hit the product trades. After all, product tankers are distributing refined products across the globe and if the demand is not there, and stocks are full, there is no need to distribute product. Price differentials between markets would normally open up arbitrage opportunities. However, in a scenario where product demand is down and there is no storage capacity available to take advantage of the price contango, trade will be significantly reduced.
Product storage on tankers can provide support to the product tanker market. It appears that this is already happening to some extent. There have been a substantial number of larger product tankers (Panamax sized LR1's and Aframax sized LR2's) utilized for storage of jet fuel and gasoline. We have even seen a report of a newbuilding VLCC utilized for the storage of jet fuel. Newbuilding crude oil tankers (VLCC's, Suezmaxes and Aframaxes) can be used to move or store clean petroleum products for a limited period of time (before they carry their first "dirty" cargo).
Storing jet fuel (and other refined products) at sea is much more complicated than storing crude oil. Jet fuel is a high specification product that is sensitive to contamination and it degrades much quicker than crude oil. Jet fuel, like most other refined products can only be stored for a few months. After that, it can no longer be used for aviation.
We have limited visibility on floating storage on smaller product tankers, like MR's. However, as more LR1 and LR2 products tankers are employed in floating storage for premium rates, MR's are increasingly being pursued as an alternative. This could support rates for MR's as well.
