## Brazil- Heavy, but Sweet
Brazil is South America's largest nation occupying more than half of the continent with its largest population of 185 million. Brazil, the tenth largest energy consumer in the world and the third largest in the Western Hemisphere after the Unites States and Canada, is undergoing rapid development with a growing population: a prescription for greater energy consumption. ## Oil Reserves
Rapidly Increasing Although Brazil has exhibited significant overall economic progress averaging 4.5% since 1981, growth has been marked by fits and starts with contractions occurring regularly followed by spurts of rapid growth. The latest retreat occurred in 2003 when the economy faltered with negative growth of 0.2%, but rapidly recouped in 2004 with growth of 5.1%. These fits and starts in economic growth are reflected in oil consumption. While the oil industry has been liberalized to permit foreign participation in the late 1990s, Petrobras still controls over 95% of the crude oil production. About 80% of Brazil's total production is offshore Rio de Janeiro state in very deep waters of the Campos Basin. The world record for drilling in the deepest waters regularly passes between Petrobras and companies drilling in the Gulf of Mexico. Crude oil from the Campos Basin is an unusual heavy sweet grade. About 1.3-1.4 million bpd of additional production is scheduled to come on stream by 2008. Considering that the gap between consumption and production has narrowed to 0.3 million bpd, it appears that Brazil is rapidly approaching a point of not only self- sufficiency, but also becoming a net exporter. Normally approaching self-sufficiency is bad news for tanker owners. But Brazil is an unusual nation in that its refineries are not geared to process heavy sweet crude. Rather than undergoing extremely costly upgrading of its refineries to handle Campos crude, Brazil has found it to be in its economically expedient to export Campos crude for refining elsewhere and importing oil products. Moreover Brazil imports lighter grade crudes for mixing with some of the Campos crude for domestic refining. This importing and exporting of crude oil removes the necessity to upgrade 13 refineries with a combined capacity of 1.9 million bpd. Additional capacity may be in the offering some years from now: in February 2005, Brazil signed an agreement with Venezuela's PdVSA to build a 150,000 to 250,000 bpd refinery in northeastern Brazil. At the present time, Brazil is a net importer of 300,000 bpd, yet exports 300,000 bpd of Campos crude. For this relationship to hold, this implies that Brazil must be importing about 600,000 bpd of light crude for blending purposes and clean products. Brazil hopes to become self-sufficient in oil during 2006 and end the year by becoming a net exporting nation of 100,000 bpd. This swing of 400,000 bpd will raise exports from the present 300,000 bpd to 700,000 bpd of Campos crude. Another 100,000 bpd is expected to come on stream in 2007, which will increase Campos crude to 800,000 bpd. Rumors of Petrobras possibly buying Citgo, the U.S. refining arm of PdVSA, and Galp Energia, which owns two refineries in Portugal, might indicate where this oil may be headed.
Petrobras' Dirty Spot Chartering Activity Taking Off! The following chart shows Petrobras' fixture activity for dirty cargoes by load area. Fixture activity was fairly constant from 1995 to 2001, then declined significantly from 2002 to 2004. So far this year, fixture activity has nearly doubled its 1995-2001 levels and tripled its 2002-2004 levels. As the chart shows, the greatest growth is Brazil's exports followed by significant growth in Caribbean cargoes. Campos exports have helped make Petrobras the seventh largest charterer of Suezmaxes in the world. Whereas in the past Caribbean cargoes were destined to Brazil, the marked increased in 2005 liftings was Campos crude being shipped from its transshipment port in the Caribbean to the United States in Aframaxes.

## Other
Med AG Carib W. Africa Brazil The chart on the next page shows Petrobras clean fixture activity. Clean fixture activity, while relatively constant from 1995 to 2001, fell substantially in 2002 and 2003 from refinery capacity additions. Fixture activity has subsequently increased with growing imports primarily from the Mediterranean accompanied by growing exports of clean products from Brazil to the United States and West Africa. Importing and exporting clean products is the time honored way of matching refined oil supply and demand without undergoing substantial refinery investments to change the product slate.

## Other
U.S. Med AG Carib Brazil A New Trend in Place We see significant growing Petrobras presence in the dirty spot fixture market for processing its new supplies of heavy sweet offshore crude. Fixture activity should remain constant for importing lighter crudes for its domestic refineries. We also see greater fixture activity in the import and export of various refined oil products as Brazil continues its policy of matching refinery output with domestic demand without having to make costly refinery investments. There will also be extra import cargoes to meet growing oil product demand. With this strategy is meeting its oil needs, we expect that Brazil will attract even greater attention with the passage of time.
