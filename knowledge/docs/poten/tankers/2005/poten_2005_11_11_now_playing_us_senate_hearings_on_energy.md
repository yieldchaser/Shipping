## Now Playing: "U.S. Senate Hearings on Energy
This past week, the U.S. Senate Committee on Commerce, Science and Transportation along with the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources held a joint and public hearing on energy pricing and profits. Each committee has 22 members for a total of 44 senators drawn together to cross examine five top executives from ExxonMobil, Chevron, ConocoPhillips, BP America, and Shell Oil. Theoretically, the intent of the hearings was to focus on gathering information for addressing the following questions:

## Chart: Ø Did the oil companies fail to add refining capacity?
Ø Are the oil companies making enough progress in exploration? The actual impact of the hearings was for those involved to have a chance to display a fairly thorough misunderstanding of the fundamental economic laws of supply and demand, the world oil markets, and the role of oil companies in dealing with the challenges of the energy business. Furthermore the hearing was a clear denial of any government role and a demonstration of the continuing lack of political will for the past thirty years to deal with events that have resulted in high energy prices. If the hearing was meant to be a serious investigation into the events leading up to the present situation of higher than average energy prices, then the wrong people were called to the hearing. But the hearing was not really a serious inquiry into key issues. If they were, we would have heard people asking questions about failed tax policies that have encouraged energy consumption, lack of investment in mass transit, and an ever increasing desire of Americans to live in big houses and drive large automobiles long distances. When the Facts Get in the Way of a Good Story! While it seems politically expedient to question energy company executives about high energy prices, and complain that it is really hurting consumers, the facts would suggest that American consumers really do not care all that much about prices nor have high prices done anything to change driving habits and hence dampen demand.

## U.S. Gasoline Consumption & Retail Price
$/Gallon Gasoline Consumption For those who live and work in such places as Houston, Los Angeles, New York, Fairfax, you have probably not noticed a significant reduction in commuting times. The incremental cost of gasoline has gone up by about $20 per week (30 miles each way to work at 15 miles to the gallon for a SUV; a $1 dollar increase in gasoline prices translates to $4 per day round trip in extra commuting expenses.) This incremental $80 per month is nominal when compared to the increased mortgage payments that have resulted from rising interest rates caused by the looming deficits by the federal government and increased taxes thanks to the Alternative Minimum Tax.
You Can't Have It Both Ways At the same time that our government representatives want answers, solutions that may be helpful are ignored. Very few proposals to reduce demand have much attraction and are hard sells such as car pools, smaller houses, smaller and more fuel-efficient cars. We also work to restrict the available supply of energy by prohibiting further development in Alaska and offshore in nearly all shoreside states. Since 1972, a Federal law has placed a restriction on terminal expansions in the Puget Sound if the expansion would result in increased crude flows. How else can refiners be expected to meet increased demand for gasoline if they can't import more crude? The energy "crisis" will be much shorter and less onerous if politically expedient grandstanding was left out of the process and more corrective solutions were proposed and implemented.
