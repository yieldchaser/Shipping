## Summary
With one major exception, crude oil closely follows economic activity. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has recently published its World Economic Outlook. The IMF has looked at the world around us and sees continued economic growth.

## Or Is It?
With one major exception, crude oil closely follows economic activity. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has recently published its World Economic Outlook. The IMF has looked at the world around us and sees continued economic growth. This means more tanker cargoes.

## U.S. 5/05
U.S. 9/04 Europe 5/05 Europe 9/04 The previous chart shows actual economic growth for 2003 and 2004. The two projections for economic growth in 2005 and 2006 compare the most current issue of the World Economic Outlook with the September 2004 issue. The IMF projection of U.S. economic growth was revised slightly upward from 3.5% to 3.6%. A regression model linking economic activity with oil consumption indicates that a continued economic growth of 3.6% will add 2 million bpd to U.S. consumption by 2010 in a world free of constraints. The IMF has lowered its estimates of economic growth for Europe. Europe is the only area of the world where there is a weak correlation between economic activity and oil consumption. The reason for this is that oil is the fuel of logistics, and the extraordinarily high taxation of motor vehicle fuels and the shift from gasoline to diesel fuels in Europe have inhibited growth in oil consumption regardless of changes in economic activity. As seen below, the IMF not only sees continued growth in economic activity in China and India, but has upgraded both.

## China 5/05
China 9/04 India 5/05 India 9/04 According to our regression model, continued growth at 8.5% per year in economic activity will increase China's oil consumption by 3 million bpd by 2010. India is projected to have economic growth of 6.7% in 2005 and 6.4% in 2006. A continuation of 6.4% growth will increase India's oil consumption by 1 million bpd by 2010. Thus the combined oil consumption increment by 2010 for the Big Three (U.S., China and India) is 6 million bpd, much of which would have to be imported as the Big Three exhibit little growth in domestic production. Several caveats, or rather constraints are in order. Current refining capacity is operating at high levels of utilization with relatively little spare capacity. With refinery expansion under construction and in the planning stage totaling only a little over 2 million bpd, a rather robust refinery construction program would be necessary to refine incremental demand of 6 million bpd. Even if the refinery situation can be resolved, we may be up against supply constraints; and even if OPEC and non-OPEC exporters can squeak out enough oil to satisfy incremental demand, oil prices will hardly have a chance to decline much from current levels. The full impact of high (over $50 per barrel) oil price has not had enough time to work its way through the global economy. Developing nations that had a tough time coping with oil at $25 per barrel will find coping at current prices that much tougher. High priced gasoline can bring about greater interest in fuel-efficient automobiles at the expense of gas-guzzling SUVs and a change in driving habits. There is some evidence that U.S. gasoline demand is leveling off. A far greater impact of high oil prices is not a change in driving habits, but a slowing of economic activity. Unfortunately, it is difficult to project a turning point in economic activity until after a turning point begins to occur.
Where Do We Stand Today? There is little evidence that points to a near-term turning point in economic activity. But we are near real constraints in crude export and oil refining capacity. IMF economic projections may be attainable in the short run, but may not be sustainable in the long run. But for now it's business as usual, and until we see some signs of "business as unusual", the IMF forecast makes for comforting reading!
