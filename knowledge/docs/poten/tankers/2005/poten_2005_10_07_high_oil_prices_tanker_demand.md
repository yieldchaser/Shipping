## High Oil Prices & Tanker Demand
With oil prices hovering above $60 per barrel and U.S. Gulf oil production currently 77.5% shut in, what is the outlook for tanker owners if oil prices remain high? The following chart shows the latest available data on U.S. refinery gasoline output, gasoline imports and inventory levels.

## Inventory
Imports Refinery Output Gasoline inventories have been falling since the beginning of July as refinery output held steady until hurricanes Katrina and Rita wrecked havoc on Gulf facilities. Imports have proven effective in keeping this market somewhat balanced in the face of refinery shortfalls.
Gasoline consumption is calculated on the basis of domestic refinery production plus imports plus changes in inventory in terms of bpd. A decrease in inventory adds to consumption and vice versa. As shown below, U.S. gasoline consumption was rising over the period June through August. However, consumption fell markedly in late August and early September primarily from disruptions in imports caused by Katrina (port closures and disruptions in distribution); not necessarily due to price increases, a conclusion one might draw in viewing the chart.

## U.S. Gasoline Consumption and Price
The classical economic relation between price and consumption is that as price increases, consumption decreases. The above chart does not bear this out. In June, July, and the first part of August, consumption actually increased along with price. American motorists complained about higher gasoline prices, but they did not change their vacation plans. But there is a price where economics, not personal desires, takes over. In reviewing crude oil prices, economic activity, and oil consumption for the United States on an annual basis since 1981, the statistical correlation between crude oil prices and oil consumption is weak (22% R Square) whereas the degree of correlation between economic activity (GDP) and oil consumption is very strong (97% R Square).
The question becomes: how do high oil prices affect the economy? Unfortunately, it is not just high oil prices that are plaguing the economy, but high energy prices. Natural gas prices are at historically high levels from increased demand in home heating and power generation. Higher competitive prices for natural gas in power generation have allowed coal companies to raise their prices as well. As a consequence, consumers are facing significantly higher gasoline, heating oil, natural gas, and electricity prices. As money pours into the coffers of energy producers both domestic and foreign, it is clear that there will be less money available for goods and services. This will have a dampening effect on the level of economic activity. The average annual growth rate of GDP in the United States has been 3.1% since 1981. The following table projects oil consumption if the historical growth rate can be sustained or if it is cut in half, as some economists predict, due to high energy costs.
U.S. Oil Consumption (000 Bpd) GDP Growth 2004 2005 2010 3.10% 20,517 20,830 22,555 1.55% 20,517 20,670 21,500 Difference 160 1,055 Whereas this year's consumption would be modestly impacted, a long period of sustained high energy prices could have a significant impact on U.S. consumption. This would lower growth in oil import demand, and hence dampen tanker demand. Investments are pouring into energy projects that will increase oil supply and dampen prices. Tanker owners do not thrive when oil prices are high, but rather when they are low to moderate. Tanker owners thrive on volume, and the traditional relationship is that lower prices yield higher consumption. Moderation of oil prices would thus enhance tanker demand by increasing economic activity, which, in turn, encourages greater oil consumption, a boon to tanker demand.
