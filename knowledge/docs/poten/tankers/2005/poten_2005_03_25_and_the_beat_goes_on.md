## Demand Accelerating
With the exception of OECD Pacific (Japan, Korea, Australia, and New Zealand), oil consumption growth rates over the last two years have been accelerating. Even Europe is showing increased growth after almost two decades of stagnant or decreasing demand. While overall demand is expected to grow in 2005, the rate of growth is slowing.

## Non-OPEC Supply Lagging
The chart on the next page shows changes in non-OPEC supply. North America exhibited no growth in supply between 2003 and 2004 and China exhibited no growth in supply between 2002 and 2003. Latin America (excluding OPEC member Venezuela) exhibited no net growth in supply for the last two years, but minor growth is anticipated for 2005. With the exception of Russia and non-OPEC Africa (Angola, Egypt and others), OPEC took the lead in filling the growing gap between world consumption and non-OPEC oil sources. Taking into consideration some degree of inventory buildup in order to keep days of inventory to consumption even with previous years, there will be some cooling of OPEC growth in 2005, but not that material. OPEC must keep producing at even higher levels in the future than now to keep oil prices within a range that does not shut down the world economy.

Will the Beat Go On? Key Question- as shown on the chart, OPEC AG production appears to be topping out, but this is misleading. Production is down for oil fields undergoing maintenance. Maintenance is being scheduled at this time in order to ensure adequate capacity to meet peak seasonal demands in 2005. However the slide in production since last fall explains some of the recent weakness in VLCC rates and maybe some of the strength in oil prices. Economists are generally opining that high oil prices, in elucidating little in adverse public response, must not be affecting the world economy. The world economy does not shift gears overnight; it takes a little time for high oil prices to work their way through the world's economies, so the jury is still out on the impact of high oil prices on the economies of various nations. Ultimately high prices are not in the best interests of OPEC nations if a world recession sets in- this is the lesson of the early 1980s. For these reasons, we think that the beat will go on, but the tempo will be a bit slower. For those who can still dance to different tunes, tanker prosperity will continue.
