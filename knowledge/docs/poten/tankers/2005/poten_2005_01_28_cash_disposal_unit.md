Cash Disposal Unit
2003 and 2004 rank among the most profitable years in tanker history. While directionally a good trend for tanker owners, this situation has created an interesting and challenging strategic issue: "How to dispose of the cash?" Spin Some off as a Dividend Of course dividend payments are more pertinent to publicly traded stocks where future stock value is presumably linked to anticipated aggregate dividends over the remaining life of the company. Publicly traded tanker companies have recently paid out hundreds of millions of dollars to their shareholders. Paying dividends transfers the challenge of cash disposal from the shoulders of the company into the arms of the shareholders. Pay off Debt This is a seemingly attractive alternative. But if the interest rate on the debt is low and there are investments with a higher rate of return, well, why not keep the debt, invest in a higher rate of return asset, and pretend you're a bank living off the spread in returns? One drawback to a fleet of fully paid off vessels is that there is little resistance in a weak market to fix vessels down to operating costs, making a mess of things for owners with debt. Although owners' desires are only part of what determine market rates, we feel that owners' would be less likely to agree to extremely low rates if they had to worry about financing costs.

## Invest in New Areas
The fabulous Greeks of the 1970s took a part of their winnings and invested in real estate, gold, art works, oil refineries, and at least one in an airline. When the longest tanker depression in history descended on them, those who invested in new areas clearly did better than those who stayed in shipping. But with a finely balanced supply/demand ratio, it is more difficult to envision a tanker depression in the near future. Yet in recent years, some owners have already diversified by investing in the rapidly growing business of transporting liquefied natural gas.

## Buy Existing Tankers
This is a tempting alternative except that ship values are at historic highs. A wait and see attitude might see lower ship values if the market remains at present levels for a period of time. Of course if too much money is sitting on the sidelines looking for lower prices, prices are not going to fall very far. Another way to accomplish this strategy is to buy another shipping company, but at today's stock prices, this is essentially equivalent to buying second hand ships at record prices.

## Order More Tankers
This is like a lottery winner who takes his winnings and buys more lottery tickets. Complete reinvestment in newbuildings over a long period of time will end up with too many tankers chasing too few cargoes. At one point or another, the winnings are going to be given up to someone else. One famous Norwegian owner managed to convert a billion dollar shipping fortune to a billion dollar negative net worth by pursuing this strategy. What To Do? This is a complex strategic question whose response varies from company to company. Paying dividends reduces the scope of the challenge by making shareholders partially responsible for addressing this issue. Paying off cheap debt when other more promising areas of cash disposal are available does not seem an optimal strategy. Investing in new areas depends on what these areas are. They could be an excellent opportunity to diversify holdings, but this depends on the nature of the investment and its risk profile as compared to shipping. In retrospect, this was the best course of action for cash generated during the 1970s tanker boom. Buying existing tankers or a tanker owning company, while certainly a viable alternative, involves paying record high values. Waiting for ship values to fall can be ultimately self-defeating if too many are waiting and watching. With shipping stocks selling substantially above book value, issuing new stock and buying ships at even today's inflated values may not be an unreasonable course of action. While ordering another vessel at today's ship prices may not seem attractive, an order or two, when blended in with a fleet acquired at lower costs, may make sense particularly if employment seems assured. In our opinion, the only dangerous strategy is for the tanker industry to double its bet by taking their winnings and ordering a slew of newbuildings. This strategy would eventually flood the market with excess capacity destroying the present delicate balance between supply and demand, bringing about another tanker recession. This is a situation that owners and charterers would do well to avoid.
