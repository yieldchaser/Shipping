## OPEC on OPEC
OPEC's website (www.opec.org) has a lot of interesting information on its plans to expand crude oil and refinery expansion. If we view high oil prices as the consequence of the supply of oil production and refinery capacity feeling the pinch of 85 million bpd demand, which continues to grow, then this information is invaluable in assessing the future direction of tanker prospects. Crude Production Constraints As we have noted previously in our report "The Fall of Oil Empires" of July 8, 2005, the United States, the United Kingdom, Indonesia, Colombia, and Egypt have falling production. "The Rise of Oil Empires" of July 15, 2005 noted higher production in North and West Africa (a mix of OPEC and non-OPEC producers), Russia, Kazakhstan (BTC pipeline), plus Brazil ("Brazil- Heavy, but Sweet" of December 16, 2005). The net contribution of the rising and falling oil empires is not enough to meet the growing worldwide demand centered in India, China, and the United States (see Tanker Prospects for details). Thus growing world demand means more cargoes from somewhere and that somewhere by default is primarily from the Arabian Gulf OPEC exporters. As seen on the next page, OPEC's production capacity is slated to grow from 32 million barrels per day to 38 million bpd by 2010. Export availability is OPEC production less domestic consumption and perhaps some expansion of spare capacity. In a world of growing demand, export availability could be interpreted as export demand. With the exception of 2006 where export availability declines by 100,000 bpd, the other years exhibit a positive incremental annual gain of 700,000 bpd. While this appears robust, every bit of this incremental supply is going to be needed if world oil demand continues to grow at 2 million bpd. From this viewpoint, we anticipate that tightness in crude oil supply will be with us at least for the next few years, if not longer.

## Chart: Refinery Capacity Constraints
Consumers however do not buy crude oil, but gasoline, diesel and jet fuel among other products. Producing more crude oil means nothing to the end user if refinery capacity does not expand to handle incremental demand. With refineries operating at near maximum sustainable operations, any shortfall in refinery output raises the price of oil products, which, in turn, can affect crude oil prices. The chart on the next page shows planned expansion of distillation plus treating and conversion capacity within OPEC by the year 2012. The total gain in refining capacity of 5.1 million bpd is significant considering OPEC's present refining capacity of 8.1 million bpd. As seen in the chart, most of the gain comes from refinery expansions in Saudi Arabia. On top of this, OPEC is also taking steps to become involved with refinery projects in China for investment purposes and to assure a market for their incremental production.

## Chart: Planned Expansion of OPEC Refinery Capacity
A Major New Trade Emerging? The significant expansion of Middle East refinery capacity along with refinery expansion plans in India and China stand in stark contrast to what is happening in the Western Hemisphere. Yes, there will be refinery creep that expands overall capacity and yes, there is talk about building new refineries (as long as they're not located in the United States). But the discussions are almost desultory compared to the determination to expand refinery capacity in the Eastern Hemisphere. If the Middle East is gearing up its refinery capacity both locally and by becoming involved with refinery projects in Asia, where are the refined products going to come from in the years that it will take to significantly build new refining capacity in the Western Hemisphere? It looks to us that some large product carriers are going to be awfully busy moving cargoes between the Middle East and the United States in the coming years.
