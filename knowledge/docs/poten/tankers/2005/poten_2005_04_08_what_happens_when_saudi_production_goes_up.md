## What Happens When Saudi Production Goes Up?
Recent press clippings indicate that Saudi Arabia is willing to expand production to prevent runaway oil prices. Apparently memories of the global economic retrenchment stemming from peak oil prices in the late 1970s and early 1980s and the associated collapse in Saudi exports still linger. What does an increase in Saudi exports mean to VLCC owners? The long term view between AG output and VLCC rates AG/Far East shows that the two are not always tied closely together.

## AG Output
WS Rates The R Square value over a fifteen year period is an anemic 28%; which is another way of saying that 28% of the variation in VLCC rates can be explained by the variation in AG exports. Part of this weak relationship is seen by the general upward trend in AG exports from 1992 to 2000 with no real improvement in VLCC rates. The reason why AG output improved without dragging VLCC rates along is delivery of a relatively large number of newbuildings during this period. These deliveries resulted from newbuilding orders placed when rates jumped during the first Gulf War. The delivery of these vessels added sufficient fleet capacity to hold rates within a narrow range. The Southeast Asia economic malaise sent both AG output and VLCC rates south in the late 1990s. After 2000, several exogenous factors affected tanker rates that were not related to AG exports. Chief among these was the impact of Erika and Prestige incidents on chartering practices to minimize chartering of older single hull tankers. In addition, active scrapping of single hull tankers from mandatory phase out provisions contained in Marpol 73/78 compensated for newbuilding deliveries keeping the VLCC fleet relatively constant in numbers until 2002.

## AG Output
WS Rates The R Square value since 2000 is a more impressive 58%. A new factor entering the picture since 2002 is the growth in the VLCC fleet. VLCC fleet size was 436 at the start of 2000 and 429 at the start of 2002. The chart on the next page shows the growth in the fleet since 2002 as VLCCs are no longer being scrapped as fast as newbuildings are being delivered from shipyards.

## # VLCCs
The R Square value between WS rates and AG output since 2002 is 60%. If we add in the VLCC population along with AG output, the R Square value for the two only improves to 62%. This means, statistically speaking, that growth in fleet size has had no impact on VLCC rates (as yet).
So Where Do We Stand? The statistical model suggests that an incremental output of AG (Saudi) crude of 200,000 bpd will increase VLCC rates by 7 Worldscale points. If Saudi Arabia adds a half million bpd of output, then we can expect an improvement of around 20 Worldscale points. This cannot be interpreted as a hard and fixed value with an R Square of only 60%, but there will be an improvement in VLCC rates. Maybe this isn't anything to get overly excited about, but it's certainly better than a reduction in AG output, which has been experienced since the start of the year along with a falloff in VLCC rates.
