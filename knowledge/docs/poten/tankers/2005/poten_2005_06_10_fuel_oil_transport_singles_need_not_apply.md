## Fuel Oil Transport - Singles Need Not Apply!
Of all forms of oil, fuel oil is one of the worst pollutants as it has no light ends to evaporate. It is a persistent pollutant that, for the most part, only nature can slowly disperse. The Erika , a Handymax of 36,694 dwt, sank in 1999 and a part of its fuel oil cargo reached the French coast creating a public uproar. Oil companies responded by chartering a greater percentage of cargoes on more modern and generally double hull tankers. The effective reduction in tanker supply by charterer preference for newer tonnage caused an upsurge in rates. A relatively short time later the Prestige , an Aframax of 81,259 dwt, sank in 2002 with part of its fuel oil cargo polluting the coastlines of Spain and Portugal. Oil companies again shifted to newer tonnage, further reducing the availability of suitable tonnage, causing rates to surge again. Tougher standards by charterers were not enough to calm the public outcry. The EU took matters in its own hands and unilaterally banned single hull tankers in European ports from carrying heavy grade oil such as heavy crudes, fuel oil, and bitumen. The ban promulgated in July 2003 became effective in October 2003. The EU also announced that it wanted to accelerate the phase out of single hull tankers calling on European ports as originally scheduled. The unilateral action taken by the EU with regard to hastening the phase out of single hull tankers was eventually adopted, with some modifications, by the IMO when it amended Marpol Regulation 13G. The EU banning of heavy oil cargoes in single hull tankers was incorporated in Marpol Regulation 13H. As of April 2005, single hull tankers above 5,000 dwt are prohibited from carrying heavy grade oils including fuel oils to and from ports of signatory nations.
In addition to the ongoing fleet renewal that is occurring, regulatory changes are clearly impacting the Panamax sector. As show below, the role of single hull Panamax tankers is clearly declining.

## % Participation Single Hull Panamaxes in Spot Dirty Fixtures
Single hull Panamax tankers remain active in the Caribs/U.S. trade. Tankers calling on U.S. ports must comply with the U.S. Oil Pollution Act of 1990, with its own single hull tanker phase out schedule. There are, however, no special restrictions for fuel oil cargoes.

## Double
Single The average Worldscale point spread between double and single hull Panamaxes trading dirty Carib/US since January 2004 has been 34 WS points, or an estimated $10,000-$12,000 per day. The point spread is indicative of two phenomena: O Single hull tankers excluded from EU and IMO nations are concentrating in areas where employment is available. However in order to obtain employment, owners of single hull tankers must compete against one another for the available cargoes.
O Charterers are concerned about the potential liabilities that can be incurred by an oil spill in U.S. waters. Double hull tankers would result in no oil spill for low energy collisions or groundings where only the outer hull is breeched. Some charterers are willing to pay a premium for double hull tankers for the added protection.
Trends Are Hard to Fight The shift in preference by charterers for newer double hull tankers in the wake of the Erika and Prestige spills, the unilateral EU ban on single hull tankers for heavy oil cargoes, and the new IMO shipping regulations with regard to heavy fuel oil cargoes have established a trend. The consequence of this trend is that double hull tankers are and will be able to command better rates than single hull tankers for fuel oil transport.
