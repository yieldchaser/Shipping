## Significant Reduction of Greenhouse Gas Emissions Targeted
During the most recent session of its Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC), which took place from April 9 th to 13 th in London, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) adopted an initial strategy to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from international shipping (the "GHG Strategy"). The headline target of the agreement is to reduce the total annual GHG emissions from international shipping by at least 50% by 2050 (32 years from now) compared to 2008. According to the IMO, which is the agency of the United Nations responsible for regulating shipping, "The strategy includes a specific reference to a pathway of CO2 emissions reduction consistent with the Paris Agreement temperature goals. While some member states and lobby groups would have preferred a commitment to even higher reductions, it appears that the IMO has reached a compromise that is ambitious enough to avoid unilateral regulations by member states, like the EU. The impact of the GHG Strategy on the shipping industry (shipowners and shipyards) will be profound. The effect of the introduction of ballast water treatment systems and the reduction in sulphur content of bunker fuels pales in comparison to the impact that the implementation of these rules will have on our industry.
Greenhouse gases, which scientists started linking to global warming in the 1960s and '70s, were first discussed by the United Nations (UN) in 1988. It became a key topic in 1992, during the "Earth Summit" in Rio de Janeiro, and in 1997, the Kyoto Protocol adopted binding targets for 37 industrialized countries and the European community to reduce GHG emissions. After Kyoto, the IMO started to pay attention to GHG emissions from ships and the First IMO GHG Study published in 2000, estimated that international shipping contributed about 1.8% of the world total anthropogenic (originating in human activity) CO2 emissions in 1996. The Second IMO GHG Study from 2009, estimated shipping's contribution for 2007 to be 2.8%. The third (most recent) study, done in 2014, estimated international shipping to emit 2.3% of global CO2 in 2012 (see table).
In 2016, the IMO mandated that ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above will have to collect consumption data for each type of fuel they use. The IMO estimates that these vessels account for approximately 85% of CO2 emissions from international shipping. This data, which the Flag States are required to collect and report will be entered into the IMO Ship Fuel Oil Consumption Database. This mandatory data collection system, which entered into force in March 2018, should provide robust data and information on the basis of which future decisions can be made. So, how will the industry go about meeting the IMOs aggressive GHG reduction targets? There are two obvious avenues: (1) improving ship efficiency, and/or (2) switching to low (or zero) carbon fuels. Given these ambitious goals, the industry will need to aggressively pursue both avenues.
Since 2011, the MEPC has started to focus on guidelines to improve ship energy efficiency as a way to reduce GHG emissions. In July of that year, the IMO mandated the Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI) for new ships and the Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan (SEEMP) for all ships. The EEDI requires a minimum energy efficiency level per capacity mile, and this level is to be tightened incrementally every five years. We are currently in phase 1 (10% reduction). Phase 2 (20% reduction) will go into effect in 2020 and EEDI phase 3, provisionally scheduled for 2025 will force all vessels built after that year to be 30% more efficient than the current fleet. It is important to note that the calculation of EEDI values is complex and varies by ship type, so the information above is necessarily simplified. SEEMP is an operational measure that establishes a mechanism to improve the energy efficiency of a ship in a cost-effective manner. It encourages shipping companies to manage ship and fleet efficiency performance over time and to incorporate best practices for fuel efficient ship operation.
The technology of Zero Emission Vessels (ZEVs) is developing rapidly. A 2017 study by LR and University Maritime Advisory Services (UMAS) identified several options, including biofuels, fuel cells, batteries and synthetic fuels (hydrogen and ammonia). However, none of these options are expected to be competitive compared to conventional fuels by within the next 10-15 years unless a significant carbon tax was applied.
At the moment, as the LR/UMAS correctly points out, there does not seem to be a "silver bullet" to replace fossil fuels in shipping. A combination of available options seems to be the way to go. There is still time, but the challenges facing the industry are so big that it we need to start addressing it sooner rather than later.
