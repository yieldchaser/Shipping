## U.S. refined product exports continue to grow
The rapid growth of U.S. crude oil production and (more recently) the rise of crude oil exports from the U.S. Gulf have grabbed all the headlines. However, the U.S. still produces and exports more refined products than crude and this trade continues to grow. In a report that came out this week the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) noted that U.S. exports of petroleum products grew by 555,000 barrels per day (b/d) in 2017 to reach 5.2 million b/d. This was a 12% increase over 2016. Not as high as the 89% increase in crude oil exports, but still a healthy growth rate. Within the group of petroleum products, there is a wide discrepancy in growth rates between the different commodities, various export destinations and, in the case of seaborne exports, what kind of vessel is used for the movement. In today's Opinion, we will elaborate on these developments to determine the impact of these U.S. exports on the international product tanker market.
The refining industry in the U.S. continues to expand, assisted by strong product demand, both domestic and international. From 2016 to 2017, refining output in the U.S. grew by 379,000 b/d (+2.3%) to reach 19,893 kb/d. The operating capacity of the U.S. refiners barely changed, but the utilization rate went up from 89.7% in 2016 to 91% in 2017. As domestic demand in the U.S. has not changed significantly in recent years, most of the additional product output is being exported. We expect that U.S. refiners will maintain their competitive edge in world market based on growing domestic crude supplies and ample cheap natural gas to power their facilities. Earlier this month ExxonMobil announced that they expect to add 400,000 b/d of capacity to their Gulf Coast refineries by 2025.
A review of the U.S. petroleum product exports since 2000 (see chart) shows that export volumes started to take off a little more than 10 years ago, primarily driven by middle distillates (diesel). Middle distillates are still the largest export product. In 2017, the U.S. exported 1.4 million b/d according to the EIA, 17% more than the 2016 volumes. Gasoline is the no. 2 commodity with average daily exports of 747,000 barrels in 2017, 18% higher than in 2016. Other export products include jet fuel and residual fuel oil. Exports streams that are not included in the chart, because they are not moved on product tankers, are Liquified Petroleum Gas (LPG), ethanol and petroleum coke. Of these three, LPG (transported in cooled or pressurized gas carriers) is by far the largest and fastest growing. Over the last 5 years, LPG exports increased more than six-fold, from 196,000 b/d in 2012 to 1,225,000 b/d in 2017. Ethanol is a relatively small (90,000 b/d) export stream that moves on chemical tankers. Pet coke is exported on dry bulk carriers. The pet coke trade reached 586,000 b/d-equivalent in 2017.
U.S. refiners export their products worldwide, although the majority of sales are to countries in the region. Europe has traditionally been a significant customer and volumes continue to trend up, although Europe's relative share has been reduced from almost 20% of U.S. exports in 2010 to a little more than 12% last year. Exports to Canada have shown a similar development. The areas that expanded their market share are Asia and Central/Latin America. They have been the fastest growing recipients of U.S. refined products. In 2017, 21% of U.S. product exports went to Asia and more than 50% to nearby countries in the Caribbean, Latin America and Central America. Given the nature of the product trades and the distances involved, it is not surprising that the MR product tankers are the dominant vessels class in these trades. Continued growth in U.S. exports bodes well for this workhorse of the product trades.
The U.S. also remains a significant importer of refined products, although imports are less than half of what they were at their peak in 2005 (2.1 mb/d currently versus 4.5 mb/d at its peak). By far the largest exporter to the U.S. is Canada (30%), but most of those volumes are moved short-haul on the East Coast, so they don't benefit tanker markets to the same extent.
