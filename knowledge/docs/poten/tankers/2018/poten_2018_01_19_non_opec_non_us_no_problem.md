## Non OPEC production outside the U.S. remains relevant
Over the last couple of years, two topics have dominated the oil (and by extension the tanker) markets: OPEC production, or the lack thereof and U.S. shale output. Judging by the amount of ink that is used to discuss these popular themes, one may think that not much is happening in the rest of the world, i.e. the non- OPEC producers outside of the U.S. that are not part of the OPEC/non-OPEC production agreement. That is not entirely true. By the end of 2017, total world oil production reached an estimated 97.8 mb/d. OPEC countries represented roughly 40% of total production. The U.S. is the largest non-OPEC producer with an output of 13.6 m/d (13.9% market share). The 11 non- OPEC producers that participate in the OPEC cuts represent another 19% of global output. That leaves about 21.5 mb/d of production that is controlled by the rest of the non-OPEC countries (Non OPEC/Non US). There is both growth and decline among these (mostly smaller) producers, both in the short term (this year) as well as in the long term. Some of these changes could have a material impact on the shipping markets.
While all the attention has been on the United States, there are two other significant producers in North America: Canada and Mexico. Canadian production continues to show steady growth. In 2016, our northern neighbors produced 4.47 mb/d. Last year, this increased 6.5% to 4.76 mb/d and in 2018 the IEA expects production to reach 5.05 mb/d another 290,000 b/d (6%) increase. Since Canada has limited seaborne export infrastructure it is expected that virtually all these extra barrels will be transported through pipelines and via rail to the United States. Mexico's production has been slowly declining in recent years. While Mexico voluntarily participates in the OPEC production restraints, oil market analysts expect that Mexico's production would have gone down regardless.
In Latin America, Brazil is the largest producer and the key country to keep an eye on. Brazil's output is steadily increasing; from 2.61 mb/d in 2016 to 2.75 mb/d in 2017 (+140,000 b/d) and an estimated 2.93 mb/d in 2018 (+180,000 b/d). Brazil is a significant exporter of crude and its oil moves to destinations in both the Atlantic as well as in the Pacific Basin, creating significant ton-mile demand. Suezmaxes and VLCCs are the primary beneficiaries. Production from other relevant producers in South America, like Argentina and Colombia has remained relatively stable in recent years.
In Asia, the key non-OPEC producers are struggling. Indonesia and Malaysia are part of the OPEC deal but their production has been on a declining trend in recent years anyway. Australian output is expected to pick up this year, but the volumes remain small. China is by far the largest non-OPEC producer in Asia, but the output of its aging oil fields is declining. Back in 2015, China's production was still 4.34 mb/d. In 2016, this had dropped to 3.99 mb/d. Last year production stood at 3.86 mb/d and a further 150,000 b/d decline to 3.71 mb/d is expected in 2018. China is the world's fastest growing oil consumer (in volume) as well as the largest seaborne crude oil importer. Therefore, China's domestic production declines are a boon for the tanker market, both in terms of tons and ton-miles. The other large oil consumer in Asia is India. However, India's production, which is much smaller (around 850,000 b/d), has been stable. The impact of the country's oil demand growth on the tanker market is also more limited due to India's proximity to the oil resources of the Middle East.
Oil production in Europe is a mixed bag. After many years of declines, North Sea production has been fairly stable in recent years. Norway has maintained its production at 2.0 mb/d since 2016, although a 70,000 b/d reduction is forecast for this year. U.K. output, on the other hand, is expected to rise by 150,000 b/d in 2018. However, a significant portion of North Sea crude stays in Europe, limiting the contribution to tanker demand.
In summary, non-OPEC crude oil production remains supportive to the tanker market, whether due to growing (e.g. Brazil) or declining output (e.g. China).
