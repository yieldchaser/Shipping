## Summary

## Why does ordering continue while markets are down?
While the tanker market has remained in the doldrums so far this year, demolition has picked up substantially. That makes sense. That is what we expect to happen when the freight markets turn down for an extended period of time. Another thing that we expect under these circumstances is a decline in newbuilding orders. When tanker rates are hovering around (and sometimes well below) operating expenses, who would want to (or can afford to) build expensive new vessels? Surprisingly, newbuilding orders are continuing despite low rates, stretched balance sheets and a general lack of financing? What is going on?
In the first four months of 2018, tanker rates have been depressed. Product carriers, in particular MRs, have fared better than crude oil tankers and across both markets, the largest vessels (VLCCs in crude and LR2s in products) are underperforming the most. VLCCs are suffering from a steady influx of new tonnage and subdued demand growth due to the continuing OPEC production cuts. Rising U.S. domestic production also has cut into long haul tanker demand as the refiners are using more domestic crude. In the face of rising pipeline imports from Canada, seaborne U.S. crude oil imports from the Middle East have declined disproportionately. Increased crude oil exports from the U.S. Gulf have been able to mitigate these developments to some extent, but not completely. The short-term outlook for the tanker market remains negative as OPEC has signaled a continued commitment to production cuts and newbuildings continue to be delivered at a steady clip. As of April 1, 9 VLCCs, 14 Suezmaxes, 16 Aframaxes/LR2s, 6 Panamaxes/LR1s and 12 MRs have been delivered, a total of 57 vessels. Another 200 vessels are scheduled for delivery over the remainder of this year, including 46 VLCCs and 33 Suezmaxes. So, why do owners continue to order new vessels?
There are a number of reasons for the continued ordering, the main ones being growth, fleet replacement and asset play. Let's look at the VLCC orderbook for example. Since November 2017, 31 VLCCs have been ordered. One third of the total (11 vessels) were ordered by various Chinese entities. Not surprisingly these vessels were all ordered at Chinese shipyards (Dalian and Jinhai). The second largest group of VLCC owners visiting the shipyards were the traditional Greeks. All of these companies, most of them family owned, have a long shipping tradition. Some are building one or two vessels while others are making a more significant commitment to the industry. All the orders from the Greek owners (9 total from November 2017 - March 2018) were placed at shipyards in Korea. Some of the Greek orders are driven by fleet replacement in combination Korean owners placed several other orders at Korean yards. Of the remaining orders, three are from Japanese owners at - you guessed it - a Japanese shipyard.
As is almost always the case in shipping, the reasoning of the owners to order these vessels makes sense from their (individual) perspective. They intend to replace an ageing fleet, take advantage of historically low newbuilding prices and/or they want to position themselves for future demand growth and higher freight rates. The problem arises if too many owners have the same ideas at the same time: the expected market improvements never materialize. We may not be at that stage yet, but the ingredients are there: many owners are positive about the medium-term outlook for the tanker market and enough of them have the financial resources to make a down payment on one or more newbuildings.
The pickup in scrapping is encouraging and increased demolition will obviously reduce the impact of the newbuilding deliveries this year and next. However, as long as the market remains significantly oversupplied, tanker owners would benefit from a pause in tanker contracting. Against that backdrop, recent reports that some investment funds are eyeing speculative asset play because of low newbuilding prices offered by Chinese and Korean yards are troublesome. That is not what the tanker market needs.
