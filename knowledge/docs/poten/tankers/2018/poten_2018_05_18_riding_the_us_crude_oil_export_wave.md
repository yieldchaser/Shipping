## Changes in U.S. crude flows are a net positive for tankers
In December 2015, president Obama lifted the ban on U.S. crude oil exports, which had been in place since the 1970s. The immediate impact was muted because oil prices were in freefall and U.S. crude oil production was in a downward spiral as a result. The challenging markets forced the U.S. oil industry to become more efficient and lower its breakeven prices. As a result, U.S. producers were well positioned for growth when oil prices staged a recovery after OPEC cut oil production, although few people expected that tight oil production would expand this much so quickly. In this weeks' Tanker Opinion, we will try to analyze what the net impact on the tanker market has been of this rapid increase in U.S. production and exports.
Before the export ban was lifted, the U.S. was predominantly a crude oil importer. While there were occasional export cargoes originating from Alaska and a steady flow of crude oil that was shipped from the U.S. Gulf to the east coast of Canada (which was exempt from the export ban), these volumes all paled in comparison to the huge quantities of seaborne crude oil that the U.S. imported from countries in the Middle East, Latin America and West Africa. Total U.S. crude oil imports (including pipeline movements from Canada) peaked in 2005 at 10.1 million barrels per day (mb/d). This coincided with record oil demand in the United States. In the ten-year period from 2005 through 2015, U.S. crude oil imports declined by more than 25%. A significant driver of this reduction in imports was the growth in domestic (shale) oil production. According to data from the EIA, U.S. crude oil production dipped below 5 million b/d in 2008, but has grown to well above 10 million b/d so far this year, with projections reaching 12 million b/d or more in the next decade.
Because of the export ban, growing U.S. crude supplies had to find a home in the domestic markets. At the same time, growing Canadian production was also competing for U.S. customers. So, the reduction in seaborne crude oil imports in the U.S. was very significant from 2005 to 2015. However, After the export ban was lifted, the economics for crude oil imports changed dramatically. For example, the East Coast of the United States (PADD 1) imported an average of 1.6 million b/d of light sweet crude in 2003. This declined to an average of 600,000 b/d in 2014 as East Coast refiners used increasing volumes of U.S. (Bakken) crude, transported to their facilities by a combination of rail and barges. When exports were allowed, the pricing advantage of domestic crudes disappeared and imports started to grow again (averaging 913,000 b/d in 2017).
What have been the implications of all these changes for the tanker market? A review of crude oil tanker demand related to U.S. imports and exports (see chart 2), shows that ton-mile demand bottomed out at 88 billion ton-miles in November 2014. Since crude oil exports were allowed, ton-mile demand from the U.S. has grown, in particular after charterers started using VLCCs for long-haul crude oil exports from the U.S. Gulf to Asia. This trade started in earnest in 2007 and has been growing quickly. Comparing Q1 2017 with the same period of 2018, VLCC imports into the U.S. Gulf reduced from 19 voyages per month in 2017 to 16 per month in 2018 (all from the Arabian Gulf). Over the same time period exports from the U.S. Gulf on VLCCs increased by an average 2.7 voyages per month (all going to Asia). Almost all these exports out of the U.S. Gulf on VLCCs are done via reverse lightering and given the distances involved, the net effect of these changes is an increase in tanker ton-mile demand.
In conclusion, the changes in the oil trades involving the U.S. since late 2014/ early 2015 have contributed to tanker ton-mile demand. The U.S. East Coast has been importing more seaborne crude, while the U.S. Gulf has more than compensated for a reduction in imports with a growing export trade. Increases in shale oil production will stimulate more crude oil exports and the infrastructure improvements in the U.S. Gulf (e.g. LOOP reversal and Corpus Christi dredging/expansion) will lead to more VLCC exports, moving U.S. crude over longer distances, further supporting ton-mile demand.
