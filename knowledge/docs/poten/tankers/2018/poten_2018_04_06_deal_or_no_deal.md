## Will Iranian Sanctions Make a Comeback?
For most of this decade, Iranian oil production and exports have been reduced due to international sanctions, imposed as a result of of its nuclear ambitions and because of humanitarian concerns. In July 2015, the P5+1 (China, France, , Russia, the U.K the U.S and Germany.) reached an agreement (JCPOA) with Iran on its nuclear capabilities in return for sanctions relief. The implementation phase of the sanctions relief started in January 2016. President Trump has for a long time expressed his dissatisfaction with the agreement and in October 2017 announced that he would not recertify the agreement. The next deadline to extend the sanctions waiver is May 12, 2018, and at this point the Trump administration may announce that the U.S. will snap back its sanctions on Iran. In this opinion we will review Iran's recent crude production and exports and discuss the possible impact of renewed sanctions on the tanker market.
In December 2015, before sanctions relief went into effect, Iran produced 2.91 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) of crude oil. Iran managed to increase production faster than many analysts expected and by December 2016, production had increased to 3.77 MB/d. Production growth has stabilized since, and currently the IEA estimates production at around 3.8 Mb/d.
Based on Lloyd's List Intelligence data, Iran has exported an average of 2.2 Mb/d of crude oil over the last 6 months. China is the single largest importer with about 0.6 Mb/d and as the 2 nd graph indicates, imports have remained largely constant, even after the sanctions were lifted. Other large Asian importers are India, Japan and Korea. Combined with China, these countries account for about 65% of the total seaborne exports. Since the lifting of sanctions, countries in the Mediterranean, especially Turkey, Italy and Greece have re- emerged into large importers of Iranian crude.
Given the high proportion of Far Eastern buyers, VLCC's represent the main vessel class for Iranian exports, about 55% of the total. Suezmaxes are used for about 35% of the total exports, and logically dominate the Mediterranean trades and about half the volumes to India also move on Suezmax tankers.
President Trump recently replaced a part of his foreign policy team. He announced that John Bolton would head the National Security Council and he nominated Mike Pompeo as the new Secretary of State (this position requires Senate approval). Both of these men are seen as significantly more hawkish toward Iran and it is generally believed that their nomination increases the likelihood that the U.S. will re-instate sanctions.
If the U.S. decides to re-instate the sanctions, the impact is far from clear. The earlier sanctions regime, which lead to a significant reduction of Iranian crude oil exports, was successful because of the cooperation of the international community, in particular the support from the EU. If the U.S. reinstates sanctions, the international response is likely much less coordinated and it is not clear what measures the U.S. might use to encourage cooperation. Many of the other parties in the JCPOA do not agree with the re-instatement of sanctions. Some of the largest buyers, such as China, India and Turkey (~1.2 Mb/d) might not participate in import reductions. Japan and Korea, with combined imports of about 0.4 Mb/d, are more likely to cooperate given their security arrangement with the U.S. and the situation in North Korea. The reaction from European buyers (~0.55 Mb/d) is unclear and different countries or companies might take a divergent approach, depending on their situation.
Given the above, U.S. sanctions would likely reduce Iranian exports by a maximum of 500-600 Kb/d. Replacement barrels for Japan and Korea would mostly be sourced from other Middle Eastern countries, reducing the impact on the VLCC market. Lost European barrels might be replaced by a combination of Middle Eastern crude and short haul Russian barrels from the Baltic and the Black Sea, resulting in a minor negative impact. The biggest immediate impact might be on crude oil prices.
