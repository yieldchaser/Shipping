## Does refining maintenance impact crude flows?
According to the latest IEA Oil Market Report, global refinery crude throughput reached 82.2 million barrels per day (mb/d) in December 2017, some 500,000 b/d more than in December 2016. For 2017 as a whole, throughput averaged 80.8 mb/d, a solid 1.5 mb/d higher than the year before. The strongest growth came from the U.S. and China, the countries with the world's largest refining capacity. There is a certain seasonality in refining throughput and during the year this may have an impact on crude oil and refined product movements as well as stock levels. In this opinion we will try to determine if there are any clear patterns in the movements to the U.S. and China.
Seasonality in refining output can be driven by a variety of factors. Changes in product output and maintenance (turnarounds) are some of the key drivers. Refinery outages are normal. They result from the planned shutdown of refinery units for maintenance and upgrades and from unplanned shutdowns from a variety of causes such as mechanical failure, bad weather, power failures, fire, and flooding. Most refineries go through a turnaround every three to five years to perform preventative maintenance, renovations or upgrades. During turnarounds, which are planned years in advance and cost millions of dollars, the refinery will shut down all or part of the process units for a period of 3-9 weeks.
Planned maintenance is typically scheduled when refined petroleum product consumption is relatively low. In the U.S. that means in the fall and in the first quarter when there is less demand for transportation fuels. This pattern is shown to some extent in the first chart. U.S. refinery outages are typically small in the summer (the "driving season") and the early part of the winter. Maintenance and turnarounds usually take place in the spring (when refiners switch from winter to summer spec gasoline) and in the fall (switching back to winter spec). Some of the spikes can be attributed to unplanned outages (like Hurricane Harvey in August/September 2017).
In China, the pattern of refinery turnarounds is different, as can be seen from the second chart. Refinery outages are low in the winter (November - March) but pick up considerably in April/May. There does not seem to be much of a pattern in the period from June through October, although it appears to be trending down. The rapid growth in refining capacity in China year over year also makes it difficult to draw clear conclusions about the seasonality of China's refining turnarounds.
The link between crude oil imports and refining turnarounds is tenuous at best. In the case of the United States, there is very little variation between the monthly imports throughout the year. Crude oil imports in the summer months (June, July, August) are slightly higher than the imports during the rest of the year, but the differences are small. In China, there is more variability. Average monthly imports tend to be increasing from January through April and again from October through December. However, the growing totals in China could skew these numbers somewhat.
It is difficult to draw clear conclusions from the above data that would help explain movements in ton-mile demand for crude oil. In recent years, the U.S. has become less dependent on seaborne crude oil due to more domestic production and pipeline imports from Canada. Also, in the U.S. individual refiners make independent decisions regarding when and how much crude they will import. Turnarounds are planned long in advance and inventories of both crude oil and refined products serve as a buffer for refiners to make sure they can meet their obligations. In case of unexpected, severe disruptions, U.S. refiners can always "borrow" crude from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. The situation in China is less transparent, but because the refining industry is dominated by a handful of state-owned companies, a significant level of cooperation and coordination is taking place to make sure that the country's refiners receive a steady flow of crude oil.
