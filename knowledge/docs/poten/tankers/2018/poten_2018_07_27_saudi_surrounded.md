## Oil from the Kingdom flows through many chokepoints
On Wednesday, July 25, Yemeni Houthi militia attacked two Saudi VLCCs after they passed through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, a waterway that links the Gulf of Aden with the Red Sea. In response, Khalid al-Falih, Minister of Energy, Industry and Mineral Resources of the Kingdom and chairman of Saudi Aramco announced that Saudi Arabia would temporarily halt oil shipments through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait "until the situation becomes clearer and the maritime transit through Bab el- Mandeb is safe." Although it appears that the Houthi militia specifically target the merchant ships from nations supporting the Saudi led Coalition (SLC), there is always a risk of collateral damage or misidentification to all merchant traffic, including those not flagged to SLC states and Intertanko has urged their members transiting to this area to "utilize the Maritime Security Transit Corridor while remaining as far from the Yemen coast as navigationally safe." While we expect that the impact of the attack on the Bahri VLCCs will be both temporary and limited, it highlights some of the vulnerabilities for the largest OPEC producer as it depends on some of the biggest oil transit chokepoints for its crude oil and product exports.
This week, the focus is on the Bab el-Mandeb Strait which is located between Yemen, Djibouti and Eritrea. Based on the latest available data from the EIA, an estimated 4.8 million barrels per day (mb/d) of crude oil and refined petroleum products flowed through this waterway in 2016, 2.8 mb/d northbound and 2.0 mb/d southbound. Northbound movements are mostly crude oil for delivery to Saudi refineries on the Red Sea coast as well as crude deliveries to the Ain Sukhna terminal (for transport through the Sumed pipeline to Sidi Kerir) and last, but not least, direct shipments through the Suez Canal to the Mediterranean and Southern Europe. Southbound movements are predominantly products, a significant portion of which originate from Saudi export refineries on the country's west coast. Saudi Arabia has built more than 1.5 million b/d of refining capacity on the Red Sea coast, with another 400,000 b/d starting up later this year. Saudi Aramco has several ways to mitigate the impact of a long- term halt of shipments through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. To feed its west coast refineries (as well as boost its crude oil exports) the Kingdom could increase flows through the 1,200- kilometer East-West pipeline ("Petroline") to Yanbu. The pipeline has a capacity of 5 million b/d and is thought to be less than 50% utilized. Saudi Arabia could increase exports of crude oil and products from the West Coast by shipping more through the Sumed pipeline and via the Suez Canal. However, a significant portion of its customers are in Asia and shipping refined products from the Red Sea to Asia via the Suez Canal does not seem economically viable.

## Note: Excludes product cargoes < 60,000 tons
The Suez Canal itself is also a potential chokepoint for the Saudis. In 2016, the Suez Canal and the Sumed pipeline combined accounted for about 5.5 million b/d of crude oil and refined product movements. We don't have exact data about how much of this traffic originates from Saudi Arabia, but it is likely a significant portion.
The third (and by far the biggest) potential bottleneck for Saudi exports is the Strait of Hormuz, which connects the Arabian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Some 30% of the world's seaborne-traded crude oil and the vast majority of Saudi crude oil exports flow through the Strait of Hormuz. As recently as last week, Iran renewed its threat to blockade Hormuz if U.S. sanctions are effective in curtailing Iranian exports. However, the importance of the Strait of Hormuz for the international crude oil trades makes it at the same time an attractive target as well as an unlikely one, because any attempt to actually block Hormuz will probably invite a swift and overwhelming response from the U.S. Navy, which has a significant presence in the area.
For the moment, the oil and tanker world does not seem to be too concerned, but there is always a risk that this conflict could quickly escalate.
