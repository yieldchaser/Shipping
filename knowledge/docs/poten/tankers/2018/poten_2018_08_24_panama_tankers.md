## As expected, Panama Canal not a major factor for crude flows
On June 26 th , 2016 the container ship "COSCO Shipping Panama" was the first vessel that officially passed through the expanded Panama Canal. Since then, the trade has gradually expanded and has developed into a critical part of the maritime infrastructure. On July 29 th , 2018 the Panama Canal registered the transit of its 4,000 th Neopanamax vessel, when the LNG carrier Maria Energy made the passage from the Atlantic to the Pacific Ocean. In 2016 we wrote an Opinion (" To Fit or Retrofit ") indicating that oil tankers where initially only a small part of the ships transiting the Canal and that it would be interesting to see whether traders and oil companies would be able to develop trades that use the Canal. Now that the Canal has been open for a bit more than two years, we thought it would be interesting to review the current utilization of the Canal by Aframax and Suezmax tankers.
The capacity utilization of the Neopanamax locks has gradually increased. The design capacity of the locks was 12 transits per day but during the first few months the average number of transits was below 4 vessels per day. In July 2018, an average of 7.52 ships passed through the new expanded locks, the first month in which the average daily transits exceeded 7 vessels per day. The latest transit statistics provided by the canal authorities show that container ships make up almost half (49%) of the ships using the Neopanamax locks between October 2017 and June 2018. LPG (25%) and LNG (12%) tankers make up the vast majority of the remaining vessels passing through the Canal. LNG tanker transits were originally limited by daylight restrictions and an encounter restriction (two LNG vessels could not navigate Gatun Lake at the same time), but the canal authorities have gradually relaxed these restrictions. LNG transits are expected to increase further as more export capacity becomes available along the US Gulf and East Coast. So far, crude oil tankers make up only 3% of the total transits.
Since the opening of the Neopanamax locks, we have counted 92 transits of Aframax and Suezmax tankers. Many of the tankers have used the canal as a repositioning shortcut as more than half (53) of the transits where for oil tankers travelling in ballast. Many of these are transits of tankers using the canal to get to load areas in the Caribbean and the U.S. Gulf after discharging on the U.S. West Coast. Only 6 of the 29 Suezmax tankers passing through the Canal were laden, which was at least partially caused by the fact that the Canal was not able to operate at design draft due to low water levels for most of the period. We estimate that at a draft of 14 m, which was in effect for about half of the period, a Suezmax tanker can only carry about 15,000 tons more than an Aframax.
The most common laden voyage through the Neopanamax locks is for cargoes loading in Covenas and discharging on the US West Coast. In some cases, light loaded ships top up at the Trans Panama Pipeline (PTP) terminal before proceeding to the USWC. The Canal is only rarely used by larger oil tankers to move cargoes from the U.S. Gulf or Caribbean to Asia, although it has occurred a couple of times. It is typically more cost effective to use VLCCs sailing around the Cape of Good Hope or alternatively using the PTP pipeline and utilizing VLCCs that discharged on the West Coast to move the cargo to Asia. In this case, one is benefiting from the freight differential between using a front haul voyage from the Caribbean to Asia vs the backhaul voyage from the West Coast of Central America to Asia. As expected, the expanded Panama Canal is rarely used for LR2 product movements. Although a fair amount of petroleum products move between the Atlantic and the Pacific and vice versa, they are typically shipped in smaller product carriers that use the regular Panama Canal locks.
So far, the expanded Panama Canal locks have been successful and well utilized. As expected, container vessels and gas carriers (both LNG and LPG) have been the main beneficiaries, while tanker tradeflows have been less conducive to the utilization of the Neopanamax locks.
