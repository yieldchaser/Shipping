## Will pipeline constraints hold back U.S. crude exports?
For any product or commodity to be successful in the marketplace, it is important that it can be produced for a competitive price and in sufficient volumes and is delivered to customers on a timely basis. These rules apply to crude oil as well. In the United States, the rapid growth in light tight oil (LTO) production, in particular from the Permian Basin in Texas, has created some logistical challenges that are currently being addressed. On the one hand expanded production requires more pipelines to expand the takeaway capacity out of the region. On the other hand, to facilitate more exports, midstream operators are also investing in crude oil terminals, storage facilities and connecting pipelines. The question is: will the infrastructure expansion be ready in time to accommodate the expected growth in oil production and exports?
There are a lot of moving parts in the U.S. crude oil production, transportation and export puzzle. In recent years, West Texas, in particular the Permian Basin has been the largest source of production growth in the United States. Total onshore production of crude and condensate in Texas amounted to 4.0 mb/d in 2017, up 640 kb/d from the end of 2015. According to the IEA, some 2.8 million b/d of new pipeline capacity is expected to be built between 2018 and 2020. This new capacity is critical to handle the rapidly increasing LTO production. At the end of 2017, production and exports were closely matched and the expectation is that with rising LTO production tight pipeline capacity will turn into a deficit in the second half of 2018 and this gap could widen until mid-2019. From 2020 onward pipeline capacity is expected to be more than adequate to satisfy production and exports. Almost every U.S. midstream company is involved in one or more pipeline projects in the Permian and Eagle Ford basins. If all projects are built, takeaway capacity will more than double to reach 5.8 mb/d by the end of 2020 (see figure 1). A lot of the planned projects for 2018 and early 2019 are expansions of existing pipelines, while significant new construction will start to come online in 2019 & 2020. The biggest new pipelines are the 550,000 b/d EPIC pipeline (due 3Q19) and the South Texas Gateway (600,000 b/d due 1Q20). All the new pipelines in Texas are expected to run between the Permian/Eagle Ford and the Gulf coast, except the Sunrise pipeline, which runs between the Delaware Basin and Cushing, Oklahoma.
Booming LTO production has led to a substantial increase in crude oil exports (see figure 2), but limited export capacity could hamper future increases, although significant investment in new pipelines and terminals is planned. Outside of these ports, there is the Louisiana Offshore Oil Port (LOOP), which operates a single buoy mooring (SBM) 18 miles off the coast of Louisiana near the town of Port Fourchon. LOOP, which was built as an import terminal, is the only facility in the U.S. that can accommodate fully loaded VLCCs. At the moment, vessels that load in LOOP export U.S. Gulf of Mexico crude, not LTO. LOOP started to export crude oil at a rate of about one (2 million barrel) cargo per month in February, but it is expected to ramp up to 300,000 b/d (at least 4 cargoes per month) in due course.
Production growth, pipeline expansion and terminal export availability all need to come together to make the process run smooth. However, since many of these projects are undertaken by different companies, chances are that there will be timing differences that will cause temporary bottlenecks. Many of these bottlenecks will be resolved through the pricing mechanism: temporary lack of export capacity will increase the price differentials between Midland, TX and Houston. Expanding price differentials will facilitate short- term remedies, such as crude-by rail.
