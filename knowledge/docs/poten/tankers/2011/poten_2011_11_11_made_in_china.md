## Made in China
It is difficult to deny the severe tanker oversupply situation. Worldwide oil demand has recovered globally surpassing its 2007 peak, despite languishing demand in mature economies. The resultant shift in trade flows and increased volumes on the water have boosted ton‐mile demand, but that alone has not been enough to lend support to freight rate levels. While China is certainly doing its part to support the tanker market from a demand perspective, the country also has the ability to significantly impact vessel supply.
A few months ago, rumors were circulating that national interests in China were on the brink of embarking on a major tanker newbuilding program. Reports of an incremental 80 VLCCs shocked the market, but the rumored order was quickly whittled down to 40 ships, then 20. While China's efforts to protect domestic industry are understandable, the news struck fear in the hearts of VLCC owners everywhere. Although some new shipbuilding contracts have been reported for this year, thankfully sizeable increases to the orderbook have yet to materialize.
Recent news suggests that China's shipbuilding objectives could be taking a much different turn. A recent Financial Times article points to the remarks made by Li Shenglin, China's transport minister, at the World Shipping China conference addressing the nation's commitment to moderating deliveries from the nation's shipyards. Amid the rising cost environment for shipowners, Mr. Li intimated that the additional vessel supply would only further bleed their bottom line. With 60% of the VLCC orderbook being constructed in China, adjustments to the amount of tonnage that ultimately delivers could help bolster freight rates in a real way.

It is easier for charterers to employ arbitrary age restrictions in a weak freight market, especially with a bounty of newly‐built tonnage. This trend is expected to continue if freight rates persist at these levels.

## Taking the Hit
If China is really committed to easing tanker supply, the real catalyst for change should come in the form of a decision to purchase older vessels with the intention to scrap them. Today there are roughly 70 VLCCs that are 15 years of age and older potentially facing commercial obsolescence. The removal of these ships could help bring the fleet count to levels seen in 2007 and 2008. Limited earning potential combined with the hurdle of a third special survey and dry docking costs could make vessels in the 12 - 14 year range candidates as well. [ See Poten's Tankers in World Markets Special Feature Prudent Fleet Pruning ] If the Chinese government were to purchase this portion of the fleet of 70 VLCCs, it would cost between $2 and $2.5 billion at current price levels of between $30 to $35 million. The subsequent scrapping of the vessels would recoup them approximately $1 billion, assuming current scrap price levels of $500/ldt, or $680 million taken at the 15‐year average scrap price of $309/ldt.
By improving the charter rates on the 43 Chinese‐owned VLCCs, the government could recover the full cost of the net investment of between $1.1 and $1.5 billion in a relatively short time period. If earnings went from today's spot levels of $15,000 per day back to the long‐term historical average of $41,000 per day, these 43 VLCCs would earn an incremental $1.1 million per day. It would take between two and three years to recover the difference between the cost of the vessels and the cash earned from scrapping.
With a number of public and private shipping companies domiciled in China, it would behoove the country to support a healthy freight environment. While it is expected that shipbuilding will continue to underpin the economy in an important way, efforts to stabilize worldwide shipping companies in the short‐term will ensure that crude oil continues to flow in a safe, reliable and profitable manner.
