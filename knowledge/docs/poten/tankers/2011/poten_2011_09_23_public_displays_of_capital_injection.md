## Public Displays of Capital Injection
When the market gets tough, the tough get going. There is no doubt that the public markets provide a tremendous resource for companies trying to raise capital, expand or execute a succession plan. Periods of positive momentum in the business cycle can sometimes supplant the immediate value that would be realized by a company simply due to a warm embrace by the public. Timing and 'buzz' can work wonders. However, companies that may otherwise wish to apply a long‐term strategy to navigating the troughs could be tempted into making hasty decisions at the behest of the public shareholder. Very often for public companies, perception becomes reality. The actions (or lack thereof) taken by companies in attempt to bolster their immediate standing can sometimes undermine the ultimate goal of long‐term sustainability.

## Staying the Course
Equity prices are often derived from expectations for cash flows to be generated by a company's assets, discounted to a present value. On that basis, the short‐sighted nature of the public investor is truly revealed. The real question becomes then, how does a company that operates in a long‐term investment cycle environment make it work? This hits home particularly for those companies operating in oil and gas, as well as shipping and infrastructure. It is no easy task to capitalize billion dollar investments, decades‐long project life spans and the intense reliance on physical assets on a very short‐term basis. The cyclical nature of these industries means that the timing of major capital expenditures is everything. A company may see a paradigm shift in the market environment while in the process of raising funds from shareholders for acquisitions; but buying in the wrong market or failing to do so in the right one could sabotage the firm's long‐term profitability. Selling a growth story is easy; having investors hang in regardless of micro‐cycle movements is not.
As companies look to tap public capital on the macro upswing, they should look to pursue private alternatives under opposite conditions. The weakening of valuations, cheap interest rates and the unavailability of credit has made publicly traded companies prime candidates for private money.
