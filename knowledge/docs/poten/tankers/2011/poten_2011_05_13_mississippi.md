## M‐I‐S‐S‐I‐S‐S‐I‐P‐P‐I
The Mississippi River is America's largest contiguous waterway, and a vital transportation way for the domestic petroleum supply chain. The mighty Mississippi flows 2,348 miles from Lake Itasca, Minnesota to empty south of New Orleans, draining water from 31 of America's 50 states into the Gulf of Mexico and tributary waterways. Ironically, the current torrent of water downstream has many foreseeing shortages in downstream supply of a different sort. The floods of late may spell O‐P‐P‐O‐R‐T‐U‐N‐I‐T‐Y for product tanker owners as markets on the US East Coast seek incremental gasoline imports to offset draws in domestic inventories.
According to the EIA, the US Gulf Coast contains about half of the nation's oil refining capacity, making it the breadbasket of US gasoline supply. According to a recent report by Morgan Stanley, eight refineries along the Mississippi River potentially affected by the recent floods together comprise 12% of US refining capacity. Although the risk of shutdown at these facilities is low, the logistical implications of flood waters and the psychological impact on US market has the potential to push gasoline prices further upward. Over the past five years, PADD 3 refiners have supplied on average over 25% of the gasoline barrels consumed in the US East Coast. ## Better Safe Than Sorry
As a result of the high crude oil prices seen thus far in 2011, US gasoline prices have been rising steadily since the beginning of the year. Additionally, speculative pressure, fuelled by geopolitical uncertainty and tighter supply of the light, sweet grades preferred for gasoline production has driven gasoline futures to remain steadily above spot prices since the end of the first quarter, as seen in the chart below.

## Spot
1 Month Forward After a brief dip at the end of April, future prices for gasoline jumped the beginning of this week upon the first reports of Mississippi flooding. Traders anticipated shortages from possible refinery closures and supply chain disruptions. Although flood damage seen thus far has been less severe than anticipated, there have been reports of charterers calling force majeure on shipments, and the high water levels have caused some operational headaches. Since the flood's beginnings, some companies have preemptively suspended operations at facilities along the Mississippi River until waters recede to safer levels.
Shipowners and charterers alike have been taking precautions to avoid risks associated with the flooding, the most prevalent thus far being the reduction in air draft, or distance between the water level and overhead obstructions and bridges. A number of shipments in the US Gulf have been cancelled or failed subjects as a result of the flooding, and the market has already seen increased demand for barging from inland refiners to meet domestic demand. Inquiry has been so high, in fact, that many owners of inland river barges have withdrawn tonnage from the spot market, using current tightness to leverage term deals.
The storm is far from over. The flood waters are expected to continue making their way down the Mississippi to reach their highest level in New Orleans on May 24 th , and the inland transportation market is likely to see disruptions until these waters finally empty to the Gulf of Mexico. As domestic prices climb, the expansion of arbitrage windows may lead to more incremental barrels sourced from the international market.
