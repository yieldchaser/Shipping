## No Need for Speed
As 2011 comes to a close, many are breathing a sigh of relief and taking some much needed time to relax. This past year's tanker market has shown us that even in the non‐stop world of global marine transportation there is virtue in slowing down. As low freight rates have been accompanied by high oil prices, tanker owners trading in the spot market have been begrudgingly applying the majority of their voyage revenues towards exorbitant bunker bills. Many independent owners have pushed for lower voyage speeds. Adjusting and optimizing speed has a material impact on earnings as the relationship between speeds and bunker consumption is not linear.
Dropping speeds by even one knot can have a significant impact on overall daily earnings at varying levels, as seen in the chart below.

## Time Charter Equivalent ($/Day)
Daily Bunker Costs In better markets, it was not uncommon for VLCCs to undergo voyages at speeds in the range of 15 or 16 knots (kt). High demand created much incentive for vessels to complete voyages and load their next cargo as quickly as possible. However the weak freight rate environment has removed this sense of urgency. Currently average market speeds are in the range of 13 to 14 knots. It is estimated that this two knot reduction in speed can allow VLCC owners to increase their daily earnings on the Arabian Gulf to Far East trade by approximately $5,400 per day at current market levels.
This boost to earnings is achievable because owners significantly reduce their fuel burn when they steam at slower speeds. Water resistance against the hull of a vessel increases as ships go faster, meaning more energy is required to propel ships at higher speeds. However, ships can generally achieve higher speeds in ballast without compromising overall efficiency because they have less mass to propel once their cargoes are discharged.

## A Friend in Need is a Friend Indeed
Slow steaming decreases overall bunker consumption but can also distribute voyage revenues over more operating days, potentially lowering the time charter equivalent. In high freight rate environments slow steaming can actually negatively impact daily time charter equivalent earnings, as shown in the chart below.

Owners of modern VLCCs trading from the Arabian Gulf to the Far East at speeds of 13.5 knots laden, 14.5 knots ballast would have grossed over $3,000 per day more year to date than if they had been steaming two knots higher.
Spot market vessels controlled by independent owners are more likely to slow steam than their counterparts on time charter to end users. Refineries generally only store enough crude oil to cover a few days of supply and depend on regular turnover to control inventory costs. Charterers foot the bills for bunkers supplied to their time chartered tonnage and are more likely to consider the schedules of their trading or refinery programs in adjusting vessel speeds than possible savings on fuel costs. Slower service speeds would either require these refineries to operate with higher inventory levels or charter more ships to fulfill their cargo requirements. For oil companies, the cost of either option is likely to outweigh any savings achievable through slow steaming.
While the variability of speeds may have minimal impact on vessels employed on refinery milk runs, the effects of slow steaming on the spot market should not be understated. While reduced fuel consumption is one obvious benefit to independent owners, the implied reduction of fleet capacity accompanying slower speeds is also likely to serve these spot market players in less transparent ways.
