## Follow The Yellow Brick Road - US Debt Limit Revision
Welcome to Oz! While it is nothing new, now more than ever it seems the United States government is making a sport of changing the rules of the game while it is in play. This week, the revision of the United States debt ceiling serves as the primary example. Perhaps it is simply the widespread news media coverage, but for once it seems that the American public is paying attention and even scrutinizing the decisions being made by our elected officials. One has to question the character of legislators deciding to revise rules and policies when it is convenient. In the case of the debt ceiling revision, changing the rules serves to delay an inevitable economic fate while saving face for those officials facing re‐election. The hyper‐dramatic tone employed by the Washington elected elite when cautioning the public about the cataclysmic result of not raising the debt limit was deceptive. Voicing fears that the sacred AAA credit rating would be compromised, the Department of the Treasury issued a paper that warned the following, "Failing to increase the debt limit would have catastrophic economic consequences. It would cause the government to default on its legal obligations - an unprecedented event in American history. That would precipitate another financial crisis and threaten the jobs and savings of everyday Americans - putting the United States right back in a deep economic hole, just as the country is recovering from the recession." 1 Instead, the actions taken will simply serve only to kick the proverbial can down the road. If history is any indication, the incremental spending that will result from an increase in the debt limit will only serve to make matters worse in the long run.

## The People Behind the Curtain
With increasingly wide‐spread politicization, it is interesting to note the reaction of crude oil prices and currencies to specific events over the past year and a half.
The publicity of the Greek debt crisis rightfully created concerns about the weakening Euro. The inflection point in this trend was seen in mid‐2010 and conversely raised concerns on the American side about the relative value of the US dollar to other currencies. Enter Quantitative Easing 2: by November 2010, the injection of cash into the US system was aimed at keeping interest rates low and stimulating lending. The effects of the last‐resort attempt to assuage fears over the US economy was short‐lived and by the beginning of this year, the commodities markets, specifically crude oil as shown below, reflected the true value of the US dollar as prices increased to the $100 per barrel level.

## CME margin hikes
$/EUR Brent Crude WTI Highlighted in the recent Opinion, Uncle Sam's Slight of Hand , the United States government stepped in once again in attempts to defuse concerns over rising commodity prices by releasing Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR) stateside. The spread between West Texas Intermediate (WTI), the land‐locked benchmark contract, and Brent crude oil, which is typically thought to be a better barometer of international prices, has persisted. The spread highlights the shortcomings of WTI as a real indicator of energy prices, even though it is widely heralded as such in America.
More to the point, crude oil prices for both grades rebounded quickly after the Strategic Petroleum Reserve release as oil companies snapped up the barrels ‐ as they should do. The net outcome of this charade reaffirms the muscle‐flexing preferences in Washington and moreover its sheer ignorance regarding the workings of the international petroleum market.

## Flying Monkeys
The confluence of the series of policy changes in recent history will hopefully raise awareness among the public about decisions being made in Washington. The most troublesome element continues to be that America's future is being dictated by a select group of power‐hungry individuals that have personal motives at heart (read: re‐election). While it is not altogether surprising that politicians are motivated by self‐interest, it is disheartening nonetheless.
The lack of accountability amongst decision‐makers plus the lack of leadership have raised questions about the soundness of policies that will have real impacts on economic recovery in the United States. The challenge for the rest of us will be to navigate and manage the shortsightedness of decisions against the long‐term impacts. With the inability to take at face value information or statistics published through a variety of government agencies, the challenge is no easy task. Fortunately, for better or worse, the commodities (including shipping!) markets serve to create greater transparency on the true state of affairs.
The jig is up; the people of America and the world have seen the man behind the curtain. It is time for the "wizards" in Washington to return to Kansas and restore the country to a more sustainable economic path. Now we know how Dorothy must have felt. Let's hope this is all just a bad dream.
