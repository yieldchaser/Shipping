## Keep the Gravy, Pass the Shale!
As the United States prepares to celebrate the bounty of the harvest this Thanksgiving holiday, some refiners in the mid Continent and US Gulf are celebrating an abundance of another sort. After years of declining domestic crude oil output, development of unconventional and offshore reserves and general advances in technology have led to dramatic increases in US crude oil production. The increased output from continental shale oil deposits along with increased Canadian imports to inland delivery centers have aggravated transportation bottlenecks in the Midwestern United States. This boost has also contributed to a persistent discount of inland grades to similar price marker crude oils. Refiners in a position to do so have enjoyed bumper margins processing these so called "advantage" crude oils, which have served to somewhat displace import barrels. However, the degree to which domestic crude oil barrels will stand in for long haul imports going forward will ultimately hinge upon the quality required by the nation's refiners and the pricing of domestic supplies relative to imported streams.
Over the past five years, US domestic crude oil production has increased as total consumer oil demand has decreased. Production in 2007 averaged just over 5 mbd, or about 25% of total US demand. 2011 year to date, domestic production has risen over 550 kbd to average 5.6 mbd, equating to almost 30% of total consumer petroleum demand of 18.9 mbd, as seen in the chart below.

## US Crude Oil Production
Total Demand Expansion of production in the Gulf of Mexico has accounted for the lion's share of this growth. Federal offshore production in the Gulf of Mexico has increased by 250 kbd since 2007 accounting for 45% of total domestic crude oil supply growth, however production over 2011 has averaged about 50 kbd lower than levels seen last year. US crude oil markets have also seen a boom in inland production from shale oil reserves. Technological advances have tripled output in North Dakota's Bakken shale over the past three years to some 450 kbd today. Shale oil production in the Eagle Ford in southeast Texas has also risen dramatically over the course of this year, rising to 272 kbd in June from 70 kbd in April.

## Are We There Yet?
Production from these shale oil reserves is anticipated to rise further over the coming years. Pipeline operators are predicting Bakken crude oil production to rise to 600 kbd in 2012. Existing pipeline networks are overwhelmed by the surge in output. Currently, more than 100 kbd of crude is now moved out of the Bakken by rail. In the Eagle Ford shale area, infrastructure to ship more than 1.5 mbd has already been proposed, with two pipelines totaling 650 kbd into the Houston area set to start up by the middle of 2012.
Increased domestic production from inland sources and higher volumes of imported Canadian crude oil have aggravated a long standing transportation gridlock at Cushing, Oklahoma. Inventories at Cushing reached historically high levels in April of 2011, and have averaged over 36 million barrels year‐to‐date relative to the five‐year average of 25 million barrels. As inventories at Cushing ramp up, so does the discount of the US benchmark crude relative to its European counterpart. Today, the discount of WTI to Brent has diminished to less than $10 per barrel. This turn of circumstances was spurred by news last week that an existing import pipeline (Seaway) would be reversed providing an outlet for the captive crude oil on the U.S. Gulf.

## Absorbing the Discount
The comparatively attractive pricing of domestic crude oil streams is likely in part a factor of their relative high transportation cost. In absence of pipeline infrastructure to easily move crude oil to its markets, landlocked oil must be transported by truck or rail. Both have limitations in their achievable economies of scale. As a result, the dollar per barrel transportation costs for these options tend to be much higher than transport by tanker or barge. Transport of crude oil by rail can average well over $10 per barrel. For comparison, at current rate levels the cost of shipping a barrel of crude oil from West Africa to the US Gulf on a Suezmax is currently assessed at less than $1.90 per barrel.
The discount of inland streams has resulted in bumper margins for refiners able to economically source and transport them. Demand for these discounted barrels, combined with a decreased appetite for petroleum from American consumers, has already begun to displace some import barrels, particularly from West African suppliers, as seen in the chart below.

Expansion of pipeline networks and trade by rail and barge is likely to increase demand for these barrels by relieving bottlenecks and expanding their markets. As transportation for these mid‐continental crude oils becomes more efficient, the pricing gap relative to internationally traded grades will lessen.
Closure of the gap between WTI and seaborne streams may curb the appetite for so called advantage crudes, particularly if other crude oil grades produce more desirable product slates. This could mitigate the effect of domestic crude oil supply increases on the erosion of foreign flagged tanker demand to refining centers along the US coast.
