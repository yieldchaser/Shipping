## How the West Was Won
This past week, an oil leak originating from a pump station serving Alaska's North Slope resulted in a shut‐down of production of nearly 600,000 barrels per day. This amount, equaling nearly 10% of total United States output, can have a dramatic impact on the worldwide energy complex, particularly with crude oil prices trading just below three year highs. However, beyond the resulting energy price movements, the leak and subsequent shutdown should shift attention to the effects such events have on tanker markets. More specifically, in this case, it raises awareness of the fragile and morphing U.S. West Coast (USWC) market.
Frequent supply disruptions around the world (think Nigeria/Venezuela) are an occurrence all tanker market participants are familiar with. Short‐term events and supply disruptions often create arbitrage opportunities involving alternative trades. For instance, Alaskan North Slope (ANS) crude disruptions have historically resulted in an increase in cargoes originating in the Middle East (given similar crude quality) for discharge on the USWC. Interestingly, this latest potentially 'market‐changing' event has been anything but. However this is not to say the USWC has been a boring place of late. A chart below demonstrating shifting trends in USWC trade activity bear testament to a rapidly‐evolving market.

## Size Certainly Matters
With full‐year 2010 data not yet available, a summary showing average projected volumes of 2010's five largest crude exporters to the USWC and their five‐year export history demonstrates a marked increase in the U.S. importation of Russian Espo Crude from Kozmino. The increase in the Kozmino trade has been mirrored by a decrease in the Middle East trade involving Saudi Arabian and Iraqi crude. In addition to the Kozmino trade, cargoes loading West Coast Canada (Vancouver) are increasingly finding their way to USWC discharge locations. Not surprisingly, this shifting trend has noticeably impacted tanker markets, and in particularly Aframax and VLCC markets in very different ways. A graph showing a five year fixture percentage breakdown leaves little doubt as to which way the market is trending.

## % of Reported Spot Fixture Volume
Panamax fixture activity discharging USWC has proven to be remarkably consistent over the last five years relative to the other tanker classes. The noticeable change occurring in 2010 came in the Aframax, Suezmax, and VLCC classes which all saw a somewhat dramatic change in percentages of overall fixture market share versus the year prior. VLCC fixture percentages declined nearly 15% relative to the other tanker classes and saw its lowest share of fixture activity in the last five years in 2010. Conversely, Suezmax fixture activity increased 5% and most impressively Aframax fixture activity increased 10% relative to the other tanker sectors from 2009. For Aframax fixtures relative to the sector, the jump represents a shocking 100% increase in fixture activity from it's own previous year's activity levels. These cargoes are undoubtedly displacing the traditional VLCC business originating in the Arabian Gulf.

## Far East Movement
United States West Coast markets will continue to evolve in the face of expanding eastern market economies, and new supply sources coming on line. What this may mean for tanker owners over the long term is difficult to predict. In the short term, however, Russian Espo crude and steadily increasing Canadian export stems paint a robust picture for the Aframax sector at the expense of the VLCC market. Whether current trends impacting the USWC market in 2010 are sustainable is yet to be seen, but regardless of which direction markets go things will undoubtedly remain... wild wild.
