## Syria of Events
The violent response of Syrian leader Bashar al Assad to the ongoing pro‐democracy protests since March of this year has been met with horror and condemnation from the international community. In an unprecedented move, the nations of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) recalled their ambassadors from Syria, showing clearly the regional lack of support for the Assad regime. The United States has prohibited import of any Syrian petroleum and has forbidden US citizens and businesses from involvement in Syria's energy sector. The European Union followed suit, and has now also banned imports of Syrian crude oil in attempt to up the pressure on the Assad government. In the short term, or at least until Syria finds alternate markets for their exports, the sanctions are likely to hit Syria right in the purse, as its economy derives about a quarter of revenues from oil. In the longer term, the combination of political upheaval and economic isolation are likely to prolong the timeline of existing and proposed regional infrastructure projects, including those aimed at increasing export volumes from neighboring Iraq.

## UK Continent
USA Syria itself is not a major crude oil exporter. Syrian crude oil production peaked in 1996 at 580 kbd and has declined over the past fifteen years. As would be expected, reported crude oil tanker fixture volumes for vessels 30,000 dwt and greater have decreased along with output. The notable exception was from 2001 to early 2003, where liftings were about 45% higher than the 1995‐2000 average. This jump was attributable to the reactivation of a pipeline linking Iraqi Kirkuk oil field with Syria's Mediterranean port of Baniyas. The pipeline was first shut down in the 1970's after political relations between Iraq and Syria spoiled. Iraq reopened the pipeline in 2000 to bypass sanctions, shipping about 200 kbd of Kirkuk crude oil to Syrian refineries, which used the Iraqi stream and sold domestic crude oil to foreign markets. The pipeline remained in operation until early 2003, when it was damaged by US airstrikes, after which Syrian export volumes continued to wane.
In 2010, it was estimated that Syria produced about 387 kbd of crude oil and lease condensate, of which 109 kbd, or 28%, was sold to export markets. US imports from Syria have averaged less than 15 kbd over 2011 year to date, making the world's largest crude oil importer a marginal market for Syrian volumes. The vast majority of Syrian crude oil exports are processed by refiners in OECD European countries, particularly Germany, Italy, and France, and the Netherlands. According to the US Energy Intelligence Administration (IEA), heavy oil accounts for about 60% of Syrian production and about three‐fourths of the country's oil exports.

## Pipe Dreams
Syria's heavy crude is not as abundant or as painful to replace as Libya's lost light sweet barrels, but in the current crude oil supply (and price) environment, any little bit hurts. Additionally, both sanctions and political upheaval are likely to stand in the way of planned infrastructure projects that would boost export capacity from neighboring crude oil producers through Syria's Mediterranean seaports. ## Proposed pipeline
The aforementioned pipeline connecting the northern Iraqi oil region of Kirkuk to the Syrian Mediterranean port of Baniyas has been under repair since 2007. Ongoing bottlenecks at export terminals have been the main impediment to increasing the flow of Iraqi oil to market, and Syria is central in ongoing plans to expand regional oil and gas pipeline networks. The market has seen in the past that fights among neighbors in the Middle East can lead to disruption of transit of crude oil volumes, whether as a result of policy or insurgency. As Iraq finds ways to bypass the Mediterranean transit route and significantly increase export volumes via southern seaports, this could increase ton mile demand for larger crude carriers, namely VLCCs, as big ships lift these incremental volumes to markets West of Suez. However, this boost will likely come at the expense of demand for Aframax tankers in the Mediterranean.
The unrest in Syria and corresponding sanctions are not as catastrophic to immediate supply as what the market witnessed with Libya. Syria's export barrels are relatively few, and from a quality standpoint, much more replaceable. However, continued unrest in Syria could be potentially devastating for regional security in multiple ways. Fears of escalation into regional war are not unreasonable, nor are concerns about continued attacks on existing energy transportation infrastructure. The turmoil seen this year is not a positive sign for the ability of Syria to actually execute proposed infrastructure projects with its neighbors and increase the flow of landlocked oil to marine terminals. Continued delay of these initiatives could seriously limit the ability to get additional barrels to market over the coming years.
