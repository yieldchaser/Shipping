## Gas Pains
Gasoline prices continue to climb despite ample supplies and adequate domestic stockpiles. The apparent disconnect leaves the public and industry members scratching their pocketbooks. Last week's Tanker Opinion discussed the rising trend of product exports from domestic refineries and its implications for the clean product tanker markets. The notion that large stockpiles exist while refiners operate at healthy utilization against steady demand will frustrate anyone attempting to apply Economics 101 to derive the current price. Although a seemingly contradictory confluence of market forces, clean product tanker owners are not complaining.
The chart below displays the average domestic refining "3‐2‐1" crack spread (or proxy of total "profit" yielded from buying three barrels of oil and selling two barrels of gasoline and one distillate in a future market). Refiners are enjoying the most profitable times seen since the onset of the recession. Higher gasoline and distillate prices create incentives for higher production, but the duration of the phenomenon is the question at hand.

## Questioning the Economics
The chart below shows US refinery utilization over the last year as well as the relative activity of PADD 1, PADD 2, and PADD 3 refiners. As discussed last week and in previous opinions, the price divergence between WTI and Brent crude oil benchmark contracts is creating a very favorable margin environment for PADD 2 refiners with access to the more cheaply‐priced WTI benchmark‐related crude oils and is evidenced through higher utilization figures. Some industry stalwarts have even gone so far as to suggest that the future of US refining will be in domestic exports. Recent gasoline exports to Mexico could indicate that this developing export trend to Latin America may remain sustained. This sentiment would seem to suggest that international markets are competing ever‐more with the US consumer for domestically‐ produced refined products, helping to lend support to prices.

## Spelling Relief
The question of high domestic pricing remains in spite of this set of extraordinary circumstances that would seemingly result in negative price movements or a better reconciliation of supply imbalances within the domestic US market. Expect the quality and price arbitrage to continue to pull product out of PADD III and sustain or even increase the need for incremental imports on the East Coast. As previous opinions have suggested , the prospect of quantitative easing‐induced inflation should not be dismissed as a possible culprit driving gasoline and refined product prices higher. Should the current trends prove sustained it will mean continued frustration for consumers already paying for it at the pump.
