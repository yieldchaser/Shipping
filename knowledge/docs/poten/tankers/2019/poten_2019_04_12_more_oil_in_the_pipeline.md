## Developments in West Texas Pipeline Capacity
As anybody who follows the oil industry knows, U.S. crude oil production has grown rapidly in recent years. According to the EIA, the U.S. currently produces around 11.9 Million barrels per day (Mb/d); 5.4 Mb/d more than in 2012. U.S. oil production growth over these six years was similar to the total production of Canada and more than the total production of any OPEC country except for Saudi Arabia. The production growth has been so fast that the pipeline infrastructure had trouble keeping up.
The production growth is largely due to production of Light Tight Oil (LTO) (often called shale oil) from fields in Western Texas and New Mexico. Production in this area (Permian and Eagle Ford) increased from 1.6 Mb/d in 2012 to 6.0 Mb/d in February 2019. Since the beginning of 2017, production has grown by an average of 100,000 b/d every month. The characteristics of shale oil production are very different from traditional production as lead times for production to come on- stream are much shorter. As a result, pipeline companies, which supply the take-away capacity, have had trouble to keep up with the rapid growth. Just like in the tanker industry, it takes time to add new capacity; the planning, permitting, and building of a new pipeline can take years. The process can sometimes be accelerated by building a new pipeline next to an existing one or by converting an existing pipeline that was designed for a different product such as the conversion of the Seminole NGL pipeline to crude oil service. The capacity of an existing pipeline can sometimes be expanded by adding pumping capacity and/or using additives to improve the flow rate.
A shortage of take-away capacity is reflected in widening price spreads between different locations. Crude that cannot fit into a pipeline will be transported in other ways, which are typically more expensive. The price graph shows crude oil prices in Midland (Permian) and at the storage terminal in Cushing. Prices remained very close until early 2018 when shortages in pipeline capacity caused the spread to widen. At the end of August, the spread increased to almost $18/bbl as oil needed to be moved by truck and rail.
Since then, several new pipelines and expansions have come on stream, often ahead of schedule: • The Sunrise expansion, added about 300-350Kb/d in November and will eventually be able to carry 500Kb/d (to Cushing) • The Bridgetex pipeline expanded from 400 to 440Kb/d (Houston) • The Midland-Sealy pipeline added 35Kb/d (Houston) • Midland to Echo II (Ex-Seminole) pipeline conversion adds 200Kb/d in Q1 2019 (Houston)

## Chart: Planned Permian/Eagle Ford Oil Pipeline Capacity vs LTO Production
Combined, these pipelines added 625 Kb/d in take-away capacity, enough to eliminate the shortfall. Several large pipeline projects, adding up to more than 2Mb/d, are scheduled to come online before the end of 2020 and additional projects for completion thereafter are announced. During an analyst conference earlier this week, a major pipeline operator anticipated that the pipeline expansion projects will exceed production growth in the coming years. The above chart by the IEA shows also that pipeline capacity growth is expected to exceed forecasted production increases. A lower discount for Permian crude will support continued production growth.
This means that the pipeline bottleneck that limited crude oil access to markets has been largely eliminated. As U.S. refineries have limited use for additional light sweet crude, exports will likely increase further. To prevent port infrastructure from becoming the next bottleneck, there are several VLCC loading facility projects under consideration, but it is unclear what the timing for completion is. For the time being, reverse lightering will continue to be the predominant way to load VLCCs and rising production should benefit all crude oil tanker classes.
