Unfortunately, that has not been the case.

## This year is going to be different, or not?
After the tanker freight market recovered in the fourth quarter of 2018, most analysts (including ourselves) expected that the beginning of 2019 would show better earnings than the comparable period of last year. Unfortunately, that has not been the case. While the start of the year was encouraging, following a strong 4 th quarter of 2018, crude tanker rates have weakened in recent weeks to levels last seen one year ago. As a result, tanker rates seem to be back to square one. What are the main reasons that tanker rates have not (yet) shown the improvements that everybody expected?
The OPEC cuts that were implemented in early 2019 have obviously had a negative impact on the tanker market. OPEC production in the first quarter of 2019 averaged 30.58 million barrels per day (mb/d). This is 1.42 mb/d (4.5%) less than the same period of 2018, which averaged 32 mb/d. A comparison with the fourth quarter of 2018 shows an even bigger difference: 30.58 mb/d versus 32.80 mb/d; a reduction of 2.32 mb/d or 6.8%. OPEC's March 2019 production of 30.13 mb/d was the lowest monthly average since February 2015 (30.10 mb/d). Saudi Arabia was the most significant contributor to the changes in production. In 2018, the Kingdom increased output from a low of 9.92 mb/d in April to a high of 11.06 mb/d in November. This was partially meant to compensate for the reduction in Iranian output because of the reinstatement of U.S. sanctions against the regime in Tehran. Iran's production fell by more than 1.0 mb/d (from 3.83 mb/d in June to 2.80 mb/d in December). Another "loser" during this timeframe was Venezuela. According to the IEA, this oil rich country in Latin America started the year with production at 1.61 mb/d and ended 2018 at 1.29 mb/d. Continued political and economic turmoil has reduced Venezuelan output to well below 900 kb/d currently.
Global oil demand increased by 1.1% in Q1 2019 versus one year ago. Since OPEC output declined by 1.6 mb/d, other producers must have stepped up and/or global stocks declined significantly. It appears that both developments played a role; stocks decreased and non-OPEC production, particularly in the U.S., increased.
To determine the impact on the tanker market, we need to look at the changes in ton-mile demand. Comparing Q1 2019 with both Q1 2018 and Q4 2018 shows an interesting picture. Global ton-mile demand for crude oil and dirty petroleum products increased from 875 billion ton-miles per month in Q1 2018 to 920 billion ton-miles per month in Q4, a 5.1% increase. All major tanker segments contributed to the increase: VLCCs (+4%), Suezmaxes (+7%) and Aframaxes (+7%). Mainly because of the OPEC production cuts, total tanker ton-mile demand in Q1 2019 declined by 5.6% relative to Q4 2018. As a result, global ton-mile demand in Q1 2019 fell below ton-mile demand in the same period one year ago. However, unlike the increase in demand, the decline in tanker demand was not evenly distributed. As you can see in the chart above, Suezmaxes (-13.2%) and Aframaxes (-12.1%) bore the brunt of the decline. VLCC ton- miles (-1.6%) held up much better because of the increase in U.S. crude oil exports. These long-haul voyages - mostly destined for Asia - benefited VLCCs more than Suezmaxes or Aframaxes.
However, while ton-mile demand for VLCCs declined much less than demand for Suezmaxes and Aframaxes, the impact on rates was the same. VLCC earnings suffered just as much as Suezmax and Aframax earnings. There are two possible explanations for that apparent paradox: vessel supply and market psychology. In Q1 2019, 17 VLCCs were delivered and none scrapped. For Suezmaxes the numbers were 18 deliveries and 2 removals and for Aframaxes 18 deliveries and 7 removals. These numbers do not explain why VLCC rates suffered just as much as the medium sized tankers, despite a much smaller decline in ton-mile demand. That leaves market psychology as the most likely explanation. Tanker rates are driven as much by psychology as by underlying supply/demand balances. While VLCCs showed better fundamentals, rates were pulled down by the poor overall market sentiment.
