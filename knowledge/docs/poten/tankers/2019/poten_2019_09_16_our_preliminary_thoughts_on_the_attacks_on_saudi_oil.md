## Our Preliminary Thoughts on the Attacks on Saudi Oil Facilities
Oil Prices and Tanker Rates are significantly higher. Berthing has resumed in Ras Tanura, after being interrupted on Saturday Two Saudi oil facilities were attacked on Saturday, September 14. These facilities process 5.7 million barrels per day, more than half of Saudi Arabia's output and more than 5% of global crude oil production. It is not clear who carried out the attacks, although Yemen's Houthi rebels have claimed responsibility. How might this impact the oil and tanker markets?
1. Oil and bunker prices • Oil prices are up significantly. This morning, Brent rose 10% to $66.37 per barrel. U.S. oil futures (WTI) rose 9.7% to $60.15 per barrel. The increase in oil prices reflects the significant uncertainty about the impact of the attacks on current and future oil supply. At this moment, there is no clarity about the level and duration of the disruption • Bunker prices increased in line with oil prices. Not surprisingly, IFO380 jumped by $30-$50 per MT as a direct result of the increase in crude oil prices 2. Tanker rates and loading activity • Tanker rates have increased significantly. VLCC rates from the Arabian Gulf to Asia are at least WS10 points higher than Friday and rising. Several fixtures have been done over the weekend and today at increasing rates. Most of this increase reflects the higher bunker prices, but there is also an element of risk management. Charterers are less willing to play the "wait-and-see" game and owners resist "last done" under these circumstances • It is unclear how many vessels are loading in Saudi Arabia, but a review of AIS data seems to indicate that loading activity has resumed at Ras Tanura, the country's main export terminal. No vessels were berthing at the terminal on Saturday (the day of the attack), but activity resumed on Sunday, with several tankers mooring at Ras Tanura. However, we have not yet been able to confirm that loading has resumed • After a slight delay, Saudi Aramco's October nominations were released this afternoon. While a reason of the delay was not given, it seems logical that the Saudi's needed to take stock of their oil supply situation before they committed to shipments 3. Oil Supply • Will there be enough oil? Yes, in the short-term. There are ample oil stocks in the main consuming areas (U.S., China and Europe) as well as in some of the producing countries (like Saudi Arabia). Strategic Petroleum Reserves can be tapped in the short term and could balance the markets for several months. Typically, SPR releases happen only after some time, once it becomes clear that the disruption will take some time to be resolved and after prices have increased substantially (+20-25%). The last global release of oil stocks coordinated by the International Energy Agency was in 2011 after the collapse in Libyan oil production • Saudi Arabia has significant stocks as well and they have indicated that they will try to compensate for the outages by exporting more from storage facilities. • If the Saudi oil facilities are out for an extended period (several months or longer), it will me more of a challenge to keep oil markets well supplied. Some OPEC+ producers can increase production. Kuwait and the UAE have spare capacity and so does Russia. However, it will take several weeks for these countries to ramp up production • The quality of the affected crude stream (Ghawar and Khurais) is primarily medium sour. Russia, Kuwait and the UAE produce similar quality crudes. The U.S. SPR also contains crudes of similar API gravity and Sulphur content. 4. What's next? • The key factor is "who is responsible?" Once the authorities in Saudi Arabia and the U.S. are relatively certain about who is behind these attacks, how will they respond? If it is deemed to be Iran, will there be a military response? • How long will it take Saudi Aramco to bring back the Abqaiq processing center and the Khurais oil field. Will it take days or weeks (or even longer)? • A long-term outage of the Saudi facilities will strain the oil markets going into the winter market and already facing the transition to low sulphur bunker fuels due to IMO2020.
