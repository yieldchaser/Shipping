## Relatively smooth sailing as new rules come into force
We are still in the early innings of the new IMO rules and it is much too early to draw any definitive conclusions about the long-term implications of IMO 2020 on the shipping and the oil industry. However, as most of us have waited for this day to arrive with some sense of anticipation (or trepidation) we thought it made sense to scan the markets to take a preliminary look at what the post-IMO 2020 landscape looks like. What is happening to bunker prices, the availability and quality of the various fuel types and what is the impact on tanker rates. What problems and challenges - if any - are people reporting?
Overall, availability of the various compliant fuels does not seem to be a major problem. This is not a surprise given the fact that the oil and shipping markets have had ample time to prepare for the implementation of IMO 2020. Oil companies, bunker providers and other market participants have built inventories to be able to handle a surge in demand for compliant fuels. Despite this there have been some reports of tight availability of Very Low Sulphur Fuel Oil (VLSFO) in a few ports. Gibraltar and Singapore in particular have been mentioned in media reports with vessels reportedly having to wait to bunker compliant fuels.
The availability of High Sulphur Fuel Oil (HSFO) is much less discussed and could become more of an issue going forward. Only vessels with scrubbers, currently representing an estimated 12% of the fleet can still use this fuel and as a result, demand for HSFO has fallen off a cliff around the world. For marine fuel suppliers, it may not make sense to dedicate storage tanks and bunker barges to HSFO in smaller bunker ports if there is no consistent demand. Shipping companies that own vessels with scrubbers will need to do more bunker planning and, in some case, may deviate their vessel from its optimal route in search of high sulphur bunkers.
Owners that opted to install scrubbers on their vessels seem to have been vindicated in their decision. On the benchmark TD3 (Arabian Gulf - China) route, the Time Charter Equivalent (TCE) for a VLCC with a scrubber is $24,000/day higher than for a vessel that burns VLSFO. For a Suezmax tanker trading from West Africa to Rotterdam, the difference is $14,000/day. On shorter haul voyages, which are more typical for Aframax tankers, the differences are much smaller. The TCE premium for an Aframax with a scrubber trading in the Caribbean is "only" $7,000/day. When vessels trade a majority (or all) of their time in ECA zones, (requiring 0.1% sulphur fuels), the scrubber premium disappears altogether.
How about fuel quality, bunkers being off-spec or batches being incompatible? Again, reported problems have been limited so far. Many owners have been quite diligent in sampling/testing the fuels beforehand and this seems to have avoided large-scale problems. However, we are only 10 days in and problems with fuel quality may take a lot longer to surface.
If long-term availability of HSFO is maintained and the economics continue to make sense, we expect more scrubber installations on larger vessels employed on long-haul trades. For larger product tankers (LR1's/LR2's) on the long-haul AG-East trades, scrubbers also appear to be a good investment. Scrubbers are not without problems and challenges, but unless there are significant regulatory changes, we expect scrubbers are here to stay. Over time, they will get better, become more reliable and cheaper to operate.
What can we expect going forward? We think that the availability of compliant fuels will increase over time. This will probably reduce the price differential between VLSFO and HSFO in the main bunkering hubs worldwide, although regional supply/demand discrepancies will remain. Fuel quality issues will be resolved and, as the bunker supply chain gets more streamlined, the market will become more relaxed about IMO 2020.
