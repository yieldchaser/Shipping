## OPEC continues to have an outsized impact on crude tankers
OPEC production has always been a significant driver of tanker demand and rate volatility. Most of its membership (and oil production) resides in the Middle East (75% in March 2019) and most of the demand for its product comes from Asia. The infrastructure on both sides of this trade is highly efficient and because of their economies of scale, VLCCs dominate the Middle East to Asia trade. In the first quarter of this year, 85% of the seaborne crude oil trade on this route was handled by VLCCs, with only 11% done on Suezmaxes and 4% on Aframax tankers. Unfortunately, the Middle East is not only the largest crude oil producing region in the world, it is also the dominant source of geopolitical tension as well as the self-appointed market arbiter that is responsible for keeping the global crude oil markets balanced. The combined result of all these factors is that the oil markets in general and OPEC's production in particular have become rather unpredictable. This is having an outsized impact on the tanker market.
The chart on the top shows OPEC's crude oil production over the last five years. It clearly illustrates the volatility of the cartel's output. OPEC's crude oil production (excl. NGLs) was at a multi-year low of 31.64 million barrels per day (mb/d) in April 2018. In May 2018, President Trump decided that the U.S. was withdrawing from the Iran Nuclear Deal. Following this announcement, OPEC producers started to increase output in anticipation of the reinstatement of sanctions on Iran by the U.S. When waivers were issued, the oil market became oversupplied and OPEC cut back again. Since crude oil production and tanker employment are clearly correlated, all these changes in OPEC production had a direct impact on VLCC rates in the second half of 2018 and into 2019.
The sanctions on Iran were not the only sources of market uncertainty and volatility. Venezuela and - to a lesser extent - Libya contributed as well. Venezuela's crude oil output and exports have been declining for years, primarily as a result of underinvestment, poor maintenance, parts shortages and general mismanagement of the oil industry by the Venezuelan regime. In 2014, Venezuela's production still averaged 2.46 mb/d. By the end of 2017, the official number was down 35% to 1.61 mb/d. Unfortunately, the declines have accelerated since then. Political turmoil and tough new U.S. sanctions have cut Venezuela's output in half again and the latest numbers show output of less than 800,000 b/d. Since a significant portion of Venezuela's production was exported to long-haul destinations in China and India, the rapid decline in output had a detrimental impact on the VLCC market. Unlike the situation in Iran, Venezuela's problems are more structural, so even if the political crisis is resolved and sanctions are lifted, it will take many years for Venezuela to turn its oil industry around and increase its output and exports.
The problems in Libya also seem to be far from a resolution and oil production and exports are quite volatile and unpredictable. Over the last 12 months, Libyan production has ranged from a low of 670,000 b/d in July 2018 to a high of 1.15 mb/d in October 2018. Where exports will go in the coming months is anybody's guess as it appears that the country is again on the verge of a civil war. Developments in Libya will have the biggest direct impact on the Aframax market in the Mediterranean.
In addition to the production issues and export challenges of Iran, Venezuela (both founding members of OPEC in 1960) and Libya (a member since 1962), OPEC is also facing regular membership changes: Indonesia has been in and out of the club several times, while more recently Qatar left the organization. Previously, Ecuador and Gabon left and rejoined. In terms of additions, Angola joined in 2007, Equatorial Guinea in 2017 and the Republic of Congo in 2018.
Who knows what's next for OPEC, but it is clear that the producer's club still plays a dominant role in the oil market. Tanker owners and charterers ignore OPEC at their own risk.
