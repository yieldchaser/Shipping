## Hurricane season in a changing U.S. oil market
Last weekend we saw Barry, the first major storm of the U.S. hurricane season, make landfall close to New Orleans. The official U.S. hurricane season runs from June 1 st to November 30 th , but hurricanes can occur outside of this window. For 2019, the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) predicts a near-normal hurricane season with 9-15 named storms of which 4-8 could become hurricanes, including 2-4 major hurricanes. This week we look at the impact of hurricanes on the tanker and energy markets.
The impact for the oil industry and for shipping depends not just on the number and intensity of the hurricanes but also on the location. The U.S. Gulf area has a high concentration of energy production and processing assets: Offshore oil platforms and related pipeline infrastructure and refineries and port infrastructure. Dependent on the path of the storms, potentially any of these types of assets can be affected. Even though Barry did not cause significant damage to energy assets, the storm still affected the oil industry: According to Government reports, about 70% of the offshore oil production, or about 1.3 Million barrel per day (Mb/d) was shut down as the storm approached and at least one refinery also shut down in anticipation of the storm, several others reduced staff, and the port of New Orleans was closed temporarily for incoming traffic. In this case, the impact of the storm was less severe than initially feared and operations returned to normal fairly quickly.
The impact of hurricanes on the tanker market varies depending on the circumstances. Hurricanes can cause wind and/or flooding related damage, either from heavy rainfall that cannot drain fast enough or due to sea water that is pushed inland by the wind. If refineries are off line, crude oil import demand declines while product imports increase and/or exports decrease. For example, in the aftermath of Katrina and Rita (Aug/Sep 2005) offshore production took months to recover as 111 production platforms were damaged, of which 52 seriously. Additionally, 5.6 Mb/d of refinery capacity was shut down after Hurricane Rita and it took several weeks to restart some of the lost capacity. To offset lost production, the IEA and the U.S. coordinated to release crude oil from strategic reserves in the U.S. and petroleum products from other IEA member states.
Since these hurricanes hit, the situation has changed considerably. The U.S. dependence on crude oil imports has declined substantially. When Harvey hit in August 2017, a significant portion of Eagle Ford production was temporarily affected, but the Permian production was unharmed. In preparation for the storm, midstream companies had moved oil out of storage tanks to create room for additional production. Crude oil was diverted to Cushing as Houston and Corpus Christi were not able to handle the crude inflows due to flooding, port outages and power outages. As a result of the refinery outages, petroleum product prices increased significantly and product tanker rates (TC2) quickly increased from $3,000/day to $24,000/day for a couple of days. Crude tanker rates (TD9) also improved from close to zero to about $20,000/day.
Now that the U.S. is a major crude oil exporter, these exports could also be affected if a major export port, such as Houston or Corpus Christi is closed. After Hurricane Harvey hit the US Gulf in August 2017, the port of Corpus Christi remained closed for 6 days and Houston was closed for a similar period and opened initially with draft restrictions. In August 2017, before Harvey hit, the U.S. Gulf region was exporting about 600 Kb/d of crude oil, compared to 2,643 Kb/d in April 2019. The importance of U.S. exports for the global oil market could mean that the impact of a major hurricane is felt much further away than in the past. Currently, there is no shortage of crude oil and spare production capacity in the world, but removing a significant part of U.S. exports from the market will still have a major short term impact.
