## Contaminated Ural crudes disrupt Russian exports
Last week, reports surfaced that Russian Urals crude flowing through the Druzhba pipeline was contaminated with organic chloride. Refiners in Germany, Poland, Belarus, Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia all stopped accepting supplies of Russian Urals crude. Official numbers have not been released, but analysts estimate that at least 35 million barrels of oil have been contaminated. Landlocked, domestic refiners In Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic have no alternative source of crude oil supply and are tapping into their countries' strategic petroleum reserves as a result. In other countries, refiners are scrambling for replacement barrels. Urals is a medium-heavy crude with a relatively high sulphur content. Sanctions on Iran and Venezuela as well as lower output from Mexico has already reduced worldwide availability of these types of crudes and oil prices have traded up as a result. Non-contaminated cargoes of Urals exported from the port of Primorsk in the Baltic Sea are in short supply and as a result command a significant premium. In this Weekly Tanker Opinion, we will discuss the potential impact of these events on the tanker market.
According to Russian officials, the contamination happened in the Volga region of Samara, which is about 1,000 kilometers east of Moscow. The Druzhba system is one of the biggest pipeline networks in the world. Among other things, it supplies about one million barrels per day of Urals crude to 10 European refineries, some of which run exclusively on this crude. Urals crude also flows via this pipeline network to the export terminal of Ust-Luga in the Baltic Sea. Over the last 5 years, seaborne exports from Ust-Luga have gradually increased from an average of 530,000 b/d in 2014 to 770,000 b/d in 2017. The export barrels from Ust-Luga mostly stay in Europe. The Netherlands, Sweden, Lithuania, Poland, Germany and France are the largest receivers. In 2018 and 2019 YTD, volumes have moderated somewhat, as Russia participates in the OPEC+ production cuts. Before the contamination problems came to light, an estimated 7-10 Aframax cargoes were already loaded from the port of Ust-Luga and these cargoes are currently being marketed at a steep discount. At the moment, buyers are staying away from Ust-Luga cargoes. One way to handle the contaminated cargoes is for traders or refiners to buy this heavily discounted crude and put it in storage, where it can be diluted with uncontaminated oil and eventually processed. How feasible this is will depend on the level of contamination, i.e. how much organic chloride is in the crude. This will be different for each cargo.
Organic chloride is a chemical compound that is generally not present naturally in the oil but can end up in the crude stream when additives, cleaning solutions or chemicals for oil recovery are used. At high temperatures, chlorides can form hydrochloric acid, which can readily corrode equipment throughout the refining process. As a result, refineries typically do not accept crude oil that contains more than 5 parts per million (ppm) of organic chloride. Reportedly, Transneft (the Russian pipeline monopoly) allows no more than 10 ppm in the crude flowing to their system. However, the levels of organic chloride in the contaminated crude have been between 80 ppm and 330 ppm.
Russian officials say that Ust-Luga should receive "clean oil" by May 5-7 and this should help restart seaborne exports from this Baltic port fairly quickly. Cleaning out the Druzhba pipeline is more complicated and will take longer to resolve, especially since Poland and Germany do not have sufficient storage space to keep the oil until it can be diluted.
The implications for the tanker market are hard to gage at this point. Since the Atlantic Aframax market was well supplied, the impact on rates has been fairly muted. Tanker rates have increased somewhat, but there has not been a rate spike. If the situation lasts longer than expected, this situation may change. Refiners will run out of inventory and need to source alternative (longer haul) supplies or (in the case of landlocked facilities) will start to cut runs, leading to more clean product imports and higher product tanker rates.
