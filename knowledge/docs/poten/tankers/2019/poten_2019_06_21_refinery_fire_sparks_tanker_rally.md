## Accident at Philadelphia facility could create shortages
U.S. oil industry news is usually dominated by reporting on shale oil, Permian crude production and crude oil export growth. On the petroleum product side, the focus is typically on growing exports from the U.S. Gulf area. This morning, as news of a fire at the Philadelphia Energy Solutions (PES) refinery came out, the tanker market was reminded of another crucial oil refining and demand region in the U.S. It is still too early to know what the damage is and how long repairs will take. However, it is likely that the Atlantic product tanker market and, to a lesser degree, the crude tanker market will notice the fall-out. This week, we will take a look at the North Eastern product market and what this mishap could mean for the tanker markets in the coming months.
The PES refinery is one of the oldest refineries in the U.S. The first refining units were built on the site in 1870. The refinery currently has a capacity of 335,000 barrels per day (B/d), which makes it the largest refinery on the East Coast. According to an article by Platts, the refinery produced 154 Kb/d of gasoline; 75 Kb/d of diesel and gasoil; 25 Kb/d of Jet fuel and 25 Kb/d of HFO in 2017.
While the U.S. crude oil production and refining markets have changed dramatically in the past years, most of these changes affect the U.S. Gulf area. The refineries on the East Coast have been largely insulated from these changes as there are only limited connections between the two regions. As there are no crude oil pipelines to these refineries, U.S. crude needs to be supplied by Jones Act tankers or by rail, which are both relatively expensive options and are only cost effective if there is a large discount for domestic crude oil. Petroleum products are supplied to the North Eastern market through a combination of local production, imports from the U.S. Gulf (mainly via the Colonial Pipeline) and imports from foreign refineries, mainly in Canada and Europe.
The U.S. Energy statistics use geographical regions called Petroleum Administration for Defense Districts (PADDs) and PADD1 covers the East Coast. In 2018, PADD1 consumed 5.8 Mb/d of petroleum products (3.8 Mb/d of Gasoline, 1.3 Mb/d of distillates and 0.4 Mb/d of LPG). About 995 Kb/d of these products were imported; 585 Kb/d of gasoline, 146 Kb/d of distillates and 46 Kb/d of jet fuel. About 40% of the product imports come from East Coast refiners in Canada and most of the remainder comes from Europe.
The region also exports some petroleum products, but the volumes are much smaller than the imports. A decade ago, diesel was the main export commodity as the refiners focused on gasoline production and they could sell surplus diesel to Europe. Exports of diesel have declined recently and the main exports commodities from the region are now LPG (about 40% of the total product exports).
The refineries in the region normally import most of their crude oil as it is expensive to ship domestic crude. In 2018, they imported 850 Kb/d. On occasion, when the Brent - WTI spread widens significantly, they will increase the consumption of domestic oil by shipping crude by rail from the Bakken region or by tanker from the U.S. Gulf.
If the PES refinery remains offline for an extended period of time, product tanker demand will increase as the region may need to import up to 30% more products, mainly by tankers. Currently, Canada is the main source for imports but it is unlikely that they can satisfy all the increased demand, so much of the additional imports will come from further away, mainly Europe and from the Middle East and India.
The impact on the crude market is more complicated. The impact depends on which refiners make up the shortfall and where they source their crude. Overall, the crude volumes represent a very small portion of the global crude oil trade while the changes in the product markets are more substantial.
