## Iranian crude oil exports continue to decline
Just when you think you have seen it all in the ongoing conflict between Iran and the United States, a report emerges that a representative of the U.S. State Department offered to pay millions of U.S. dollars to the master of the Iranian tanker Adrian Darya 1 to divert the disputed tanker to an American designated destination. The Adrian Darya 1 is the ex- Grace 1 , the VLCC that was detained by Gibraltar under suspicion of violating European Union sanctions on oil shipments to Syria. After the vessel was released and renamed, it set sail again. Iran provided the authorities in Gibraltar explicit written assurances that the 2 million barrels of Iranian crude oil on board the vessel would not end up in Syria. However, the vessel, which switched off its AIS (Automatic Identification System), has been skirting the Syrian coastline and the expectation is that it will somehow attempt to deliver its cargo to its original destination, either directly or by using ship-to- ship transfers to other Iranian vessels in the region. The incident has made the Adrian Darya 1 the most widely followed (and closely tracked) vessel in the world and there appears to be little chance that she will be able to discharge her cargo unnoticed. The game of cat and mouse continues.
The relentless pressure from the U.S. authorities, who are continuously expanding the reach of their sanctions, have made it near impossible for non-Iranian tankers to move Iranian crude without risking penalties. Iran is having to rely increasingly on its domestic fleet of owned tankers to deliver their crude. To limit the impact on oil production, Iran has also increased the use of floating storage. The Iranian authorities are going through great lengths in their efforts to hide their vessels' movements and they continue to look for new creative ways to circumvent the sanctions and export their crude. Despite this, the impact on Iran's export flows has been quite dramatic.
During the previous sanctions period, which ended in January 2016, Iran was able to maintain crude oil exports above 1 million barrels per day (mb/d). When the U.S. decided to withdraw from the JCPOA (Iran Nuclear deal) in May 2018 and reimposed sanctions, Iran's exports fell 66% in 6 months, from 2.5 mb/d in May to 850,000 b/d in November. After the U.S. announced that some countries (China, India, South Korea and Turkey among others) would receive partial waivers, exports recovered to 1.5 mb/d by March 2019. These waivers ended in April and the Trump administration has since then targeted 100% compliance to reduce Iranian exports to zero. Exports have collapsed as a result. Data from Lloyd's List Intelligence shows that crude oil exports from Iran have averaged less than 300,000 b/d in the period May - August 2019. Previous clients like India and Korea stopped importing altogether. From the regular customers, only China and Turkey are continuing their purchases, but even they have significantly reduced their volumes. It has to be noted that it is likely that actual exports from Iran are somewhat higher than this movement data suggest because of Iran's efforts to hide shipments and avoid the tracking of its vessels. The sanctions against Iran have reduced OPEC production and exports by some 2 mb/d. Ironically, this has helped OPEC. Without these restrictions on Iran, global oil markets, which are already awash in crude due to the rapid increases in U.S. shale production and exports, would be even more oversupplied, and OPEC would need to cut back further to avoid a collapse in oil prices.
Where do we go from here? While the U.S. maintains maximum pressure on the regime in Tehran, president Trump and his Iranian counterpart Rouhani have indicated some willingness to engage in talks. A deal would help Iranian exports and, while bearish for oil markets, this could be bullish for tanker demand. If the two side cannot agree to move toward a deal, China may step into the void. China is already Iran's largest customers and in 2016 the two countries negotiated a long-term strategic partnership. This 25-year deal (part of China's One Belt, One Road Initiative) will give China preferential access to Iran's energy sector in exchange for $280 billion of Chinese investment in Iran's oil, gas and petrochemicals sector. A deal between Iran and China may increase oil movements between them but it would also ratchet up tensions with the U.S. and this could put further pressure on the global economy.
