## Start Your Engines
This weekend may serve as one of the first official tests of whether demand for products can withstand current price levels. With crude oil prices hovering around $130 per barrel, refined product prices have been on the rise as well. The American Automobile Association estimates that 37.87 million Americans will travel 50 miles or more this weekend, marking a .9% decrease from last year. The decline in driving is far smaller than one may expect given the sizeable increases in product prices since Memorial Day 2007. The chart below shows the price of West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil, two grades of gasoline, and diesel fuel. At current price levels, the United States can expect to rack up at least a $6 billion fuel bill this weekend alone. It still seems as though high price levels are doing little to curb demand in a relevant way.

## Diesel does it
Higher diesel prices in the States have directly contributed to gasoline stock draws in recent weeks: refiners are not replenishing gasoline stocks and concentrating on more profitable diesel production. The chart below shows the relative stock levels for both diesel and gasoline.

## Gasoline Stocks
Distillate Stocks The current supply balance for gasoline suggests that short-term demand bursts, such as the holiday weekend, can contribute to a price spike. In addition, diesel's favorable pricing reduces refiners' incentive to produce gasoline, thus supporting overall higher gasoline price levels in the longer-term.
The logical way to supplement any shortcomings in domestic supplies is through imports from Europe. With prices for gasoline expected to increase there is more of an incentive to buy cargoes in Europe today and sell them into the US market at higher prices in a few weeks. In the immediate term, it is difficult to see gasoline prices coming down in the States, but high prices may begin to erode demand in as soon as a few months.
