## Undiscovered Resources
There is an estimated 45 billion barrels of oil in the US Gulf of Mexico between the Western, Central and Eastern Planning Areas. Production is generally categorized as follows: shallow water is less that 1,000 feet, deep water is between 1,000 and 7,500 feet and ultra-deep water is greater than 7,500 feet. While development in shallower water permits the use of fixed structures, there is increasing use of floating structures, such as Floating Production Storage and Offloading (FPSO) units, as the production moves toward deep and ultra-deep water.
After the exploration and decision to produce is made, field operators and partners must choose which topside unit - spars, semi- submersible units, disconnectable floating units (FPUs), or FPSO - to employ. This decision will coincide with the choice between pipeline or shuttle tanker for crude oil transportation to shore.
There are several considerations to take into account when choosing between shuttle tankers and pipelines. Perhaps first and foremost is protection from the different crude oils mixing in the pipeline that can affect the crude oil integrity and the ability to market the oil to more than one discharge location. Weather can be another factor to consider; the affects of hurricanes on fixed structures in the US Gulf of Mexico has been well documented. However fog and other weather delays must be taken into account. The ability to tie into existing pipeline infrastructure, due to distance, underlying terrain, or material constraints such as pipeline capacity, may prove to be other hurdles in the near term for new field production.
Perhaps the most interesting field development from a shipping perspective has been the Cascade-Chinook fields, in the Walker Ridge, that is lead by Petrobras. The company drew on their existing FPSO and shuttle tanker expertise in other parts of the world to become 'first movers' on the use of shuttle tankers in the US Gulf. By teaming up with experienced and quality operators for the FPSO and shuttle tankers, the company concluded that shuttle tankers would provide, safe, reliable and cost-effective transportation for crude oil.
Going-forward expect more companies to follow the lead of Petrobras. The use of the FPSO and shuttle tanker model is expected to grow in the US Gulf. In fact, as more operators adopt the system, the unit transportation cost of crude oil can be better optimized as vessels can improve their utilization by trading among different fields. We can anticipate that a North Sea-type model could work in the US Gulf adding significant economic value to operators. In fact, this could be an even more attractive alternative as ships can be designed with less rigorous design specifications, such as dynamic positioning that are not required in the US Gulf. Even if a field operator chose the dynamic positioning system the costs could still be competitive with pipelines. This is an exciting time in the US Gulf and some real progress toward meeting the ever increasing demands for energy.
