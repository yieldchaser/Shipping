## Why Is The Oil Price High
To give some perspective on the many intertwined factors that continue to push oil's price to ever loftier levels, Michael D. Tusiani, Chairman and CEO of Poten & Partners, shared his views of not only these factors, but also of what should be done to mitigate them with Lloyd's List readers earlier this week. The article that follows captures the full essence of what was published by Lloyd's List on June 9th.
Why Is The Oil Price High And What Can Be Done About It?
The primary reason the price of oil is high is that America's political leaders, for individual gain and popularity, have failed to enact a coherent and effective energy policy. They have acted irresponsibly by avoiding addressing the problem of increasing global demand and lagging supply and by lacking the resolve to establish measures to curb demand and waste. Americans are just beginning to suffer the consequences of such poor leadership.
The other reason is financial speculation which is being fueled by inconsistent government actions. Increasing Global Demand and Lagging Supply Rhetoric notwithstanding, there are no viable substitutes for transportation fuels such as gasoline and jet fuel for at least the next few decades. In addition to the rapidly increasing demand from China, India and other countries eager to grow and improve their living standards, there is also continued demand growth from developed markets such as the United States. Economic growth is not possible without increasing energy supplies.
Producing countries are not withholding oil from the market. Quite to the contrary, they are supplying the quantities requested by their customers. This is evidenced by the fact that ships are not delayed at load ports. Fifteen years ago the world had the benefit of roughly 9 million barrels per day of spare crude oil producing capacity. Today that surplus has been reduced dramatically, probably to no more than 3 million barrels per day. At that same time, refining capacity was also in surplus. Although existing refineries have been expanded, no new ones have been built in the developed world. And, as an added complication, because of the differences in product specifications, certain crude oils can only be processed at specific refineries. Thus, any headline event, such as a temporary loss of supply, a natural disaster or a weather calamity, has a magnified impact on oil price levels. In addition, the easily accessible oil has been found and new supplies, much of them located in politically difficult geographies, will be very costly and complex to bring to market. Price volatility is high. Lack of an Effective U.S. Energy Policy Our political leaders have been unwilling to take decisive actions that could have prevented today's situation. They should have taken measures to halt the wasting of energy and planned for its more effective use in the future. The U.S. Government did not intervene when American interests in Venezuela were, in effect, nationalized. Dictators such as Hugo Chavez have been able to abrogate legitimately negotiated contracts and ultimately curtail the development of resources in their countries without opposition. The U.S. Government has mandated the use of increasing quantities of ethanol, which consume more hydrocarbon energy to produce than the traditional fuels they displace. This policy has resulted in a huge reallocation of farmland to corn from which ethanol is produced. The result has been a significant agricultural foodstuff inflation, which in turn has further fueled the general rise in commodity prices. Congress continues to prohibit drilling for additional oil supplies in promising areas, onshore and offshore, even though this development activity could be undertaken in an environmentally safe manner. The U.S. Government continues to have inconsistencies in its domestic policy. Multiple mandated gasoline quality specifications across the country make it more difficult to balance a shortage in one area with an oversupply in another.
Legislators, for political gain, allow well meaning, but misguided, environmentalists to block construction of needed infrastructure projects that would enable companies to compete internationally for the much-needed supply.
Leading politicians continue to send the wrong message to OPEC by repeatedly telling them that our goal is energy independence or, in other words, we do not want their oil. This gives OPEC a legitimate disincentive to invest in increasing their future production capacity. Let us not fool ourselves; we will need OPEC oil for many years to come.
The implied "weak dollar policy" by the Federal Reserve has contributed to the upward commodity (and oil) price spiral, debased its role as the world's reserve currency, and sparked dislocations in those countries pegged to the dollar. The Administration persisted in stock piling crude oil for its strategic petroleum reserve (SPR), notwithstanding current price levels and market tightness. This gave financial speculators the wrong signal and supports upward price movements. Financial Speculation Stocks, bonds and real estate have had significant corrections resulting in huge pools of capital seeking to play in "the hottest game in town" - oil trading. It has become highly attractive because of the large and liquid futures and paper markets supported by tightening supply and demand. In addition to investment banks, hedge funds, and other financial institutions active in this market, it is probable that some oil producing countries are also speculating with physical supply and paper positions, adding fuel to the fire. Furthermore, the media's focus on gloom and doom scenarios has caused it to burn out of control.
Without any serious attempt being made to increase supply, reduce consumption or halt the fall of the dollar, the price of oil has risen and, more than likely, will continue to do so. In these circumstances, wagering on upward price movements has become close to a sure bet.

## So, what can be done?
In recent months, in order to demonstrate their willingness to combat the high price of oil, some legislators have been suggesting some rather nonsensical solutions such as a summer moratorium on the federal gas tax; a windfall profit tax on oil companies; a clampdown on futures trading; an attempt to claim that OPEC is an illegal monopoly under US Law; halting the approvals of military sales to Saudi Arabia, etc. These efforts are only detracting from the actions that need to be taken.
In the short term, the U.S. Government should: tighten CAFE standards for trucks and other light vehicles used for normal transportation; eliminate ethanol tariffs so that imports can displace domestic production to reduce pressure on corn and other grain prices; turn down heating and cooling systems; unitize product quality standards across the country. Although the intended purpose of a petroleum stockpile is not to manipulate price, if only for psychological reasons, the willingness to provide a short-term supply jolt may be necessary in today's market environment as an expedient measure to introduce downside risks for speculative players. Therefore, the Administration should not only temporarily halt the purchase of oil for storage to demonstrate that it is unwilling to buy regardless of price but, in conjunction with the EU, Japan and some farsighted OPEC producers, be prepared to release specific volumes of crude and products into the market as circumstances warrant. Although these solutions have been proposed in the past, today's circumstances make each of these actions critically important.
In the long term, obvious actions should be pursued. Our Government must encourage the development of oil and gas resources (for which there are no foreseeable substitutes). But, also incentivize research in alternative energy technologies such as carbon capture to allow the increased use of domestic coal. In addition, facilitate the construction of new nuclear power capacity and the improvement of infrastructure to allow the United States to import the energy it needs more safely and economically. Reform product quality regulations to set performance standards rather than mandate product components such as ethanol use in gasoline. Improve construction standards for buildings and factories to encourage more efficient energy usage. Accelerate modern air and ground traffic control systems to halt the waste of aviation fuels. Finally, we must restore the integrity of the U.S. dollar.
The time has come for us to act sensibly. Today's high oil price may be a blessing in disguise. It affords our political leaders the opportunity to admit and rectify their mistakes of the past. They must inform the American people that our nation has a potentially serious problem and that sacrifice is expected from all of us to solve it. Hopefully, this will lead to a well-balanced, sustainable energy policy for the future.
Michael D. Tusiani is Chairman/CEO of Poten & Partners and a Senior Fellow at Columbia University's Center for Energy, Marine Transportation and Public Policy.
