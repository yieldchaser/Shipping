## Energizing the Last Thirty Years!
This week the Energy Information Administration (EIA) threw a thirtieth birthday and invited 1,600 of their closest friends from over 35 countries! Industry representatives convened in Washington, DC for the EIA annual energy conference to discuss the challenges ahead and likely outcomes facing energy policy and the markets at large. For three decades the EIA, part of the US Department of Energy, has been a policy-neutral agency providing the industry with data, forecasts and analyses.

## Crude Oil Refinery and Blender Net Inputs
During the past twenty years, there has been a marked increase from 17 percent of refinery inputs in the mid-1980's to current unprecedented level of nearly 70 percent of imports.
Encompassing a gamut of energy topics, the EIA's conference nurtured the diverse views of speakers who were not necessarily on the same side of issues and challenges. Talks on both near and long-term petroleum market forces ranging from outlooks of 2008's summer transportation fuels to prospects for life in a peak oil environment captured the interest of many attendees. As coming legislation - both energy and environmental - will play a pivotal rule in drawing the landscape of the future energy mix, the sentiment and outlook of key legislators on these issues proved particularly poignant.

## No Facts of the Future
While no "facts" are yet available for the future, the EIA's conference abounded with a variety of outlooks for it. Reassuring for players in the liquid hydrocarbon markets is the EIA view that petroleum will remain the reigning source of BTUs for the US through 2030. Tempering its dominance, however, is petroleum's rather tepid consumption growth rate outlook of only 0.4 % per year.

## The Whole World Enchilada
In contrast to a rather weak US liquid petroleum consumption growth outlook, the EIA's takes a rather sanguine view of the rest of the world's continuing insatiable thirst for crude oil. Although the outlook for liquid petroleum's BTU market share is seen as slipping four percent from 2005 to 2030 to 34 percent, its absolute yearly contribution could rise some 40 percent to nearly 250 quadrillion BTUs. Demand growth from emerging non-OECD countries is expected to be robust and therefore account for much of this rise.
Moving this anticipated increase in liquid petroleum will necessitate a variety of transportation modes. Undoubtedly, tankers will remain the steadfast pillar in the seaborne movement of these new anticipated volumes. Given that the coming incremental demand will shift more towards these emerging consumers, the trade patterns for clean and dirty products as well as for crude oil will likely also shift.
