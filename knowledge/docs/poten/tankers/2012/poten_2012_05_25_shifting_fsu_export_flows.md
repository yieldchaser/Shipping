## Summary
Shifting FSU Export Flows
After providing 2.8 mbpd of incremental liquids supply during 2004‐09, production growth in the former Soviet Union (FSU) slowed significantly during 2010‐11, as shown in the chart below. Easing global oil demand growth, field maintenance issues in Azerbaijan and Kazakhstan, expansion work on the Caspian Pipeline Consortium (CPC) pipeline and new field development delays all contributed to the FSU slowdown. Russian production, however, has continued to hit new post‐Soviet highs, benefitting from additional output at the Vankor field and other eastern Siberia greenfield projects.

## Russia
Kazakhstan Azerbaijan Other FSU Despite the limited production outlook, FSU producers have invested significantly in export infrastructure. With the new 600 kbpd BPS‐2 pipeline to Ust‐Luga, the 700 kbpd expansion of the CPC pipeline in 2013‐14, the 160 kbpd increase in the Kazakhstan‐to‐China pipeline in 2013 and the 400 kbpd addition to the ESPO pipeline to Kozmino due in late‐2012, the FSU region will have added a cumulative total of 1.9 mbpd to its export capacity by 2015.
Pipelines are not the only infrastructure projects being pursued by FSU producers, as they are set to add 778 kbpd of incremental distillation capacity during 2012‐16, including this spring's 140 kbpd expansion at Rosneft's Tuapse refinery. Russian refiners are also focusing on upgrading capacity, in an effort to raise the conversion ratios of its long‐neglected refining system and to meet tighter Euro specs. In a static crude production environment, Russia is determined to capture additional value from exporting clean products and to reduce its role as the leading fuel oil exporter. In fact, the introduction of last year's "60‐66" export tax regime, designed to support upstream investment, also raised fuel oil duties, which should encourage additional conversion projects and a reduction in Russian fuel oil yields. This should pressure less‐sophisticated topping refineries with high fuel oil yields, leading to static Russian crude runs, but a shift in crude runs to more sophisticated plants. Meanwhile, FSU refineries outside Russia could see lower runs, as Russia cuts crude allocations to them, such as Kazakhstan's Pavlodar refinery, to meet crude export demand.

## Export
Crude Runs Within the current framework, however, the emergence of this much pipeline capacity against declining export volumes provides flexibility to producers. The 400 kbpd increase of the combined ESPO complex to 1.0 mbpd will allow Russia to supply additional crude into the growth markets of Asia, while potentially establishing ESPO Blend as the benchmark crude in the region, supplanting thinly‐traded Tapis. The pipeline also allows Russia to balance its supply between Western and Asian markets to meet customer requirements and to maximise netbacks.
Full utilisation of the new Asian pipeline infrastructure would severely curtail western exports, but ultimately, regional pricing and these netbacks would determine allocations between Western and Asian markets. The Russians are likely to adopt a more balanced approach to meeting customers' requirements, keeping in mind that European refiners should continue to rationalise capacity. The chart below highlights a potential scenario in which Russia slowly raises ESPO utilisations in 2013‐16, while Kazakhstan adopts the same approach with China volumes. This scenario would still produce an approximate 0.8 mbpd plunge in western flows during 2012‐16 to support 0.4 mbpd of Asian gains. Then, even modest rises in CPC volumes would imply 0.6 mbpd of export declines from Baltic and other Black Sea ports during the period, provided Druzhba flows drop by 0.2 mbpd.
