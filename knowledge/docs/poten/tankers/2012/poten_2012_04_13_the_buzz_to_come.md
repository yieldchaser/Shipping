## The Buzz to Come
To stop the United States' gasoline production crashing into the 10% ethanol blending wall, the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) recently swerved and approved registrations for blends with up to 15 percent of the diluent. While owners of older model vehicles and other internal combustion engines might harbor concerns that use of E15 gasoline might transform their prized possessions into roadside trash, the EPA does not. Elaborate procedures have been formulated by parties-at-interest to prevent the real world miss-fueling and this seems to be enough assurance for the Agency.
Declining US gasoline demand conspired with increasing mandated ethanol blend volumes derived from corn and other sources to speed towards its previous 10 percent limit. In allowing the sale of E15 gasoline - but not requiring it - the EPA has helped push more ethanol at US motorists. Other accommodations are needed, though, to make room in gasoline for all the future mandated volumes. As the chart below suggests, shoehorning the entire mandated ethanol volume in 2015 into withering or at best stagnant US gasoline demand without breaching a 15 percent blend limit for all gasoline seems problematic.

## Percent Ethanol in Gasoline
Ever quick to claim that E15 gasoline will add to ethanol's role in trimming the US dependency on foreign oil, the Renewable Fuels Association (RFA) ignores the real reduction in imports due to burgeoning domestic oil production and dwindling US gasoline demand. Notwithstanding the exaggerated theatrics of ethanol promoters, tanker owners are bound to wonder what, if any, impact the acceptance of E15 gasoline by consumers could have on international oil trade flows - especially those into and out of the US.
Refineries along the US Gulf Coast (PADD 3) typically take a little over half of the country's crude oil imports. They also make refined product surplus to service local needs. Some of these streams are shipped elsewhere in the nation - mainly to the East Coast by pipeline - and some travel to export markets by tanker.
Since 2005, the following chart reveals that while crude oil processing rates at these refineries may have crept upwards, the combined output of their gasoline and distillates did not. In order to meet the shifting refined product tastes of the American consumer, PADD 3 refineries produced more distillate and less gasoline.

## Refinery Distillate Produced
Total Mogas & Distillate The EPA's goal is to disperse E15 gasoline from 10,000 stations to vehicles they deemed safe to use this fuel by 2017. Representing less than 10 percent of country's retail outlets, the planned E15 gasoline pumps should not make noticeable ripples through tanker markets.
But the lingering hangover of how the US copes with its mandated inclusion of ethanol in gasoline creates big headaches for many. Tanker owners, in particular, may express concern over how these mandates are realized. Even if the US' need for hydrocarbon gasoline components diminishes by virtue of higher ethanol blend ratios, refineries along the Gulf Coast will still need to satisfy ultra-low sulfur diesel demand. Although rising domestic oil production may reduce crude oil imports, refineries will still distill lots of oil as they chase diesel barrels. Along with needed distillates, Gulf Coast refiners will also spew out gasoline stocks. When more volumes of ethanol arrive to meet the mandates, additional hydrocarbons might be displaced from their gasoline pool. These orphaned hydrocarbons will likely travel to markets elsewhere via clean product tankers.
