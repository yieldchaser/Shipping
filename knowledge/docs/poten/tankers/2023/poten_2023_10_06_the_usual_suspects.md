## China remains key to crude tanker demand in 2024
The IEA estimates that China has now overtaken the US as the country with the largest refining capacity with 18.2 million barrels per day (Mb/d) in 2022, representing about 17.7% of the global capacity. The IEA projects that China's refining capacity will grow by a further 1.5 Mb/d by 2028 to 19.7Mb/d. As China only produces about 4.3 Mb/d crude oil, they are by far the largest importer of crude oil and therefore a significant driver of the tanker market. This week we will examine the dynamics of Chinese crude oil imports.
So far in 2023, Vortexa data shows that seaborne crude oil and condensates imports of China have averaged about 10.4 Mb/d. This is about 1.4 Mb/d more than in 2022 and 0.7 Mb/d more than in 2020. Additionally, China has agreements to import about 800 Kb/d of pipeline crude from Russia through the ESPO pipeline system and for about 200 Kb/d piped from Kazakhstan into northwestern China. So far in 2023 Saudi Arabia is still the largest exporter of seaborne oil to China with 1.66 Mb/d, followed by Russia at 1.44 Mb/d (although Russia exceeds Saudi Arabia in total imports due to pipeline volumes) and Iraq at 1.04 Mb/d.
Since the Russian invasion of Ukraine, China has increased its imports of seaborne Russian crude from 0.8 Mb/d in 2021 to 1.4 Mb/d currently. Similarly, as Iran has been increasing exports, China has expanded its imports of Iranian crude from 560 Kb/d in 2022 to 845 Kb/d in 2023 (in recent months Iranian exports to China have exceeded 1.0 Mb/d). Both exporters were discounting their crude oil in 2023 due to sanctions which limited the number of potential buyers. China is more sensitive to prices, and they seem to opportunistically buy more crude oil when they perceive prices to be low while using more crude from inventories when prices are high (see Figure 1). The graph clearly shows increased imports in the spring of 2020, when prices collapsed as COVID led to lockdowns in many countries and cut imports in 2022 when prices were high after Russia invaded Ukraine.
Most of China's refineries are controlled by state oil companies, but there are also privately owned ones, often referred to as Teapot Refineries. Many of these independent refineries are located in Shandong province, on the East coast of China (although there are also some state-owned refineries in Shandong). Figure 2 shows the seaborne imports from sanctioned countries (Russia, Iran and Venezuela) into ports located in Shandong versus imports into other Chinese ports. Initially, the Teapot refineries increased their purchases of Russian crude to take advantage of the price differential between Russian grades and alternatives. Since the beginning of 2023, state refiners in other parts of the country have increased their imports as well, somewhat crowding out the Teapot refiners.
Oil demand in China has been recovering well after the decline in 2022 driven by Covid lockdowns. The chemical industry has performed very well in 2023 (boosting Naphtha demand) and gasoline consumption has also recovered to 2021 levels. This is partially offset by slower recovery of jet fuel demand. Domestic flights have largely recovered, but demand from international travel is still lower. The IEA forecasts Chinese oil demand for 2023 to reach 16.29 Mb/d, 1.6 Mb/d higher than in 2022 and 1.2 Mb/d higher than in 2021.
Chinese oil demand for 2024 is more uncertain, even though economic issues don't seem to have affected oil demand significantly so far in 2023. The economic outlook for China is unclear, because of the problems facing several large real estate developers. The IEA takes a cautious approach with an oil demand increase of only 0.6 Mb/d for 2024.
The import patterns of Chinese crude oil are also critically important to the tanker market: This year, China uses VLCCs for 83% of its seaborne crude imports from countries other than Russia and only 5% Aframaxes. For Chinese imports from Russia the picture was reversed with 63% Aframaxes and only 6% VLCCs. Imports from Russia seem to have peaked, and China is sourcing more crude oil from the Atlantic Basin (U.S. and Brazil for example). These longer- haul trades will boost ton-mile demand, in particular for VLCCs. As has been the case for the last two decades, the key to the tanker market performance continues to lie in China.
