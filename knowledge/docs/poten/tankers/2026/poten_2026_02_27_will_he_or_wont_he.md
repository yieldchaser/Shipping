## Considering alternative Iran scenarios
Iran, its nuclear program and the regime's strained relationship with the U.S. and Israel is dominating the oil and tanker markets again. Talks about a new nuclear deal are under way. Yesterday, representatives from the U.S. and Iran concluded a third round of talks at the Omani embassy in Geneva. Both sides agreed to move to "technical level" discussions, starting on Monday, March 2. To pressure the Iranian regime into a deal, the U.S. has assembled its largest concentration of air and naval power in the Middle East since the 2003 invasion of Iraq. President Trump has set an early March deadline for Iran to agree to a new nuclear deal and warned that all options are on the table if a deal is not reached. While mediators talk about "significant progress" amid "intense" negotiations, a deal does not appear to be imminent. The oil and tanker markets watch the back and forth with bated breath since each potential outcome could have different implications for oil prices and tanker rates. In this Weekly Opinion, we will describe some alternative outcomes.
In the most benign scenario, the U.S. and Iran continue to work towards a negotiated settlement. Iran will change its nuclear posture as a result of diplomatic pressure combined with the aggressive military positioning from the U.S. A direct military confrontation is avoided, but until the negotiations conclude successfully, sanctions will largely remain in place. Iran's exports will not be impacted; with virtually all of its crude oil heading to China on dark fleet vessels. However, some of the risk premium in oil prices will dissipate. Tanker rates lose the impetus of additional Middle East exports, but the other factors supporting the freight market remain in place. Eventually, when a deal is reached that leads to sanctions relief, Iran will re-enter the international oil markets. Iranian oil on the water, including what is kept in floating storage, reached a record amount of between 160 and 170 million barrels last month. This will gradually be released into the market. The same applies to the Iranian tanker fleet. The National Iranian Tanker Company (NITC) fleet is old, not well maintained and lacks vetting approvals, limiting their trading flexibility. Modernizing their fleet will take time and money and Iran will likely have many competing priorities for its oil revenues if/when the precious dollars start flowing again. A reentry of Iran into the global oil market will doom many of the dark fleet tankers, especially the VLCCs which have already been eliminated from the Venezuelan trades and are not useful for Russian exports. This will likely boost VLCC rates.
An alternative scenario involves limited military strikes against the regime in Tehran. Targeted U.S. strikes with a contained Iranian response will likely lead to a short-term spike in oil prices and tanker rates. However, if oil infrastructure in the region is spared and maritime transport through the Strait of Hormuz continues without sustained interruption, the impact will be limited to some shipping delays, higher insurance costs and tighter sanctions enforcement.
The first two scenarios are the most likely, but there is a risk of a third outcome that cannot be ignored: a large-scale U.S. attack followed by a significant Iranian counterstrike involving attacks on oil installations in the region and attempts to restrict flows through the Strait of Hormuz. This scenario could lead to a sustained increase in oil prices and tanker freight rates. The U.S. military would be compelled to protect freedom of navigation and has the hardware to keep the Strait of Hormuz open. Tankers can enter and exit the Arabian Gulf with military escorts for protection, similar to what eventually happened during the 1981-1988 "Tanker War" between Iran and Iraq.
From a tanker market perspective, it appears that whatever happens, there is little risk that freight rates will go down as a result. The tanker market is already very tight, and rates are near historic highs. Under these circumstances, the risk is to the upside. Over the long-term, the best outcome for everybody involved would be a negotiated solution that would de-escalate the tension in the Middle East, stop the nuclear proliferation in the region and - as sanctions are lifted - improve the fate of the Iranian people. Unfortunately, what is best for the world is not always what happens.
