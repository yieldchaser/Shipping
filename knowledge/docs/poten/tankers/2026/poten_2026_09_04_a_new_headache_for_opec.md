## The Americas are the growth engine in global oil and shipping
Prior to the shale oil revolution in the United States, which started in the early 2000s with production rapidly accelerating after 2008, the general assumption was that the global oil markets would become more and more reliant on the vast and cheap oil resources from the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC). This trend seemed to become even more "inevitable" when Russia and nine other producers joined the cartel to form "OPEC+". However, for a variety of reasons, it has not turned out that way so far. In this Tanker Opinion, we will discuss some key developments in global oil markets, likely future trends and how these will impact the tanker market.
A brief history of OPEC's influence on global oil production. OPEC's market share peaked at 50% in the early 1970s, before wars in the Middle East, and a policy of trying to maintain high oil prices by cutting production started to erode that market position. Rapidly increasing prices reduced global demand. At the same time, non-OPEC production surged, especially from new sources, including Alaska, Gulf of Mexico, and the North Sea. By 1985, OPEC's market share had dropped to 26%, and Saudi Arabia, which had acted as a swing producer, abandoned this role and raised production sharply. OPEC's market share recovered on the back of low prices and surplus capacity.
In the early 2000s, China almost single-handedly bailed out the oil industry. Rapid demand growth in emerging economies - led by China - fueled a commodity "super cycle" that pushed oil prices above $130/barrel in 2008. OPEC greatly benefited from this boom, as did the tanker market. China's rapid oil demand growth boosted tanker demand, especially for VLCCs.
After the 2008 global financial crisis, oil prices initially collapsed, but they rebounded above $100/barrel when OPEC cut production. Meanwhile, U.S. shale output - driven by technology and operational innovation - -greatly expanded supply, eroding OPEC's market share. OPEC raised output in response, pushing prices below $30/barrel in an effort to put shale producers out of business., but U.S. output continued to expand. In response to low prices, OPEC formed OPEC+ in 2016 to coordinate output cuts with 10 non-member producers.
However, this expanded cartel never became the market force OPEC anticipated: The 2020-2021 Covid-19 pandemic, the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, the 2026 regime change in Venezuela and the U.S. - Iran war, closing the Strait of Hormuz, all severely reduced OPEC's market influence. On top of that, several OPEC member states (Ecuador, Angola, and most recently the UAE) left the cartel and Venezuela may follow suit. The geopolitical tensions and export restrictions resulting from the wars in Europe and the Middle East have created a powerful incentive for growth in non-OPEC production. Nowhere has this potential presented itself more prominently than in the Americas. Crude oil production in Canada, the U.S. and South America (Guyana, Brazil, Argentina) expanded dramatically over the last decade. This production growth has translated directly into more seaborne exports (Chart 1). The U.S. remains an export powerhouse, Canada boosted exports through the expanded Trans Mountain pipeline, Guyana is adding one FPSO after the other, and Brazil keeps expanding its offshore output. At the same time, Argentina, with its vast shale reserves and Suriname, with a similar geology as neighboring Guyana, are waiting in the wings. Venezuela is in the early stages of its recovery, but has huge potential, which the U.S. is eager to explore and develop.
For the tanker industry, these are generally positive developments. Many of the countries that have growing production are targeting the export markets, either because of a saturated domestic market (U.S. and Canada) or, in the case of Guyana and Suriname, because of limited domestic demand. While some of the oil from the Americas is exported to the U.S. and Europe, most of the incremental barrels (now and in the future) are targeting Asia. All main tanker segments are benefiting from this development (Chart 2).
The relentless rise of the Americas is a potential headache for OPEC. It will make it more difficult to recapture market share after the conflict in the Middle East ends. Future global oil demand growth will likely slow and the logistical challenges in the Middle East will motivate Asian buyers to diversify away from Persian Gulf producers towards more stable geographies in the Atlantic Basin.
