## Venezuela has a bright future, but challenges remain
Things can change quickly in the oil markets. A few weeks ago, on December 12, 2025, we wrote a Tanker Opinion entitled "Pirates Of The Caribbean?", discussing President Trump's maximum pressure campaign against his Venezuelan counterpart, Nicolas Maduro. Now, Maduro is in custody in New York, and the United States is "running" Venezuela and its oil industry. The situation is developing so fast that it is difficult to keep up. What is happening in Venezuela, now and in the future, can have significant implication for the oil industry and the tanker market. In this Tanker Opinion we will try to dissect what we know and what could happen in the future.
The changes in Venezuela will have implications for the oil and tanker markets in the short, medium and long term. A lot of the details still need to be worked out, but we can already draw some preliminary conclusions. Given the poor state of Venezuela's oil production facilities and export infrastructure It is unlikely that Venezuelan production and exports will increase dramatically in the short term beyond current volumes of around 1.0 Million barrels per day (Mb/d). Venezuela has the world's largest petroleum reserves, which gives it enormous potential in the long term, but most experts agree that the upside within the next three years is limited to about 500,000 b/d. For Venezuela to return to 3.5 - 5.0 Mb/d will take much longer (7-10 years). International oil and oil service companies will need clear legal protections, proper fiscal terms, operational control over exports, credible political ‑ stability and payment assurances before committing large ‑ scale capital to Venezuela's oil sector.
Who will likely invest in the Venezuelan oil industry? President Trump has called on the U.S. oil companies to step up, but the response has not been overwhelmingly positive given the political, legal, commercial and regulatory challenges mentioned above. Several companies, however, have responded positively. Chevron and Repsol, who are already active in Venezuela seem interested to expand their footprint in the country. Hilcorp, a large privately owned U.S. company may also be interested to get involved.
In the short-term, the biggest change in Venezuela may be a redirection of the trade. Prior to the Trump naval blockade and Maduro's removal, the vast majority (~70%) of Venezuela's crude was exported (mostly on sanctioned "dark fleet" VLCCs) to China, while the remainder went mostly to the U.S. under the Chevron license. Now that the U.S. government is controlling the exports, it is likely that more Venezuelan crude oil will be directed to refiners in the U.S. Gulf. After all, many U.S. Gulf refiners are configured to run the heavy Venezuelan crude. The short sailing distance gives U.S. refiners a freight advantage as well. More access to Venezuelan crude will help U.S. refiners that previously bought Mexican grades, since Mexico's exports have been in decline.
So, what will China do? If (most of) Venezuela's crude will end up in the U.S., where will the Chinese source replacement barrels? Will they pick up more Canadian barrels, which are of similar specifications? If so, will they source that crude from the U.S. Gulf on VLCCs or from Vancouver on Aframaxes? If it comes from Vancouver, will that mean less Canadian crude going into the U.S. West Coast? That would mean refiners in California need replacement barrels. Will those come from Latin America or the Middle East? All these decisions will also impact which tanker segments will benefit or suffer Time will tell how this will all play out. Global commodity giants Vitol and Trafigura seem to be involved in the marketing and export of the initial 30-50 Million barrels. The initial step for some of the barrels does not seem to be direct exports, but an interim step via storage in the Bahamas. To facilitate smooth exports, some of the U.S. sanctions are likely to be lifted, although the ongoing blockade gives the U.S. government leverage over Venezuela without boots on the ground.
One thing seems certain. Sanctioned shadow tankers have no business anymore in the Venezuelan trade and that will boost employment opportunities for the mainstream fleet.
