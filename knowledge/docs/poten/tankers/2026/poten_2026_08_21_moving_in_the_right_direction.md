## Venezuela's production and exports are recovering
On January 3 rd of this year, Venezuelan president Maduro and his wife were captured by U.S. special forces in a predawn raid and flown to the United States to face criminal charges. Delcy Rodriguez, Maduro's former Vice President and Oil Minister, was officially sworn in as the interim president of Venezuela on January 5, 2026. The new government is actively cooperating with Washington. Under pressure from the U.S. government, Rodriquez quickly passed sweeping new energy legislation, reversing decades of strict state control and reopening the country to foreign investment. While the country's oil industry faces enormous challenges due to decades of corruption, underinvestment and infrastructure decay, there has been some progress in Venezuela's crude oil production and exports are starting to recover. In this Tanker Opinion, we'll discuss what has been achieved so far, what still remains to be done and how the regime change impacted the tanker market.
By the end of 2025, Venezuela produced between 840,000 and 1 Mb/d of crude oil. At the low point, exports were down to around 500,000 b/d. About 30% of Venezuela's crude was exported to the U.S. by Chevron, the only meaningful Western presence remaining in the country. The other 70% of Venezuela's crude was going to China. China's close relationship with Venezuela started some 20 years ago under then-President Hugo Chavez. Through the China Development Bank, Beijing funneled between $50 billion to $60 billion in loans into Venezuela. These loans were explicitly structured to be repaid not in cash, but in physical oil shipments to Chinese oil companies. When the United States imposed heavy economic sanctions on Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A. (PDVSA) in 2019, the traditional Western buyers fled the market to avoid U.S. penalties. China stepped in as a financial lifeline by utilizing its independent "teapot" refineries, privately owned domestic refineries. Because these teapot refineries had little to no financial exposure to the U.S. banking system or American markets, they were mostly insulated from the threat of U.S. secondary sanctions. However, Venezuela paid a steep price for China's help. It was forced to sell its heavy, sour crude at a significant discount to global prices. Transportation costs were high as well since the oil was shipped to China on "dark fleet" VLCCs, which routinely charged 60% to 100% more than regular mainstream tankers. On December 17, 2025, the U.S. started a naval blockade of Venezuela, designed to intercept the dark fleet and starve the Maduro regime of funds. The blockade was strategically designed to maximize pressure on the upper echelons of the Venezuelan government, directly setting the stage for the Delta Force extraction of Maduro and his wife. However, after January 3 rd of this year, the situation changed significantly. Production has increased to 1.25 Mb/d and exports regularly exceed 1.0 Mb/d. Exports to China stopped completely. The United States government took charge of PDVSA's energy sales and, to reduce Chinese and Russian influence in the Western hemisphere, Venezuela's heavy crude was systematically re-allocated. The U.S. authorized mainstream Western commodities traders like Vitol and Trafigura to clear out backlogged storage and direct the oil to U.S. Gulf Coast refineries, which are uniquely engineered to process Venezuela's heavy, sour crude. Some 50-75% of Venezuela's crude is now shipped to the U.S., with the remainder going to Europe and India. This has also shifted the tanker segments that are being employed. Prior to January 2026, VLCC shipments to China dominated the transportation landscape. VLCCs are still being utilized for shipments to India, but the U.S. and Europe are primarily served by Aframaxes and Suezmaxes. And this time transportation is done on mainstream, regular tankers, not shadow tankers from the dark fleet.
Venezuela is still far away from the 3.2 Mb/d of production that they reached in the late-1990s. The industry is bottlenecked by decaying infrastructure. Decades of underinvestment, corruption, and systemic neglect have left pipelines, refineries, and wells in severe disrepair. However, the country does have huge potential. It has the largest proven oil reserves in the world, and, after some initial hesitation, Western companies have started to sign new deals with Venezuela. These investments will boost future production, exports and tanker employment. As long as Venezuela keeps moving in the right direction, the future is bright.
