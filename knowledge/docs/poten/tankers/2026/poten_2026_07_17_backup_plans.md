## How will additional pipeline capacity impact tanker trades?
When the US-Israeli war against Iran broke out, leading to the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz (SOH), quick calculations showed that about 20 million barrels per day (Mb/d) of oil and oil products would be impacted. Many of the countries in the Middle East rely heavily on the SOH to export their crude oil and refined products. Only two countries (Saudi Arabia and the UAE) have access to meaningful pipeline capacity circumventing the Strait. The crisis in Hormuz has led to the creation of new and the revival of old plans to build and/or expand pipeline capacity to circumvent the SOH. In this opinion we will discuss some of these options, their timing and what the impact may be on the tanker market in the future.
Starting with the existing capacity, Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline has a capacity of 7 Mb/d (including 2 Mb/d dedicated to Saudi Arabia's west-coast refineries. That means that Saudi Arabia currently has 5 Mb/d of export potential through the port of Yanbu. Saudi Arabia has started discussions about expanding the East-West pipeline by 1-2 Mb/d, but any meaningful increase in throughput would require debottlenecking at Yanbu, which has reached its sustainable export capacity.
The UAE has the Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline (ADCOP) with a capacity of 1.8 Mb/d. It runs 230 miles from the Habshan oil fields in Abu Dhabi to the port of Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman. The UAE, which formally exited OPEC on May 1, 2026, wants to double its export pipeline capacity to 3.6 Mb/d by adding a pipeline parallel to the current ADCOP network. This project, which also requires upgrades to Fujairah, has been fast tracked due to the SOH crisis and could be complete by mid-2027.
Iraq has access to the Mediterranean via the Kirkuk - Ceyhan pipeline. It has a theoretical capacity of 1.4 Mbd, linking the northern Iraqi oil fields to the Turkish port of Ceyhan. However, recent flows have been limited, and the pipeline does not currently connect to Iraq's major southern Basra export system. Iraq is considering various options to gain more access to the Mediterranean either via Turkey to Ceyhan or via Syria to Baniyas. Another alternative for Iraq is a pipeline through Jordan to the port of Aqaba on the Red Sea. Once (one of) these projects is selected and approved, it will take at least 3-5 years to complete.
Countries like Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain have limited options, although Kuwait is having discussions with its neighbor, Saudi Arabia to piggyback on some of their expansions to the Red Sea.
Iran itself also has an alternative route, avoiding the SOH. Iran inaugurated the 1.0 Mb/d Goreh-Jask pipeline and the Jask export terminal on the Gulf of Oman with a single export cargo in July 2021. However, loadings have been sporadic, and most analysts see this pipeline as effectively non-operational at the moment.
If and when all these expansions and new projects come on-line in the next three to five years, it could theoretically reduce the dependence on the SOH by several million barrels. This will significantly reduce Iran's leverage over its neighbors in the Persian Gulf. It should be noted however that the current pipeline backup plans seem to primarily focus on crude oil. The 5.0 Mb/d of refined products coming from the Persian Gulf will remain at risk from a SOH closure.
The impact on the tanker markets of the additional export pipelines will depend on the geopolitical and security situation in the Middle East over the next 5-10 years. If the situation settles down and the SOH returns to its pre-war status (i.e. unimpeded transits, no restrictions or fees), the new or expanded pipelines will likely be mostly used as a backup plan. The SOH will remain the dominant transit route. On the other hand, if regional tensions remain high and commercial shipping continues to be at risk, the pipelines will be utilized to limit the impact on the crude oil markets.
The crude tanker market will probably benefit from these alterative pipeline routes. It will keep more barrels flowing and reduces the risk of a large number of vessels getting stuck in the Persian Gulf. Overall, the development of alternative outlets will reduce Iran's leverage and may discourage them from playing the Hormuz card.
