## China holds the key to the tanker market
China is by far the largest crude oil importer in the world, most of it seaborne. Over the last 10 years, China's seaborne imports grew from 6.9 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) in 2016 to 10.6 Mb/d in 2025, an average increase of 5% per year. This upward trend continued in the early months of 2026, until the closure of the Strait of Hormuz started to impact China's import levels. It is difficult to overestimate the impact of Chinese crude oil imports on the tanker market. BIMCO (the Baltic and International Maritime Council), the world's largest international shipping association, estimates that Chinese crude oil imports drive slightly more than 20% of crude tanker volumes and about 30% of crude tanker tonne-miles. Given its dependence on imports, it is no surprise that Beijing has been preparing for potential supply disruptions, such as the one caused by the current conflict in the Middle East. How China is dealing with the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and what Chinese refiners will do when it reopens will have a significant impact on the tanker market. In this Tanker Opinion we will dive into some of these factors and their potential impact.
The tanker market experienced a significant rate spike when the Iran war started and the Strait of Hormuz closed, trapping hundreds of tankers in the Persian Gulf. However, freight rates were already on an upward trajectory for several months prior to the start of the conflict. An important driver of this trend was Chinese stockbuilding. Oil analysts estimate that 83% of the increase in Chinese crude oil imports in 2025 was stock driven rather than purely consumption driven. China does not report official data on its oil inventories, but third-party sources estimate that Chinese commercial and government held inventories are somewhere between 1.2 and 1.4 Billion barrels.
During the war in Iran, China became a shock absorber. China's large inventory cushion allowed it to reduce imports sharply rather than compete aggressively for scarce replacement barrels. China's seaborne imports were about 11 million b/d in the months prior to the war, fell to 8.3 million b/d in April 2026, and are estimated to be just 6.5 million b/d in May. The reduction in imports is driven by several factors. Refiners have cut runs as higher crude prices have destroyed margins. According to Platts, the average run rate of China's state-owned refineries was 71.6% in May, the lowest since March 2020, when the country was hit by the coronavirus pandemic. Refiners have also dipped into their strategic and commercial inventories.
For tanker markets, China's reduction in imports have created a split effect. The war-risk premium and disrupted Persian Gulf logistics are bullish for rates, but lower Chinese spot buying reduces cargo count. That is why the market can show very high freight rates on constrained routes while actual Chinese import volumes fall. China's import restraint has also had a moderating effect on oil prices, which would have probably been much higher if China tried to maintain imports at pre-war levels.
What will happen when the war ends and the Strait reopens? The immediate post-conflict phase could initially pressure freight if war-risk premiums collapse, the oil tankers stuck in the Persian Gulf are released and trade flows start to normalize. However, the medium-term effect should be supportive because oil prices will come down, oil demand will recover and inventories will need to be rebuilt. The EIA's May 2026 outlook assumes that global oil inventories fall by an average 8.5 million b/d in 2Q26, before flows gradually resume and withdrawals lessen. There will be a global restocking impulse after the crisis, which could add 950 Million to 1.2 Billion barrels of oil demand over several years. That demand will be spread out, but it still creates a structural floor under seaborne crude trade after the Strait reopens.
China will probably also continue its diversification drive away from the Persian Gulf. After the Strait of Hormuz closed, China has been the main beneficiary of the increased Saudi flows out of Yanbu. It will be interesting to see how the flows develop in the future. To the extent that China imports more crude from the Atlantic Basin (Brazil, Argentina, Guyana, U.S., West Africa) it will boost ton- mile demand. VLCCs would be the main beneficiary. More imports from Canada (Vancouver) will mostly benefit Aframaxes. Overall, China will continue to be the main driver of global tanker demand, even when Chinese oil demand growth slows in the future, as expected.
