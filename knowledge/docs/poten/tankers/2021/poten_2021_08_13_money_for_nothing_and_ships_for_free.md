## Explaining the variability in tanker owners' TCE's
Shipowners use a concept called Time Charter Equivalent earnings (TCE's) to compare earnings across alternative trade routes and to measure spot rates relative to the earnings of a time charter. TCE's, which are measured in US dollars per day ($/day), are defined as the daily earnings that are left after the owner pays for fuel and port expenses (voyage related costs). These TCE earnings are meant to pay for the fixed daily costs of operating a vessel, i.e. crewing, technical management, insurance, general & administrative (G&A) as well as contribute to the capital cost, debt service and interest. The TCE's that vessels generate on the spot market are generally much more volatile than the daily hire that is paid under a fixed term contract, which is called the daily Time Charter Hire. The tanker market, as expressed in TCE's has been very depressed over the last 12 months. VLCCs. the largest crude oil tankers, have been particularly hard hit. TD3c is the benchmark VLCC route that depicts the earnings of a large crude oil tanker moving 2 million barrels of crude oil from the Middle East to China. The Baltic Exchange (an independent source of maritime market information) as well as all the major shipbrokers (including Poten) report the TCE's on the TD3c-route daily. Once a quarter, these industry averages get a "reality check" when various publicly traded shipowners release their quarterly earnings, including the TCE's for their various vessel segments. In this week's Opinion, we will try to find out why there is sometimes a wide discrepancy between actual earnings and broker reported TCE's.
The Chart compares Poten's TCE assessments on the TD3 benchmark route with a range of earnings reported by publicly listed tanker companies (International Seaways, Euronav, Frontline and DHT). It is noticeable that - over time - the earnings are trending similarly, but during certain periods the tanker companies markedly outperform the benchmark. The period from Q3 2020 thru Q2 2021 was a case in point. While the TD3 earnings averaged a measly $6,000/day during this period, the average from the four listed tanker owners mentioned above was more than $23,000/day. How can this be explained? There are a number of factors that need to be considered.
First, there is a difference in timing: In a volatile market, reported company earnings tend to lag spot market results. It takes time for the owners to fix their vessels to the higher rates, especially for long-haul VLCC voyages. On the other hand, when rates decline, large owners will still have many vessels employed on higher rates and their earnings will only gradually adjust to the lower spot market. The effect of timing was very visible in 2020: VLCC rates spiked in Q2 and then collapsed in Q3, but the TCE earnings of tanker owners showed a smoother decline after they maxed out in Q3.
The second reason why benchmark TCE's can be different from the company earnings has to do with the assumptions that are used for the calculations. For its TD3c calculation, Poten uses a theoretical VLCC with a laden speed of 13 knots, consuming 70 Metric Tons (MT) of VLSFO per day. For the ballast leg, we assume a speed of 13 knots, consuming 61.5 MT/day. In today's market, this theoretical VLCC would generate a TCE of -$6,600/day. Actual vessels have a wide range of speed & consumption numbers. For illustration, a modern ECO VLCC with speed/consumption levels of 13 knots/52 tons (laden) and 12 knots/42 tons (ballast) would generate earnings of $1,000/day, an improvement of $7,600/day. If this vessel was equipped with scrubbers and burn cheaper HFO, the TCE would improve by another $5,100/day to reach $6,100/day. If the owner of this vessel decided to "slow steam" on the ballast leg to conserve fuel, the TCE's could improve even more. These examples go a long way in explaining the difference between the earnings reported by the large tanker owners and the benchmark TCE's on broker reports. But there is another important factor: vessel utilization.
The standard TD3 voyage assumes that the vessel loads in the Arabian Gulf, sails to China (laden) and returns back to the Arabian Gulf in ballast (50/50 laden/ballast). However, in reality, many vessels trade worldwide, and large owners with experienced chartering staff that have good customer relations, will be able to optimize the routing of the vessels to improve the ballast/laden ratio. An ideal example would be if an owner can secure a cargo loading in the Arabian Gulf and discharging in the US Gulf (LOOP), followed by a laden voyage from the U.S. Gulf to China, before the vessel ballasts back to the Arabian Gulf. This "triangulation" voyage generates a TCE of $25,000/day, compared to the -$6,600/day on the standard TD3 voyage.
In conclusion, there is no doubt that the market has been very bad for the last 9-12 months. However, large owners with modern, fuel-efficient vessels, many of which are equipped with scrubbers, are "outperforming" the benchmark TD3 route. Under the right circumstances, this outperformance can be quite substantial.
