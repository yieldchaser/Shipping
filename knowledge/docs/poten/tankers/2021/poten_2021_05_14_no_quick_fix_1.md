## No Quick Fix
Tanker market pundits seem to be in either one of two camps. The optimists foresee a rapid recovery of rates in the second half of 2021, driven by post Covid demand recovery, while the pessimists don't see any recovery before 2022 or even 2023. The main argument of the pessimists is the overcapacity in the tanker market. Everybody agrees that the tanker market will recover, it is cyclical after all, but people disagree on the timing. In this weeks' Tanker Opinion, we will take a look at the developments in ton-mile demand for crude oil tankers from Q1 2019 until Q1 2021. We will compare this to the changes in the fleet size over the same period to see if we can explain the current rate environment and shed some light on the timing of the recovery in the crude tanker market.
Back in 2019, pre-pandemic, the average demand for crude oil transportation was approximately 29 Billion ton-miles per day (Btm/d). In 2020, after the worldwide spread of Covid-19 led to a significant drop in global oil demand, tanker demand declined to 24 Btm/day (-17%). From September 2020 through January 2021, ton-mile demand gradually recovered, although it remained well below 2019 levels, as OPEC+ producers (mostly) maintained their production cuts. In January 2021, Saudi Arabia announced, in a surprise move, that it would unilaterally cut an additional 1 million barrels per day (mb/d) of crude oil production starting in February. The impact of this decision on ton-mile demand is clearly visible in the chart. It cut ton-mile demand by 5.6%. For April of 2021 (the most recent data available), crude oil tanker demand was 25.9 Btm/d, still 10% below the same period of 2019. Based on this data, it should be no surprise that the crude oil tanker market is in the doldrums.
However, there is light at the end of the tunnel. OPEC has started to increase production and inventory drawdowns will likely subside, so future demand growth should translate into more seaborne transportation. It will take some time though before oil (and transportation) demand is back to 2019 levels.
Freight rates are driven by the interplay between tanker demand and supply. With demand on the path to recovery (but still well below pre-pandemic levels), let's look at tanker supply. Unfortunately, the picture is not encouraging.
In the first quarter of 2019, the size of the large tanker fleet (VLCC, Suezmax and Aframax) was approximately 430 Million DWT. VLCCs represented 230 Mdwt (53% of the total). While oil (and tanker) demand dropped significantly during 2020, due to the Covid-19 outbreak, the fleet continued to grow as deliveries continued and scrapping remained low. In April 2021, the size of the crude oil tanker fleet is 11.5% higher than it was in January 2019, while ton-mile demand is 10% lower than the beginning of that year. The market has to bridge this gap before a recovery can take hold.
As we mentioned before, the outlook for tanker demand is positive, although new outbreaks of the virus could make the demand recovery somewhat choppy and unpredictable. However, it is tanker supply that poses the bigger problem.
Expected tanker deliveries for 2021 are similar to the levels of 2020, so, everything else being equal, the large tanker fleet will grow by another 4-5% this year. With oil demand still well below pre-pandemic levels, this fleet expansion poses a problem.
The tanker market needs a supply adjustment. While a low-rate environment will encourage slow-steaming, that is only a temporary solution. The only quick and permanent answer to the oversupply of tankers is demolition. However, tanker scrapping has not (yet) picked up in a meaningful way, despite the low rates. In 2020, and so far in 2021 we have only seen very limited scrapping. The last time the market experienced significant demolition was in 2018, when 33 VLCCs, 24 Suezmaxes and 39 Aframaxes hit the beaches. This wave of demolition helped set the stage for the market recovery in 2019. Demolition at similar levels in 2021 would go a long way in solving the current supply/demand imbalance.
