## Another eventful year comes to a close
At the start of 2021, we were hopeful that - after the rollercoaster ride of 2020 amidst a global health pandemic - the tanker markets would gradually improve. Multiple Covid vaccines were available and a new president in the United States would bring more stability to global markets. There was a large vessel supply overhang and the recovery in oil demand was expected to be gradual, but the overall consensus was that the market would start to improve in the second half of the year. For a number of reasons, this cautious optimism proved to be misplaced. In this year-end review of 2021, we will discuss some of these factors as well as a few other developments that caught our eye throughout the year and that we discussed in the other 42 Tanker Opinions that we published this year.
There were a number of key drivers that led to disappointing tanker earnings during 2021. Global oil demand growth was slower than forecasted, in particular in the countries that were the key drivers in previous years. China's zero Covid policy contributed to a down year in the oil markets. According to Bloomberg, China is on track to record its first annual decline in crude oil imports since records began in 2004. India was the center of the Delta variant in the early part of 2021, hampering its oil demand recovery. Oil demand did recover in the OECD region in 2021 (+2.5 Mb/d), but almost 50% of this demand growth was satisfied through inventory drawdowns, dampening the impact on tanker demand. The release of vessels from floating storage was a double-negative for the market. It reduced the demand for tankers and increased its supply at the same time. With the Omicron variant rapidly spreading across the globe, the outlook for the early part of 2022 is not promising. In their December Oil Market Report, the IEA adjusted its expected Q1 2022 oil demand down by 600,000 b/d (relative to last months' projections), evenly split between OECD and non-OECD countries.
The reduction of global oil inventories, both on-land and on tankers (floating storage), which were accumulated during the early stages of the pandemic was an important goal of OPEC. This objective was largely achieved through the slow return of the OPEC+ cutbacks, in addition to a voluntary 1 Mb/d Saudi "bonus cut" in the spring of 2021. and aided by two significant weather events in the U.S.; the Texas winter storm in February (" Texas Deep Freeze " - 19 February 2021) and Hurricane Ida in August (" Weathering the Storm " - 3 September 2021), both of which knocked out significant energy infrastructure in the U.S. Gulf.
The tanker supply side did not help freight markets. The year started out with a significant oversupply of tonnage and while scrapping picked up in 2021 relative to 2020, it is nowhere near the levels required to balance the market (" Demolition Derby " - 7 May 2021). The total demolition numbers in 2021 are likely to be more than double those of 2020 (85 tankers in 2021 YTD versus 40 total for 2020), but the numbers are skewed toward the smaller product tankers and the totals remain well below the 137 tankers that were scrapped in 2018. Overall deliveries for 2021 were 16% higher than in 2020, primarily due to more Aframax and MR vessels.
Another important factor that gained prominence in 2021 was the so-called "rogue" or "subterfuge" tanker fleet. These are mostly old and poorly maintained vessels that are engaged in clandestine trades, such as the shipment of U.S. sanctioned Iranian and Venezuelan oil (" Back To The Negotiating Table " - 9 April 2021). This fleet, estimated to be around 150 vessels (mostly tankers) is widely considered to be a drag on the tanker market.
Other events that made headlines in 2021 included the closure of the Suez Canal and the cancelation of the Keystone XL pipeline. While both events were impactful in their own right, their immediate impact on the tanker market was limited. The same applies to all the regulatory changes that were being discussed and will be implemented to combat shipping's Co2 emissions. New regulations will be a major driver of changes in our industry in the years to come. Over the next 6-12 months, old-fashioned supply and demand will drive rates. The tanker market is in a better position than this time last year, but a lot of uncertainties remain, in particular as it relates to the impact of Covid.
