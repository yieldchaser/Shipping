## OPEC(+) decisions becoming more difficult and less impactful
This Sunday, June 2, the group of oil producing countries (OPEC+) will convene to discuss production volumes of the cartel going forward. As always, the meeting (via video conference, not in person) has three possible outcomes: further production cuts, maintaining the status quo or lifting output. While the tanker market, in particular the VLCC segment, would welcome a production increase, a decision to maintain the current production levels seems more likely. However, with OPEC, the situation is never as simple as it seems. In this weekly opinion we will discuss some of the complexities facing the cartel (some of which are self-inflicted) and how this may impact the tanker market going forward.
A recent opinion article in Bloomberg highlighted some of the challenges facing OPEC. The production cuts itself are not straightforward. There are "official" cuts affecting most members, "voluntary" curbs that involve only a handful of nations and so-called "compensation" cuts that are imposed on members that did not make the official cuts or did not fully implement the voluntary cuts. Some of the cuts are based on crude oil production, while others are using exports to measure compliance. Russia negotiated a unique position: it can choose to apply the cuts to a mix of crude oil and refined products. Some countries, for one reason or another, are not covered by production cuts at all: Libya, Venezuela, and Iran. Libya is still recovering from its Civil War and Venezuela and Iran are subject to U.S. sanctions. Combined, these three countries currently produce 5.35 Mb/d, not an insignificant volume.
And then there is the widespread cheating that is always a problem within OPEC+. There can be a wide discrepancy between the official numbers that the OPEC members publish and the real production or export volumes. Most countries have overproduced at one point or another. At the moment, it appears that Iraq, Russia, the UAE and Kazakhstan are producing more that their quota allows. The IEA reports that Saudi Arabia also exceeds its quota. On the other hand, there are a limited number of producers, like Nigeria and Azerbaijan, that are unable to meet their quota.
At the end of the day, OPEC faces a difficult balancing act between price and volume. It needs to assess global oil demand, inventories in the various regions, non-OPEC production as well as the geopolitical landscape. Most OPEC+ members are highly dependent on oil revenues to balance their budget. If they want to increase revenues, they need to push up the price of oil and/or sell more barrels. However, global demand for oil is finite and non-OPEC producers will sell as much as they can based on the market price. OPEC is the balancing producer and satisfies that part of global demand that is not covered by non-OPEC producers, hence the term: "Call on OPEC" used in some forecasts. To some extent, OPEC+ can try to increase prices (and revenues) by cutting production and exports. However, in the short-term, this could invite more cheating from producers with spare capacity and in the long- term, higher prices will bring on more production from Non-OPEC.
So, how does all this impact the tanker market? The many variables and uncertainties that we discussed before have make it increasingly difficult to interpret how any particular decision will impact tanker employment and rates. It used to be simpler: Middle East exports used to dominate OPEC prior to 2016, when the "+" countries were added to the mix. As can be seen in Chart 1 above, VLCCs used to dominate OPEC exports and any change in production or exports would have a disproportionate affect on the large tanker market. However, the inclusion of Russia, Kazakhstan, and Azerbaijan, among others, dramatically changed the picture (see Chart 2). These countries predominantly use Aframaxes and Suezmaxes for their exports and their combined exports of 6.5 - 7.0 Mb/d are enough to make a difference in the overall tonnage utilization. The various types of production/export cuts and exemptions are making it difficult to give a clear answer as to where the market might be heading after an OPEC decision. On top of that, there is a group of countries that don't adhere to their quota and/or are subject to sanctions. Some predominantly use the dark fleet for their exports, further obfuscating the impact on the tanker market.
