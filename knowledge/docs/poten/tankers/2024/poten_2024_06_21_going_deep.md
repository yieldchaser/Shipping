## Deepwater port development will help VLCC demand
Over the last decade, the U.S. has developed into the largest crude oil producer in the world. This is largely due to the shale oil developments in Texas and some other states. As most shale oil is very light crude and most of the U.S. Gulf refineries were designed for the heavier grades prevalent in the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico, the U.S. exports significant volumes of crude oil and condensates, while also importing heavier grades. Additionally, U.S. refineries on the West coast and East coast continue to import crude oil as they often don't have easy pipeline access to these shale crudes.
Due to this rapid oil production growth, the U.S. Gulf export infrastructure has had trouble to keep up with the rapid export growth, especially for shipments using the most economical tankers for long haul trades, Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCC). As the U.S. Gulf coast is relatively shallow, no normal ports can accommodate fully laden VLCCs. The only exception is the Louisiana Offshore Oil Port (LOOP), which was designed as an offshore oil import terminal and upgraded in 2017 to also allow exports. However, the port is far removed from the most prolific shale oil production regions in the Permian and additionally, the use as export terminal is complicated by the pipeline configuration which requires the emptying of the line when switching from imports to exports.
As VLCCs cannot be used in most of the export ports, smaller ships, such as Aframaxes and Suezmaxes are used to shuttle cargoes to VLCCs that are waiting in open water and offload the cargo via Ship-to-Ship (STS) operation, so called reverse lightering. Two terminals, Enbridge Ingleside Energy Center (EIEC) and South Texas Gateway Terminal (STGT), actively accommodate VLCCs to first load a part of the cargo and then use one smaller tanker to move the remaining cargo to the VLCC offshore. As it would be more efficient to directly load VLCCs, several companies have developed plans to build offshore deep- water ports that can fully load VLCCs, without need for lightering.
Four different projects are currently being considered and at different stages in the permitting process. The most advanced project, Sea Port Oil Terminal (SPOT) completed the permitting process in April 2024. These projects are all planning to use Single Point Mooring systems (SPM) in deep water off the coast that are connected by pipeline to a storage terminal on shore. However, we believe that the market will only be able to accommodate one or maybe two of these projects.
We created three shipping scenarios for VLCC voyages from the US Gulf to the Far East to analyze the transport economics of these projects: 1. VLCC loaded using 3 lightering cargoes, 2.
Loading in port with 1 lightering top-off, and 3. Fully loading at a deepwater port.
For the transportation cost analysis, we used the average of 2023 freight rates, lightering costs and demurrage rates. For the first scenario we assumed three Aframax lightering voyages and 12 demurrage days for the VLCC resulting in total costs of $5.31 per barrel. For the second scenario we assumed that the VLCC loads at EIEC followed by one top-off Aframax cargo with 3 demurrage days for a total costs of $4.68/bbl. For the third scenario, using a new deepwater port, the total transportation cost would be $4.19/bbl, excluding the extra fees for the recovery of the costs of building the port. A new deepwater port would likely first replace the most expensive option using multiple lightering voyages and to a lesser degree replace the voyages loading in port with a top-up lightering voyage.
According to Vortexa, the U.S. exported 5.0 million barrel per day (Mb/d) of Crude oil from the U.S. Gulf between January and May of 2024. VLCCs accounted for about 42% (2.1 Mb/d) of these exports and about 80% (1.68 Mb/d) of these VLCC exports were bound for Asian destinations.
It will likely take several more years until the first deepwater terminal is operational, but once it is ready, VLCC voyages will become more competitive, especially for the shorter voyages to Europe where smaller vessel types are currently more often competitive. On the other hand, Aframaxes will lose the reverse lightering voyages.
