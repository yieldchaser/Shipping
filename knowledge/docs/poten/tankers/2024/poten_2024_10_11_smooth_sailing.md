## Taking stock of the TMX expansion
The Trans Mountain Expansion (TMX) pipeline exported its first crude oil cargo from the Westridge terminal in Burnaby, British Columbia, in May of this year. Now, some 5 months later, it is time to take a preliminary look at the impact that this important new export outlet on the west coast of the Americas is having on the oil and tanker markets in the Pacific. Did the oil flows change the way we anticipated, i.e. is the crude going where we thought it might go or have we seen unexpected developments? Let's dig into the details and see what patterns have emerged so far.
A quick primer for those readers that are not familiar with the TMX pipeline. This pipeline has been around for a long time. The Trans Mountain Pipeline Company was established in 1951 after significant oil deposits were discovered in Alberta in the late 1940s. Construction of the pipeline began in February 1952 and the first shipment reached the terminal in Burnaby in October 1953. The initial capacity of the pipeline was 150,000 b/d. Over the decades, the pipeline was expanded serval times and by 2008, the capacity had grown to 300,000 b/d. However, oil production in Alberta continued to grow and in 2012, Kinder Morgan Canada announced plans to expand TMX to 890,000 b/d. The project ran into significant delays due to opposition from indigenous and environmental groups. Faced with massive cost overruns, Kinder Morgan sold TMX to the Canadian government. In May 2024, the project was finally completed. Infrastructure constraints restrict the export terminal to the use of Aframax tankers. These restrictions were expected to have an impact on the competitiveness of Canadian crudes in long-haul markets such as China and India, relative to short-haul destinations on the U.S. West Coast.
Prior to the expansion, most of the 300,000 b/d of crude oil that was flowing through the TMX ended up in refineries in the Pacific Northwest (via pipeline). Seaborne flows out of Vancouver were small (30-40,000 b/d) and most of those were also destined for the U.S. West Coast. The volume of seaborne exports changed dramatically when the TMX expansion became operational (Chart 1). From January - April 2024, seaborne crude oil exports from Vancouver averaged 44,000 b/d. In May, it reached 79,000 b/d and it jumped to 323,000 b/d in June. The highest level to date was 366,000 b/d in August. Based on the capacity of the TMX expansion (580,000 b/d), there is still significant upside to these volumes, but it is not clear if and when these numbers will be reached.
As expected, the additional TMX exports have found a home in both Asia and on the U.S. West Coast. In Asia, China is the largest destination of Canadian barrels (> 120,000 b/d in JunSep 2024), with occasional volumes going to India and South Korea. The U.S. West Coast takes about 165,000 b/d, which is about 130,000 b/d more than pre-TMX expansion. By comparing the last four months (June-September) with the period from January 2021 to May 2024, we can see who gained and who lost market share on the U.S. West Coast (Chart 2). Imports from Iraq, which were considered at risk due to the similar crude qualities of Basrah versus Western Canadian Select, have been stable. Imports from Panama (likely a combination of Guyana and Ecuador) are up and so are Argentina and Brazil. Lower volumes are coming from the United States (ANS crude from Alaska is down 57,000 b/d) and Saudi Arabia (down 80,000 b/d).
For some of the charterers, scheduling seaborne exports out of the Westridge terminal was a new and to some degree challenging experience. However, they all figured it out, using a combination of export voyages. The exports to the U.S. West Coast are all done direct on Aframaxes, as one would expect. The trades to the Far East are approached differently, depending on the destination, relative freight levels and the availability of tonnage. Initially, a fair number of Aframax cargoes destined for the Far East were reverse lightered into VLCCs at the Pacific Area Lightering (PAL) off the coast of Southern California to take advantage of the economies of scale of the larger vessel. While this still happens now, currently more Aframaxes are making the direct voyage to the Far East. Overall, the startup of the TMX expansion has been smooth, without any major glitches. It has increased Aframax employment in the Pacific, without creating shortages or a spike in freight rates. We expect that further increases in flows from Vancouver will also be handled smoothly.
