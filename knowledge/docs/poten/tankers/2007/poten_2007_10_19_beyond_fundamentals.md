## Beyond Fundamentals
It appears that those who initially predicted the days of $100 barrels of oil may soon be able to say "told you so." Back in April 2005, we wrote a Weekly Opinion entitled "$100+ Per Barrel Crude: April Fool?" This Opinion focused on a report wherein Goldman Sachs analysts predicted price levels of such magnitude. In the past few weeks the price of crude broke into the $80 per barrel price range, above where it had generally remained for most of the second quarter, before pushing its way toward $90 per barrel. The chart below shows the price for West Texas Intermediate (WTI) on a year-on-year comparison with 2006.

## WTI Prices
The scapegoat, for lack of a better word, for these price spurts has been "geopolitical tension" but it is difficult to attribute a near $20 run-up in the price of crude to this generally pervasive condition that has been present in the market for quite some time. Since mid-August, crude oil prices have increased approximately 25%.
A jump in prices as the result of political tensions would be logical if there was an imminent supply crunch in the future. Today, however, the opposite conditions appear to be present and many believe that the market is well supplied with crude oil.

## Stocks Speak
According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), crude stock levels for OECD countries have generally been increasing since March of this year and appear to have been at the top of the five-year range through the end of the second quarter. OECD stock levels are nearly a billion barrels, according to the most recently available data.
The most recent data from the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) suggests that although there have been stock draws in recent weeks, the United States still has essentially the same number of days supply as has been seen in 2007 to date, between 21- 22 days.

## Crude Stocks
Days Supply While it is likely that producers may increase production to meet the actual increases in demand going forward, there is little reason to believe that they will increase production simply as a mechanism to control prices.
When taking a fundamental approach to the market, it is easier to see that the price increase is likely driven by those in the market taking speculative positions. The general strength of commodity markets has attracted a wide-range of institutional investors and funds that have taken positions and used commodities as an investment instrument. For the sake of world economies everywhere, let us hope that this market does not become too much of an unstable house of cards.
