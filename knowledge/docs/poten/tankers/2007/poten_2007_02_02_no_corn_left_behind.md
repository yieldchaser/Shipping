## No Corn Left Behind
An accelerated growth trajectory was spotlighted in President Bush's State of the Union Address for the US ethanol fuel program. From an already mandated blending of nearly 500,000 barrels per day into gasoline by 2012, Mr. Bush proposed catapulting ethanol's inclusion to 2.2 million barrels per day by 2017. The chart below represents the expected ethanol content in US gasoline through 2017. Rather than join critics saying that production of the 2017 ethanol goal is impractical, it is more instructive to accept this goal as a fait accompli in order to envisage the landscape ten years hence. Considering that all presidential candidates must run a gauntlet of farm state primaries early in the critical formative stages of their campaign, it is not unreasonable to expect that future Presidents will also embrace blending more ethanol into gasoline.

## Farmland Use: What About The Chicken?
To jump from 2012's ethanol mandate to 2017's goal implies an impressive production growth rate of over 35 percent per year. The anticipated strains on land use and tax money to provide subsidies to distillers are also impressive. Ethanol productivity from corn is estimated to be around 370 gallons per acre. So to provide the incremental 27.5 billion gallon jump in ethanol production between 2012 and 2017, just over 74 million additional acres of suitable farmland would be needed. Expressed differently, the acreage needed to satisfy 2012's mandate requires about 25% of the nearly 82 million acres devoted to corn production in 2005. The land needed to satisfy 2017's goal amounts to over 115% of the current corn farmland. Clearly, 2017's goal would divert significant amounts of farmland from feeding people and livestock to feeding automobiles. This change of focus will likely increase the prices for many other edible consumer goods. Given a subsidy of 51 cents per gallon, handouts to distillers in 2017 could jump some 14 billion dollars per year over those in 2012.

## The Gasoline Composition
From 2000 to 2005, US gasoline consumption grew at a rate of nearly 1.6 percent per year. That compounded rate, however, masks the growth spurt of 2.7 percent between 2001 and 2002 and the lethargy of 0.6 percent between 2004 and 2005. Applying a conservative one percent per year growth rate from now through 2017, consumption could reach 10.3 million barrels per day.
By 2012, ethanol is projected to contribute about 5 percent volume to gasoline. That portion translates to about half the pool being E10 - gasoline with 10 percent ethanol. 2017's goal implies an ethanol contribution to the pool of over 20 percent which implies the production of both E10 and E85 (85 percent ethanol content). Given the projected gasoline consumption, as the volume of ethanol in the gasoline pool rises, its hydrocarbon portion naturally declines.

## US Gasoline Supply
Expected Hydrocarbon Content The implementation of President Bush's ethanol vision could quickly reduce the hydrocarbon component of gasoline to below their present volumes. Hence, refiners now contemplating additional hydrocarbon-based gasoline production might pause to reflect on the implications of this vision on investment returns. For refiners pushing to meet the US's burgeoning diesel demand after 2012, the risk of a product imbalance in the market can be daunting. Refiners will be forced to either scale back on total utilization or risk producing surplus gasoline.
While the practical balance between ethanol and gasoline in the future remains unclear, opportunities may arise for both clean and dirty tanker operators. Gasoline and diesel imports may rise as refinery capacity investments wither in the face of rampant ethanol production. Alternately, refineries may continue to run at high levels making diesel and gasoline surpluses that will be available for export.
For either scenario, US imports of crude oil will likely continue to grow as domestic crude oil production is projected to continue its decline. But the rate at which crude oil imports grow depends on which scenario plays out. Only time will tell the nature of the opportunity to tanker owners. But since change breeds opportunity - bring it on.
