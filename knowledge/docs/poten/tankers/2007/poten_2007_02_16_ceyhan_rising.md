## Summary
Ceyhan Rising
Bereft of traditional Iraqi Kirkuk crude oil barrels by the US invasion of Iraq, liftings from Ceyhan withered from their bustling activity of the late 1990's. Despite the continuing unreliability of the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline, this port's lifts are exhibiting signs of rebounding lifting activity. ## Ceyhan Rising
Bereft of traditional Iraqi Kirkuk crude oil barrels by the US invasion of Iraq, liftings from Ceyhan withered from their bustling activity of the late 1990's. Despite the continuing unreliability of the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline, this port's lifts are exhibiting signs of rebounding lifting activity. Key to this recovery is last year's introduction of Azeri crude oil to world markets through inauguration of the Baku- Tbilisi-Ceyhan (BTC) pipeline (see Poten Weekly Opinion June 9, 2006). With this pipeline's throughput increasing, sweet and relatively light Azeri crude oil is beginning to supplement the diets of refiners world-wide.

## Pushing Barrels
The nearly 1,100 mile BTC pipeline began filling its 10 million barrel working inventory in May 2005. Eleven months later, Ceyhan's first cargo of Azeri crude oil was loaded onto a tanker late last June. Flows through the BTC pipeline since the first parcel sailed have reportedly totaled some 10 million tons, which translates to an average daily rate of over 360 thousand barrels per day. Current expectations are that the pipeline will push throughput to 750 thousand barrels per day by the end of 2007 and then attain its intended one million barrel per day capacity during 2008.
The Crude Oil Doesn't Go Where You Might Think The terminal at Ceyhan accommodates vessels up to VLCC's, but the majority of spot fixtures since June 2006 have been on Aframaxes. As throughput has increased though, VLCC activity has also increased as 2 fixtures were concluded this week alone.

With refiners continually seeking sweet crude oils - especially those in the Far East - movements of crude oil to destinations East of Suez will continue. At a time of some uncertainty in the tanker market, this added demand has helped keep rates relatively firm. Whether there will be enough crude oil flowing to far off destinations to absorb the number of deliveries expected in 2008 and 2009 is open for debate, but it will take more than 10 to 15 large ship movements per year to have enough of an impact.
