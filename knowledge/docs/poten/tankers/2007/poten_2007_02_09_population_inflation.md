## Oil and Growth
Robust economies, particularly in certain emerging markets, can be linked to population growth. While all countries aim to improve their economies, the priorities each maintains can vary greatly. Newly industrializing nations are working to build infrastructure any way possible and may be less concerned with energy efficiency. Other nations may be politically driven to improve energy efficiency through the use of new technology, or a movement away from an industrial to service economy.

## GDP (Thousand $) per Barrel of Oil Consumed
Those countries that experience a lower amount of GDP per unit of oil, specifically those to the left of the $1 million GDP mark, might be more inclined to sacrifice petroleum based energy in favor of alternate sources of energy. Those that experience a high percentage of GDP per unit of oil would be less likely to give it up.
It should be noted that the four right-most countries on the graph- Philippines, China, India and Bangladesh- are also those with the lowest per capita GDP of the represented countries. At current staggering nominal growth rates, China can expect to see its population increase by nearly 7.8 million people per year while India's population will see an increase of more than 18 million per year. At this rate, India's population should surpass China's by the year 2030.
In the near term, rapidly growing populations and economies will increase the reliance on crude oil and petroleum products in these four countries. Furthermore, it is not clear how any of these countries may actually adhere to the Kyoto Protocol.
Today the per capita consumption of oil in China and India is still relatively low compared to other countries. As these economies develop, and consumption increases as it has in other industrialized nations, consumption in India and China should expand even further, creating a patter of strong demand for petroleum East of Suez.
So while the overall population growth has been impressive, the growth in daily oil consumption from 21.3 million barrels per day in 1960 versus today's 85 million barrels per day is equally impressive. With these types of growth rates, maybe the amount of new tankers coming on line in the next three years is not so hefty after all.
