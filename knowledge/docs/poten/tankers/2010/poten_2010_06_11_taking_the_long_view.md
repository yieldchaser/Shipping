## Taking the Long View
As the company deals with an array of short‐term challenges related to the disastrous oil spill in the U.S. Gulf of Mexico, BP briefly turned its attention to the long‐term this week with the release of its annual Statistical Review. For the 59 th year, BP has provided the energy industry with an overview of up‐to‐date statistics on production and consumption of various energy sources in markets all around the world. This year's report and analysis focuses on the search for lessons in the aftermath of 2009, a tumultuous year for the international economy as well as oil markets worldwide.
According to the 2010 Statistical Review, the first contraction in world trade since the Second World War led to a 1.1 % decline in primary energy consumption last year. As shown in the chart below, 2009 marked the first drop in energy consumption since 1982 and the largest percentage drop since 1980. Defined by BP as including use of oil, natural gas, coal, nuclear and hydro power, primary energy consumption fell in all regions, excluding the Asia‐Pacific and the Middle East. This contrast underscores a theme of last year's report which has only been reinforced by the events since: that the countries outside the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) will increasingly be the drivers of future demand growth.

## US: Leading Non‐ OPEC Growth
BP sees global oil consumption as having fallen some 1.2 million barrels per day last year, which is also the largest fall since 1982. Drops in oil demand were not consistent across regions, though, with OECD demand displaying the sharpest decline on record while oil demand in non‐ OECD countries continued to grow, albeit at a slower pace. This disparity helped lead to the second consecutive decline in global oil trade, according to BP, with the United States accounting for the majority of the net decline in imports.
On the supply side, sustained production cuts from the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) last year led to the group's most significant output decline since 1983. These cuts accounted for most of the 2.6% drop in worldwide oil production reported by BP, which occurred despite a slight increase in non‐OPEC production. Production increases in Russia, Brazil, and Azerbaijan contributed to this growth, but the largest source of non‐OPEC supply growth last year was the United States.
The United States increased its oil production by 460 kb/d in 2009, a 7 % increase from the previous year, as shown in the chart below. BP credited developments in the Gulf of Mexico for most of this increase. Recognizing that this may make some uncomfortable in light of recent events in the Gulf, BP reminded that the central importance of such complicated development projects were "a piece of the reality in which we all live."

## Thousand Barrels Per Day
Last year's plunging demand for oil helped to make it a landmark year which many in the energy and tanker markets would like to forget. Although many of the figures related in this year's Statistical Review serve to remind just how challenging conditions have become, they also contain important reminders of long‐established trends that will support oil and tanker markets in the future. History teaches us that although there are likely to be bumps in the road; demand growth is set to continue its positive trajectory as the international economy continues its recovery.
