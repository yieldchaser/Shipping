## Unwinding Subsidies
Alongside China, India has emerged as an important driver of many of the trends expected to determine the direction of future oil and tanker markets. Its rapidly‐growing economy has been important in shifting the center of oil demand growth eastward. Additionally, its export refineries have impacted both regional and international refined product trade flows. In a development likely to churn future oil and tanker markets, in late June, India moved to begin phasing out its subsidies for transportation fuels. The decision is an important step for balancing the Indian economy and may eventually provide an example for other countries looking to make similar changes in the years to come.
It is strong economic growth over the past decade which has helped transform India into an important oil consumer and refined product exporter. Strength in India's economy is expected to continue going forward, with 9.4% GDP growth projected for this year according to the updated economic outlook released by the International Monetary Fund earlier this week. Demand for crude oil is also expected to continue its growth; from 2.7 mbd this year to 3.5 mbd by 2014, as shown in the chart below. India's removal of subsidies for gasoline is part of an effort to control the effects of its demand growth and could mark the beginning of an important transition for the entire Indian economy.

## Diversification to Follow?
One of the most important motivations for the liberalization of gasoline prices in India has been a desire to minimize the economic effects of government subsidies in light of India's recent demand growth. While the Finance Ministry acknowledged that the removal of subsidies would likely bring about an increase in inflation in the near term, the change is also expected to help reduce India's $5.5 billion oil subsidy bill. Fuel subsidies represent about 2.5% of government spending and there is hope that these savings will play an important role in helping India meet its goal of cutting the budget deficit to 5.5% of GDP from almost 7 % last year.
The change is also expected to boost profits at India's state‐owned refiners, who were previously required to charge prices dictated by the government. While India's state‐owned refiners are expected to see more than $20 billion in losses from these subsidies this year alone, the nation's private refiners have been able to shift their focus to international markets. The removal of gasoline subsidies will now allow state‐run refiners to raise the price of gasoline by about seven percent and has inspired plans by private and state refiners alike to expand their distribution networks inside the country.
India's crude oil imports have come predominately from the Arabian Gulf and West Africa, as shown in the graph below, which displays reported spot crude oil volumes into India by major load zone since 2007. As India's refiners are able to capture more profit in domestic markets that enables them to grow to their full potential, they are likely to return some of those proceeds towards securing access to future supplies, an effort in which they have often been outmatched by China in recent years. The Indian government has recently taken several steps to encourage its companies make acquisitions overseas, which could ultimately feed ton‐mile demand growth in the region.

## AG
CAR MED RS SEA WAF Government subsidies are intended to ensure that the poor have adequate access to fuel supplies, and the debate about subsidies reform in India has been a long and heated one. A previous attempt to remove subsidies in 2002 was derailed when it was ensnarled in election‐ year politics and the recent announcement has also been met with protest marches and strikes.
The announcement of India's liberalization of transportation fuel prices came on the eve of a meeting of the G20 in Toronto, where nations gathered were to discuss a previous pledge to phase out subsidies made last year in Pittsburgh. The communiqué produced at the recent G20 meeting called on member states to consider the development needs of vulnerable groups in developing country‐specific time tables to phase out inefficient subsidies. These and other important oil consumers are in various stages of their own efforts at price liberalization and the reform of government subsidies. India's recent action is an encouraging first step on a gradual path to a more market orientated economy that will likely provide an example for nations considering similar reforms.
The continued gasoline subsidies are thought to have encouraged wasteful consumption that is another target of the reform. In the short term, their removal could lead to a sharp enough drop in consumption to translate into a surge in Indian exports, some of which could find their way to markets in the U.S. It is more likely, though, that the changes set in motion by the removal of subsidies will unfold over the long‐term, as consumption patterns adjust to the new environment and India's refiners come into their own.
