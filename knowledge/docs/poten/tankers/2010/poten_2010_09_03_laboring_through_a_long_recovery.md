## Laboring Through A Long Recovery
The Memorial Day kickoff to the summer driving season offered hope that 2010 would see a step towards normalization of seasonal demand patterns for gasoline in the United States. Buoyed by indications of strengthening recovery in the global and domestic economies, conditions seemed poised for an uptick in gasoline demand that would mark an important milestone following the economic turbulence of recent years. As the approaching Labor Day weekend signals the end of driving season, prevailing sentiment about the economy has turned negative but the cautious hope which began the summer has been partially fulfilled.
Even at the beginning of driving season in late May, an oversupply of vessels muted expectations that an increase in summer gasoline demand in the United States would bring sufficient imports to produce a sustained rise in clean freight rates. While arbitrage economics prompted a flurry of clean fixtures into the United States from Europe for a period in July, it was stifled by excess clean tonnage in the sector once cargoes from the continent started to wane. Fundamentally, the influence of strengthening gasoline consumption on clean freight rates this summer was also blunted by substantial gasoline stockpiles. Gasoline inventories have risen above the seasonal average to 214 million barrels, as shown in the chart below, in which the blue band represents normal inventory range.

## A Long Process of Recovery
These inflated inventory levels can be attributed partially to persistently‐high utilization rates at domestic refineries this summer, which reached a high of 91.5% in July before beginning their traditional late‐summer decline. Those high utilization rates were partially a response to positive signals of U.S. demand earlier in the summer. While growth rates have slowed recently, gasoline demand has grown by about 1% over 2009 levels through much of this summer. The Energy Information Administration (EIA) has predicted that 2010 will end a four year streak of falling liquid fuels consumption, with gasoline demand expected to grow by 0.3 percent this year and a further 0.8 percent in 2011 . While these growth rates may be too modest to counteract the negative news on the overall economy, they will be an important step in the long‐term recovery of refined product demand in the United States, and in turn, the clean tanker market.
As mentioned above, it had been expected that overcapacity would limit the impact the summer driving season would have on freight rates this year. There was, however, a period in July when a surge in gasoline exports from Europe brought earnings on the benchmark Rotterdam- New York voyage to a recent high of more than $17,000 per day, as shown in the chart below.

In the absence of a sustained support from clean imports into the United States, refined product deliveries to West Africa have helped keep the Transatlantic MR market from reaching last year's lows this summer. As demand recovery in the United States continues in the years to come, imports to help cover driving season gasoline demand are expected to reassume their role as primary market driver during the summer months.
