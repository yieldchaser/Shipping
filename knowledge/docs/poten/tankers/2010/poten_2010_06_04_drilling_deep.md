## Drilling Deep?
While it has been evident for weeks that the Deepwater Horizon oil spill is a transformative event for the entire energy industry, a widespread sense of confusion following the announcement of a six‐month moratorium on deepwater drilling in the Gulf of Mexico leaves its near‐term impact largely unclear. If, as some fear, the moratorium drags on past the current six‐ month timeframe, it could exacerbate the United States' long‐term dependence on imported oil and have potentially significant consequences for the development of regional tanker trade flows.
On May 27, President Obama announced the cancellation of a recently‐announced lease sale off the coast of Virginia, a delay of plans to drill for oil in Arctic regions and a six month moratorium on drilling in greater than 500 foot water depths within the Gulf of Mexico. While the moratorium did include an important distinction which will allow drilling in the Gulf's shallow waters to continue, the administration said that the temporary ban was necessary to ensure the safety of future deepwater operations. The delay comes at a critical time for the development of shallow as well as deepwater resources, which was expected to reach peak production in the years to come, as shown in the chart below, based on projections released before the spill.

## Confusion Reigns
The sense of uncertainty which has greeted the announcement of the moratorium reflects the likelihood that attempts to "pause" deepwater operations in the Gulf for six months could result in longer delays. Waiting for a report and list of recommendations by a commission appointed by the President could prolong the ban, especially as the political storm surrounding the spill continues to build as mid‐term elections approach. In the midst of a still‐recovering economy and high levels of unemployment, the most potent source of opposition to the moratorium is likely to be its impact on employment in the areas surrounding the Gulf, already hurt by damage the spill has brought to the region's fishing industry.
The moratorium is expected to effect 33 drilling rigs, 22 of which are currently employed in the Gulf of Mexico with the remainder scheduled to work there some time during the next six months. The rippling legal consequences of disrupting contracted deepwater operations in the Gulf could drag on for the foreseeable future. Earlier this week, a publically‐listed operator of offshore support vessels vowed to defend the enforcement of existing contracts in the Gulf of Mexico. The company said it would resist any attempt to declare force majeure as a result of either the Deepwater Horizon spill or the moratorium while raising the possibility of relocating some of its vessels to markets overseas.
The tightening of regulation likely to result from the disaster could make future deepwater exploration in the Gulf of Mexico more expensive, complicating the decision‐making of companies considering long‐term investment. Growing concerns that delayed production and tightened regulations enacted after the disaster could inflate the price of oil in years to come may have already been reflected recently in futures markets. Contracts for oil to be delivered in July 2015 have seen their premium over promptly‐delivered supplies rise from $7.68 per barrel before the explosion to $15.28 per barrel today, as shown in the chart below.

These developments all threaten to undermine a plan to use domestic drilling to help mitigate dependence on foreign oil announced by the President in March. Deepwater production was an important piece of that plan and an extended delay could result in as much as 400 kb/d in reduced domestic production, according to the American Petroleum Institute.
The effect of the moratorium on operators in the region will be different from company to company and at this point, the most important variable for predicting challenges and opportunities likely to spring from the aftermath of the Deepwater Horizon is the length of the production stoppage. If the delay of existing production stretches past six months, it could begin to produce an increase in imports that would boost long‐haul ton‐mile demand.
