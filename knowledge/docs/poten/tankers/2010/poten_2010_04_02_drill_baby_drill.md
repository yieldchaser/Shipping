## Drill, Baby, Drill?
Yes, but this time it's not Sarah Palin. In what feels like a sharp contrast to his pre‐election mantra, this week saw President Obama announce an end to the prohibition on oil and gas exploration in selected coastal areas of the United States. While claims that this expansion of domestic drilling will bring the United States closer to "energy independence" can be dismissed as political posturing, the change is a positive first step for the oil and gas industry that has been largely marginalized since the new administration took office. Could the United States be on the doorstep of a feasible energy policy?
This week's announcement opened previously restricted areas off the East Coast of the United States and the eastern portion of the Gulf of Mexico to oil and gas exploration, as shown in the map below. While opening access to those areas, the administration simultaneously restricted oil and gas drilling in the Northeast, the Pacific Coast and within 125 miles of Florida's shores. Interestingly, the Administration also moved to cancel some scheduled lease sales in Alaska while allowing others to go forward, identifying additional areas of that state which will be the subject of further study and protection.

## Same as the Old Boss?
In 2008, Congress allowed a decades‐old ban on offshore domestic drilling to expire. The President's announcement this week effectively served to revive an effort spearheaded by the Bush Administration following the end of the drilling moratorium. Obama's announcement allows the scheduled sale of licenses to explore a triangular area 50 miles off Virginia's coast to move forward. The Department of the Interior estimates that the area may hold as much as 130 million barrels of oil and 1.14 trillion feet of natural gas. The sale of permission to explore for those resources will be the first new domestic exploration leases sold outside of the US Gulf for more than 20 years. Pending environmental approvals, the sale is expected to be completed by the Bush Administration's original target date of November 2011 (though the Interior Secretary has raised the possibility it could be delayed into 2012). Next, the Obama Administration will develop its 2012 - 2017 Drilling Plan, which will dictate the conditions governing exploration in other areas of the United States.

## True Impact to Come
Exploration and development of oil and gas resources in the regions opened by Obama's announcement, contingent as they are on environmental assessments and local approvals, will likely take years to materialize. Offshore reserves are currently estimated to hold between 39 and 62 billion barrels of oil and between 168 and 294 trillion feet of natural gas.
While this could add up to around four years of consumption on a net basis, it is important to remember that the daily contribution from the fields could end up being fairly small on a practical basis. That said, existing estimates of domestic offshore reserves are based on surveys conducted more than 30 years ago. Until seismic studies using modern equipment are completed, the true magnitude and impact of opening these resources will remain largely unknown.

## A Step Forward
Aside from a desire to create desperately needed jobs, Obama's belated embrace of offshore drilling is partially intended to win the support of Republicans and reluctant Democrats for broader energy legislation. A version of the energy bill passed the House last year before stalling in the Senate, where a new compromise version is expected to be considered in coming weeks.
This week's announcement is an important step in the right direction for the national debate about energy policy, but it is important not to exaggerate its potential to affect our import dependence. The announcement's real significance is that it suggested recognition on the part of the Obama Administration's that the transition to cleaner fuels will be impossible without the participation of the traditional oil and gas industry. Whether or not the concessions announced this week will be enough to engender that cooperation remains to be seen.
In the years to come, the tanker industry will watch these offshore seismic studies closely, waiting to see whether new domestic production will back out long‐haul imports to the United States. What is more likely is that new production will simply serve to offset some of the declines in existing domestic fields.
