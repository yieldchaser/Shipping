## Up Against the Wall
Last month, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued its response to a March 2009 petition from a group of fuel ethanol promoters and producers to allow bringing to market gasoline containing up to 15% ethanol by volume (E15). The response came in the form of a carefully worded waiver and promptly drew howls of complaints from both proponents and opponents of more ethanol blending. The clatter of discontent from both aisles surrounding this issue left some people wondering what exactly was gained by the EPA's expenditure of time and taxpayer money to arrive at the decision.
The EPA's partial waiver allows, but does not require, light‐duty motor vehicles from model year (MY) 2007 and newer to use E15, but does not allow the use of E15 in these vehicles built in MY 2000 and earlier. A decision on the suitability of E15 in vehicles from MY 2001 through MY 2006 was deferred pending the review of data from tests currently being conducted. To reach its partial waiver, the EPA evaluated E15 on four criteria: tailpipe emissions, evaporative emissions, material compatibility, and the ability to drive and operate the vehicles. Not surprisingly, this waiver came with conditions attached. One of these conditions requires that parties involved in the manufacture and sale of E15 submit plans to reduce the potential for mis‐fueling.

## Shared Unhappiness
One thing parties on both sides of the issue seem to have in common is their displeasure with the waiver. Some ethanol proponents bemoan the waiver for only MY 2007 and newer vehicles as a lost opportunity to reduce the nation's dependence on foreign oil as well as a chance to create unelaborated economic opportunity. While these proponents decry the partial waiver as a scientifically unjustified fragmentation of the US car market, so far their carefully gathered data on the suitability of aged vehicles to E15 does not seem available. Without that data, any criticism of the EPA's move appears to be self‐serving and baseless.
Opponents of E15 raise questions of how E15 might increase food prices as it pressures feed‐ corn prices. They also wonder how much water and air pollution would increase as a consequence of using of more fertilizer and pesticide to grow more corn on more land converted into cornfields. But irrespective of any E15 use, previously mandated increases in the levels of corn‐based ethanol blended in gasoline are bound to bring about more pollution anyway. Additionally, opponents voiced concerns that older vehicles and engines in motorcycles, boats and outdoor power equipment might react adversely to E15. In a perfect world, the yet‐to‐be specified EPA conditions to minimize mis‐fueling would, of course, work perfectly. But in the real world and in light of instances of past fueling around by consumers, concerns of mis‐fueling have a tinge of prescience. The foregoing concerns were only a brief sampling of the pitfalls opponents see in E15.
Come what may for E15, it seems clear that the hydrocarbon component of future gasoline is threatened with decline by the mandated onslaught of both corn‐based and non corn‐based fuel ethanol. ## Avoiding the Wall
The average gasoline consumption last month across the US was in the vicinity of 9 million barrels per day. This rate was used to gauge how the percentage of ethanol will change with its coming increases in volumes blended. In actuality, improvements in fleet average vehicle per gallon mileage stands to reduce gasoline consumption, but motorists' increased mobility and ethanol's lower heat content stand to negate any gains. From the preceding chart, if all US gasoline were produced as E15 by 2015, a new blending wall would likely be hit soon afterwards. This estimate, however, does not account for consumption by FlexFuel vehicles capable of using gasoline with up to 85% ethanol (E85). Efforts to have more FlexFuel vehicles in time to drain the coming volumes of ethanol away from the conventional gasoline pool could be potentially more helpful to avoiding collisions with future blending walls than merely petitioning to raise ethanol's limit in conventional gasoline.
Repercussions of the EPA's partial waiver have yet to impact tanker owners. Much depends on how MY 2001 through MY2006 light‐duty vehicles respond to E15. Another presently unknown is how motorists able to use E15 will weigh its price and lower miles per gallon against that of E10 gasoline. There also is the need to mesh the assurances of the EPA that newer vehicles meet their E15 test criteria with car manufacturers' warranty on newer vehicles. Directionally, though, putting more ethanol in US gasoline reduces the need to both import crude oil and gasoline stocks into the US. But for now, it is difficult to envision the quick adaptation of the nation's gasoline distribution and marketing infrastructure to serve up E15 even if the EPA's waiver conditions were resolved tomorrow. While the juggernaut of ethanol blending may still face a de facto wall, tanker owners will likely ride the tides of continuing oil imports into the US for awhile to come.
