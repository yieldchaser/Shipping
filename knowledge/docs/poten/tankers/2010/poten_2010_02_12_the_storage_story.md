## The Storage Story
Use of tankers as floating storage has seemed to consistently capture the attention of those in the tanker and broader oil markets for over a year. In the early part of 2009, crude oil tankers were the bearers of most of the storage activity, but as the year continued clean product tankers made a significant emergence into such employment. The right combination of freight environment, spot commodity prices and future sentiment seem to create the perfect recipe for tanker demand. Sustainability of such a balance, however, can prove to be more of a challenge.
Throughout 2009, crude oil stored on tankers fell from levels of over 80 million barrels to current levels of about 25 million barrels. Clean product floating storage reached its peak in December 2009, when 127 vessels were reported for such employment, for a total of about 80 million barrels.
Today, about 57 million barrels of clean refined products ply waters worldwide waiting to find a home in the market. The chart below shows the historical storage trends for the past few months, highlighting the scale of clean product storage relative to crude oil. Recent movement in clean storage activity, specifically the release of some charters, jostled the market in January but is this a trend that can be expected to continue?

## Clean Storage
Dirty Storage In January, freezing temperatures across North America, Europe and Asia gave a boost to distillate demand. At the same time, spot freight rates for clean product tankers began to rise, creating short‐lived optimism among ship owners that may have otherwise renewed storage charters. With product moving through the system, bullish signals rippled through the supply chain.

## Still room to contango
Although the spread between spot and future prices for distillate has narrowed significantly, opportunities will likely persist for traders and shipowners interested in storage plays. This current narrowing can be seen as the result of the uptick in near‐term demand for product, but material recovery in worldwide demand for industrial, heating and transportation fuels will be required in order to change the structure of the curve toward backwardation on a more permanent basis. In fact, it would seem that the release of product from floating storage into a fairly saturated market would further deepen the contango, especially as the northern hemisphere heads into warmer weather.
