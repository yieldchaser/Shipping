## Appreciating the Gradual
By some accounts, the gridlock on the east side of Manhattan mirrored the pace of negotiations inside the United Nations this week, as China and the United States staked out opposing positions on a number of recent issues . The annual meeting of the U.N. General Assembly in New York coincided with Chinese and American interests diverging on issues including North Korea, territorial disputes in the South China Sea, and sanctions on Iran. However, it was China's currency, the Renminbi, that took center stage in bilateral talks as President Obama reportedly devoted the majority of his meeting with China's Prime Minister to insisting that it be allowed to further appreciate. The issue's prominence was likely a result of political pressures stemming from the upcoming mid‐term elections as Chinese imports can be seen as displacing American domestic products. In reality, China's exchange rate may ultimately have only a moderate effect on the American economy.
In June of this year, China ended its policy of maintaining the value of its currency at a flat exchange rate against the dollar and introduced a managed floating exchange rate of +/‐ 0.5% per day. In the time since this moderate loosening of control, China's currency has appreciated by less than two percent, as shown in the chart below. As seen this summer, the slight appreciation of the currency allowed thus far has done little to spur positive change in the United States.

## Jobs, Jobs, Jobs
The U.S. trade deficit with China was $227 billion last year and could reach as high as $267 billion this year by some estimates. As shown in the chart below, US imports from China have increasingly outpaced U.S. exports to the country for years, with 2010 on track to continue a trajectory interrupted by the recession of 2009. An appreciation of the Chinese Renminbi is a part of the re‐balancing of the international economy called for by many economists. It is moreover a testament to the country's ever‐growing industrial muscle.
A recent report from the Economic Policy Institute projected that China's currency controls and resulting trade deficit could displace about 540 thousand American jobs this year. On a practical basis, reluctance from China to cooperate with respect to the currency wishes of the West could result in extreme measures such as the U.S. Commerce Department imposing trade sanctions.
The most significant of the retaliatory measures proposed as responses to continued inaction on China's currency would impose tariffs that would have widespread impact of trade of all kinds. Successfully pressuring the Chinese government to sharply raise the value of their currency risks the possibility of social disruption and economic stagnation as was seen in Japan following its rapid re‐valuation of the Yen. Facing these undesirable likely outcomes, this is an issue that is perhaps best framed by the long‐term challenges facing both countries.
The currency valuation dispute highlights China's unique position as a developing country with a de‐facto role as an economic leader. As policymakers in Beijing attempt to balance these demands, it is important to consider that a gradual appreciation of its currency is a step towards a transformation into a more‐domestically‐focused Chinese economy. While it is true that manufacturing jobs in the United States have declined as the U.S. has come to rely on Chinese‐ produced goods, the structural challenges facing the American economy are more complex than China's careful management of its exchange rate.
As a result of China's growing prominence in international oil markets, issues surrounding it's currency valuation could have a significant impact on the tanker business over the years to come. Considering the role China has played in sustaining tanker demand throughout the economic disruptions of recent years and the importance of its continued economic growth for the industry's future, the tanker market would be well served to support China's gradual approach.
