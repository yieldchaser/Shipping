## Driving Towards Normality
The American summer driving season that typically kicks off with Memorial Day weekend is a period when oil and tanker markets traditionally anticipate an uptick in refined product demand. For each of the past two years, however, the durability of this seasonal demand surge has been tested by extraordinary conditions in oil markets driven largely by conditions in the economy. In 2008, traffic volumes were depressed by oil prices that hovered near $130 per barrel and last year demand was sapped by lingering effects of the then‐recent economic melt‐down. This year, signals of increasing refined product demand have arrived just as summer is getting under way, raising the possibility of a move towards a normal driving season.
The Automobile Association of America (AAA) expects approximately 32 million people to travel at least 50 miles from home this Memorial Day weekend; a 5.4 % increase from last year and the first such increase since 2005. AAA expects 87 % of these trips to take place in automobiles and the number of motor vehicle travelers to increase by 5.8 % from last year. If AAA's expectations prove accurate, they will join a number of other indications that suggest a normalization of travel patterns from the anomalies of the past few years. According to the US Department of Transportation , there was a 2.3 % year‐on‐year increase in travel on all roads and streets in March of this year. That increase supports an expectation that total miles travelled will continue to recover from the decline which began in 2007, as shown in the chart below. The restoration of gasoline demand hinges largely on the confidence and spending of the American consumer.

## Priced to Move
Pressured by the European debt crisis, a strengthening dollar and various geopolitical concerns, recent weeks have seen the price of crude oil drop sharply; from an average of about $84 per barrel in April to the current level of $74.55 per barrel. The Energy Information Administration (EIA), reports that April witnessed a stabilization and modest turnaround in gasoline consumption in the United States. As the crude oil sell‐off has brought down gasoline prices, it has fueled hope that recent signals of increasing gasoline demand will continue through the summer. Consumer prices remaining near current levels would still be an increase from last year, but would nonetheless offer relief from the prices confronting Memorial Day drivers at the pump in other recent years, as shown in the circled prices in the chart below.

## Premium
Regular Current Price Premium Current Price Regular Whether incremental demand is met by imports or higher output from domestic refineries is generally determined by a delicate equilibrium between freight rates and refinery utilization. In years past, the seasonal uptick in refined product demand that accompanies summer driving season could be relied on to lend some support to freight rates for clean tankers. This year, a well‐supplied tanker market makes it less likely that freight rates will receive such support.
On the refining side, seasonally high stockpiles and the recently‐increasing rate of refined product production in the United States have dampened expectations that this summer's driving season will lend support to tanker freight rates. After battling back from a recent low of 77 % in January, US refinery utilization has averaged 87.3 % since April 1 st , rising alongside indications of increased domestic demand. Despite recent draws, stockpiles of both gasoline and diesel remain above seasonal averages and the percentage yields for those products suggest that some high conversion domestic refiners have shifted their slates to produce more ultra‐low sulfur diesel. While some of this product has been used to meet a welcomed increase in industrial demand here in the United States, a struggling European economy limits the chances of volumes on transatlantic trade routes increasing significantly.
The decline in motor‐vehicle distance travelled since 2007 is the result of more than just recent years' gyrations in the oil market. The statistics reflect changes in transportation patterns which also stem from other economic and demographic factors. While current market conditions are likely to limit the direct impact of the summer driving season on the tanker market this year, indications of a normalization of traffic patterns and a return towards a more normal summer driving season can only be seen as a good thing.
Here's hoping the American public has a reconnection with its favorite pastime!
