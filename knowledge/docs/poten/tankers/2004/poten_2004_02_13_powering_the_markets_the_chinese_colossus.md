## Powering the Markets: The Chinese Colossus
On Tuesday February 10, the Norwegian-American and Hellenic- American Chambers of Commerce held their tenth annual joint shipping conference in the New York Helmsley Hotel. This year's conference entitled "Powering the Markets: The Chinese Colossus," focused specifically on the economic, financial and legal factors within China. China is fueling shipping demand across the entire industry. The conference drew well over 150 shipowners, charterers, lawyers, brokers and others. This year's conference was co-chaired by Blaine Collins, vice president and regional manager for North and South America of Det Norske Veritas and John Stratakis, a partner with Poles, Tublin, Stratakis, Gonzalez & Weichert LLP. Opening Remarks Yuelin T. Yang, director of corporate development of IMC Pan Asia Alliance, opened the conference, reminding us that Norwegians and Greeks dominate shipping because, as he said, they "have shipping in their blood." He then quickly brought the focus back to China. As Napoleon noted, "Let China sleep, for when she wakes, she will shake the world," China is now wide awake and shaking the world. He provided several examples of why this is true.

The Dry Bulk Shipping Effect Anthony Whitworth, chief executive officer of Navios Corp. discussed, "Measuring the Impact of China on the Dry Bulk Markets." Mr. Whitworth said China's colossal importing capacity is tempered by congestion in its ports. He spoke about the implications of communism on China's ability to grow into a world economic power and quipped, "The Chinese are Communists the way the French are Catholics," taking a line from a journalist for The Atlantic Monthly . In 2003, China consumed 21% of the world's trade aluminum; 25% of its zinc; 29% of its iron ore; 19% of its copper; and 24% of its stainless steel. He cited new demand for Panamax bulk carriers fueled by China's need for coal and agricultural products like soybeans (49 new vessels for coal and 40 for soybeans). The Chinese consumer is driving growth. China surpassed Germany this year in light vehicle sales to become the third largest car market in the world. He said besides port congestion, other factors might conspire to put a kink in China's growth such as inflation and commodity prices influenced by unforeseen circumstances such as terrorist attacks. Robert Shaw, executive vice president and legal counsel of Navios Corp. moderated the next panel called "Dry Bulk Markets Panel Discussion," which included Angeliki Frangou, director of Franser Shipping; Calypso Nomikos, director of AM Nomikos Transworld Agencies; Gary Vogel of VOC Shipholdings VC; and Anthony Whitworth. Shipbuilding in China The next panel titled "Shipbuilding in China" was moderated by Andy Westwood, vice president and regional manager for China for Det Norske Veritas, who spoke about China's orderbook. He said ship orders increased by 2.5 times last year compared with 2002. Mr. Westwood spoke of China's plans to develop the world's largest shipyard in Shanghai and plans to build 50% of the world's ships by 2050. China has the most shipyards in the world, though most are currently struggling with by poor productivity and inability to meet owners' specific requirements. The nation is the third largest shipbuilder in the world behind Korea and Japan.
Magnus Granrud, director/partner of O-J Libaek & Partners spoke on the "Negotiating and Building" panel citing China's enormous shipbuilding potential. As an aside, Granrud mentioned that China has many jobs to fill while the US has unemployed workers. Even after Chinese laborers are trained to work in a shipyard and leave to find work in Japan, he said, there are still millions of workers left to replace them. Capt. Graham Westgarth, president of Teekay Marine Services, Ltd. and Chris Williams, director of Graig China Ltd. and Graig Shipping Group also gave their views of the ship building market in China during their panel discussion entitled, "Shipyard Capacities & Capabilities: Designs, Risks and Quality." Cross the 'T's'; Dot the 'I's' The conference then turned to banking, finance and legal issues in China. The next panel titled "China Business: Developments in Banking and Financing" was moderated by Jim Lawrence of Marine Money International. Peter D. "Dicky" Yip, chief executive of China business for HSBC spoke about China's developments in financing to support its shipping industry. Mr. Yip said China's involvement in the World Trade Organization since Nov. 2001 is forcing its legal system to become more transparent to foreign investors. Yip also cited China's GDP growth rate at 7% per year, while expected growth rate is 8% a year. Dagfinn Lunde, a member of the board of DVB Bank AG, also participated in the discussion and said Chinese banks were eager to finance shipbuilding projects. Paul Kontoyannis, director of Investments and Finance Ltd. spoke next about Greek ship orders in China. Greek orders represent 11% of the Chinese orderbook. More than $1.9 billion is committed to shipbuilding in China. Mr. Kontoyannis mentioned a few hurdles to ordering ships in China including communication gaps and the required payment guarantee by a shipowner, which Korean and Japanese yards do not request.
