## Summary
What If
Oil prices are "down" to the high $40's and everyone is breathing a sigh of relief. There are some important things we can think about: § new refineries in India and China have been built for medium to sweet crude; § there is limited spare oil export capacity for medium to sweet crudes; § spare oil export capacity is concentrated in sour crudes; and § refineries that process sour crudes are operating at maximum sustainable capacity. We might be at the crux of a supply demand crisis.

## What If
Oil prices are "down" to the high $40's and everyone is breathing a sigh of relief. It might be premature. There are some important things we can think about: § new refineries in India and China have been built for medium to sweet crude; § there is limited spare oil export capacity for medium to sweet crudes; § spare oil export capacity is concentrated in sour crudes; and § refineries that process sour crudes are operating at maximum sustainable capacity. We might be at the crux of a supply demand crisis. If demand for medium or sweet crudes continues to escalate or if the supply of sweet crude falls such as from cessation of Nigerian exports, prices can climb back to the mid-$50's, or higher. High oil prices adversely affect economic activity. The oil crisis of 1973 set off a global economic retrenchment. As consumers pay more for oil, there is less money to spend on goods. Companies seeing their sales decline do not spend money on capital improvements. A fall in consumer consumption and capital spending means only one thing for the global economy: retrenchment. A few economists have opined that an era of high crude prices in the $50- $60 per barrel range would cut world economic growth rates in half. What If oil prices remained in this range over the next ten years? What would this mean to tanker owners? We have a model for projecting tanker demand based on oil prices and economic activity. Using a statistical relationship derived from historical data going back to 1981, assumptions of future growth in economic activity and oil prices can be used to obtain a projection of oil flows; and from that, we can derive future tanker (Vlcc) demand. However the projection is no better than the underlying assumptions. The model also assumes that we hit no natural constraint on export capacity, something that we ignored in the past, but perhaps can't in the future. We have assumed two scenarios. One is low oil prices and economic growth rates in line with IMF projections and past average rates. The other is for high oil prices with economic growth rates cut in half. North America North America oil import demand is net of domestic production in the United States, Mexico, and Canada. Low oil prices and economic growth in line with the past average of 3.5% shows a resumption of oil imports after the bursting of the dot.com economy, comforting for producers and tanker owners alike. But if a hike in oil prices cuts economic growth in half, oil imports flatten out like a pancake!

## Europe
Oil imports into Europe is net of production in the North Sea, Russia and the Caspian Sea region. In the past, oil imports fell as North Sea production rose. Looking into the future, North Sea production is in decline with rising oil production in Russia and the Caspian Sea region. The model projects improving economic activity in Russia independent of the international price of oil, which increases its consumption of domestic crude, but not enough to stop the rise of exports.
Europe is different than North America and Asia in that the connection between oil consumption and economic activity is relatively weak as seen in its past oil consumption being relatively flat despite modest increases in economic activity. Nevertheless oil imports are slated to increase despite increasing crude production in Russia and the Caspian Sea region because of falling North Sea production and modestly rising demand. But, as expected, this rise is diminished in a world of high oil prices and sluggish growth in economic activity.

## Asia
Domestic oil production in Asia is essentially flat with gains in China and elsewhere compensating for declines in Indonesia. Indonesia, an OPEC member, is about to become a net oil importer from falling production and rising consumption.

## Vlcc Demand
The model assigns flows of oil imports to various oil exporters in the Atlantic basin. Any deficit is satisfied by AG exports. Vlcc demand is based on the number of vessels required to handle AG exports plus a portion of West African exports plus backhauls to Asia with minor Vlcc employment serving the Middle East pipelines terminating in the Mediterranean, including the BTC pipeline currently under construction, plus servicing the Sumed pipeline. Demand for Vlccs for both the low and high priced crude oil scenarios follows.

## # Vlccs
It is not the price of oil itself that matters, but the impact of oil prices on economic activity. High priced oil basically flattens out Vlcc demand whereas low priced oil encourages economic activity and, consequently, Vlcc demand. However in examining the growth of oil imports, particularly in Asia, one begins to wonder if the implicit assumption of an infinite supply of oil is realistic. Present additions to oil are barely keeping up with growth of the order of 1 million bpd per year. The BTC pipeline of 1 million bpd coming on stream next year only compensates for one year's worth of incremental growth in oil consumption. Present expansion plans of oil exports in North and West Africa can handle another two or three years of incremental growth. What happens after that? Hitting supply constraints will be a new experience for oil consumers and tanker owners alike.
