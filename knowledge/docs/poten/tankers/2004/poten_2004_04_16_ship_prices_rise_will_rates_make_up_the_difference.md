## Ship Prices Rise: Will Rates Make up the Difference?
What every ship owner knows post the 1973 Middle East oil crisis is that he really never knows. And with VLCC newbuilding prices the highest they've been in nearly 10 years, wild cards such as an oil crisis are decidedly less palatable to the taste. Last year, with record high rates, volatility worked in the owners' favor. But whether rates will meet the expectations of shipowners in the future remains to be seen. Owners looking to build a ship are considering three things: increase in oil demand (the US EIA predicts global demand for oil will rise by 40 million bpd over the next 20 years compared to the current world consumption of 77 million bpd), the development of new trading routes (West Africa to the Far East) and China's demand, so long as it remains. Peering into the future is not easy. While a decent time charter provides a secure income for an owner in the long run, playing the spot market under the present high conditions is likely to be more profitable - but for how long? Looking at the orderbook and measuring growth in annual ton/mile demand against it and the existing fleet is the best benchmark an owner has. Currently, high steel prices and ship demand are causing newbuilding price quotes to skyrocket. The lack of steel in some shipyards of the three major shipbuilding nations is causing newbuildings for 2007 and 2008 to be delayed. Some shipyards are not accepting any more ship orders beyond late 2007 or 2008 delivery because of the lack of berth availability, insecurity stemming from the dollar's weakness, as well as uncertain steel cost. All of this is contributing to higher newbuilding prices. In addition, LNG ships, container ships and bulk carriers are in greater demand which also taxes shipyard space and steel availability. A Sample of Ship Prices In 1999, a VLCC cost around $70 million to build. This month, the newbuilding price for a VLCC is $86 million for a 2007 delivery. Current ship supply/demand is balanced since double-hull ships are not overbuilt and will be needed to meet upcoming environmental regulatory requirements. The fact that yard space is lacking also helps to keep ship supply more predictable. In spite of rates being low, VLCC prices were high from 1995 to 2000, as seen in the graph below. One reason is because in the early 1990s, owners looking to come into compliance with environmental laws ordered the first batch of double hull ships. Current market rates for VLCCs (WS 97 last month AG to Far East which equaled $61,000 in monthly earnings) are expected to decline to lower levels, with seasonal change.

## Second Hand Prices
China May as Well Build Ships, They're Taking all the Steel Steel prices have increased by $140 during the past two years as shown below. China's need for steel is blamed - or alternatively credited - for the increase. The steel needed to build bridges, roads and housing to accommodate the millions of people relocating to China's urban areas from remote rural villages has sent scrap prices above $400 Ldt from the low $100s/Ldt approximately two years ago.

## US$/ton
And the Other Vessel Classes? Suezmaxes, Aframaxes and Panamaxes are seeing the same type of price increases. A Suezmax newbuilding is quoted at just over $57 million this month, the highest quote in the past decade. Aframax quotes are up to $47 million this month from $36 million in April 2000. Panamax newbuildings are quoted at $37 million from $27 million two years ago.

## Three Nations Remain on Top in Shipbuilding
Naturally owners want to make sure their investment in a newbuilding is profitable and hope that rates will be high. Shipbuilding prices may decline with more competition from Chinese shipyards, as was seen in the early 1990s when Korean shipyards moved into the picture. This of course depends also on steel price softening and the strength of the US currency situation. Three nations are still holding their own as the top countries for shipbuilding with South Korea beating out Japan and China by far for first place. They also take top dollar for their ships. Hyundai delivered a VLCC for $82 million in 2003.
