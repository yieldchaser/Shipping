## Naphtha Seeks Tonnage
For Voyages to Exotic Places Naphtha, unlike other clean petroleum products, has no retail market of any significance. No one buys a truckload to heat his house or a tankful to power his automobile. Until a few years ago, someone in your neighborhood might have bought a few tons at a time to fuel his F-16. But those days have gone. Now, all the fighter jets, even consumer models, burn kerosene. Only refiners, petrochemical companies, and the occasional power station or fertilizer plant want naphtha today. They don't buy their supply at a filling station. They usually get it delivered by ship.
Most of the world's naphtha doesn't trade. Refiners distill it from crude oil and immediately turn it into motor gasoline. But a very large amount remains for other ("merchant") uses. Last year, 2003, refiners and gas processors produced 596 mmtons (14.7 mmb/d) of naphtha. A little more than 341 mmtons (8.4 mmb/d) went directly to the gasoline supply. Petrochemicals manufacturing took three-quarters of the remaining 254 mmtons (6.3 mmb/d). Of that 254 mmtons, 76 mmtons crossed at least one national border on the way from producer to consumer. Now we get to the good part. 50 mmtons moved from one region of the world to another in a tanker.
This week's report comes from Poten's Feedstock & Petrochemical Department which will publish a new edition of Naphtha Prognosis , a comprehensive forecast of naphtha production, consumption, trade, and prices. We also offer Naphtha News , the industry's only weekly analysis of naphtha markets and pricing. For more information please contact the department at 212-230-2000.
In 2007, three years from now, production should reach 643 mmtons (15.9 mmb/d). Motor gasoline will take the first 375 mmtons (9.2 mmb/d) leaving 269 mmtons (6.6 mmb/d) of merchant supply. The amount trading internationally will climb to 85 mmtons with 58 mmtons traveling across major bodies of water. Best of all, from a ship owners' point of view, much of the additional inter-regional trade will require long voyages such as AG to east Asia and AG or Red Sea to NW Europe. Those inter-regional movements will invite use of Panamax and small Aframax tankers - ships the naphtha trade insists on calling LR1's and LR2's. These large clean ships took over long-haul Eastern routes during the last decade. Over the past two or three years, they've found some employment in the West, too.

## Long-haul
Short-haul The graph above shows naphtha traffic on long voyages increasing more than 5 mmt/y between 2003 and 2007. The LR's won't get all of that business. But they'll certainly do well east of Suez. As seen on the next page, three developments, more than any others, will shape international naphtha trade during the next three years. One continues a familiar trend. East Asia will need more than 15 percent more long-haul naphtha in 2007 than it did in 2003. The second makes immediate sense to anyone who pays a little attention to investments in refining and gas processing. Middle East countries will offer quite a lot of new naphtha supply between 2003 and 2007.
In fact, availabilities from the Arabian Peninsula and Iran will increase 31 percent over that four-year period. The third comes as more of a surprise. Europe will regularly require supply from distant sources. The clean tanker business may not find European naphtha imports all that remarkable. After all, 25 ktons naphtha Baltic/UKC or Black Sea/Med or cross Med or Med (north Africa)/UKC happens every day. Europe has depended on imports from its neighbors - north Africa, the Near East, and Russia - for decades. For almost ten years those neighbors have offered enough cargoes to meet Europe's needs and leave a few every month looking for homes in USA, Brazil, or Asia. The European naphtha trading zone - Europe, north Africa, the Near East, and Russia - ran a persistent surplus. That won't last much longer. As soon as 2005, Europe will want all the naphtha its neighbors can provide and then some. At least it will much of the time. Actually, the tanker business has seen hints of this European zone pivot from long naphtha to short. As recently as the first half of 2002, ten to twelve Handy-size cargoes sailed every month, usually from the Med, for Asia and other distant destinations. ## Atlantic Basin to European zone
Med/States continue, but not predictably and at an average level no higher than four to six Handys per month. The predominant flow of naphtha has shifted from West-to-East to East-to- West during the last two years. Med/Asia stopped dead in January and shows no signs of returning to life. Since May, surplus Mideast naphtha has sailed to the West. Most of the cargoes go to USA. But Europe takes one now and then. Over the next twelve to eighteen months, those proportions should reverse. Europe should become the predominant destination. Asia will remain the principal recipient of Middle East naphtha. But Eastern demand will not increase fast enough to take all of the export supply which will become available. A portion of Mideast sales will require somewhere to go in the West. We've seen the leading edge of this need to penetrate another territory during 2004. The East's emerging surplus and the European zone's impending shortfall complement one another. In 2003, the European trading zone - remember, that includes north Africa, the Near East, and Russia - produced over 68 mmtons of merchant naphtha and consumed a little less than 66 mmtons. The region had almost 3 mmtons to spare. Disposing of that excess required a long list of shipments through Suez to the East and across the Atlantic to the Americas. By 2007, production will approach 71 mmtons. Consumption will climb to 74 mmtons. The 3 mmtons to cover that shortfall will have to come from the Middle East, perhaps with a little help from the Americas and west Africa. Nowhere else has the supply to offer. The European zone's pivot from net exporter to net importer presents a interesting shipping problem. Not many European naphtha receiving facilities can accommodate large ships. Handy-size tankers have served this region for decades. Many terminals don't have the draft or suitable berths for ships 60 kDWT or larger. The ones which do often don't have sufficient shoretank capacity, at least not for naphtha. Shipments of 75 to 80 ktons faced limited sales opportunities this Spring, as a result. Only Rotterdam successfully took full cargoes of that size. That lack of options has forced costly two-port discharges. Unless receivers invest in tankage, access to deep berths, and other solutions to this problem, a Europe turning to long-haul imports may continue to employ small tankers much of the time instead of the more economical Panamaxes and Aframaxes. Small ships and large, new trade directions and old, this petroleum product offers interesting opportunities in years to come, even if no one buys any in your neighborhood.
