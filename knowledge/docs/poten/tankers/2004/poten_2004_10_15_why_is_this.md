## Summary
As tanker owners, we know what happens when demand gets ahead of supply- tanker rates go up and they go up a lot. Right now Vlcc rates are at a historically high WS 220 AG/East. The oil industry is experiencing the same phenomenon of demand getting ahead of supply with current oil prices hovering close to $55 per barrel.

## Why Is This?
As tanker owners, we know what happens when demand gets ahead of supply- tanker rates go up and they go up a lot. Right now Vlcc rates are at a historically high WS 220 AG/East. The oil industry is experiencing the same phenomenon of demand getting ahead of supply with current oil prices hovering close to $55 per barrel. Speculators are anticipating higher prices, as they correctly had for the past six months as seen by the growing open interest of crude oil futures.

## Vlcc Fixtures Up
Saudi Arabia has stated for months on end that it will increase production to get control over prices. ## WS Rates
AG/East AG/West Higher fixture activity in the Arabian Gulf shows that Saudi Arabia and other producers have increased exports starting in 2003, yet this increase in exports has not been sufficient to cut prices nor slow speculation in oil futures.
It Is Not Low U.S. Crude Inventories U.S. crude inventories are down from the cessation of U.S. Gulf offshore production and the interruption in offloading Vlccs from the hurricanes. The interruption to offloading Vlccs was transient. About one-third of U.S. Gulf production was shut in by the hurricanes, but this has been essentially restored. Nevertheless this has caused crude inventories to remain flat, whereas, in comparison to other years, crude inventories should be building up in anticipation of the winter heating season. SPR is a considerable 662 million barrels, still slowly growing.

## (Excluding SPR)
While replenishing U.S. crude oil inventory puts pressure on both the oil and shipping markets, it doesn't come close to explaining record high oil prices and shipping rates.
It Is Not Growth in OECD Oil Demand Total OECD oil consumption, which includes North America, Europe, Japan, Korea, Australia, and New Zealand averaged, according to the IEA, nearly 48 million bpd between 2000 and 2002. This expanded by 0.7 million bpd in 2003 and another 0.4 million bpd during the first half of 2004. This is not sufficient to spike both the oil and the shipping markets. We have to look elsewhere for an explanation.

## The Only Explanation Left
We have no place else to look except India and China, both booming economies. Of the two, China is the greater and its consumption of copper, steel, iron ore, coal and other commodities has been a boon to suppliers and its exports and imports of goods have been a boon for container vessel owners. As to oil imports, the IEA has upwardly revised its estimates of China's oil consumption repeatedly. The question is how much of the incremental OPEC production is being consumed and how much is being put away for a rainy day? If inventory accumulation is occurring in China and elsewhere, then when the tanks are full, there is going to be a day of reckoning for those long in oil futures. The problem with this scenario is an apparent lack of sufficient tank capacity to accumulate huge volumes of inventory. Only a few VLCCs have been fixed for floating storage. If China is consuming incremental OPEC production, and if it continues to do so, then we may have crossed over to a world where demand is consuming the entire world's supply of oil. If true, forget about the oil prices falling; rather look forward to rising prices until they reach a level that dampens global oil demand. This will then give supply a chance to catch up with demand putting pressure on oil prices. Economies can cool rapidly if we look at the speed that Southeast Asian economies contracted during the Asian crisis in 1997/98.
Where Do We Go From Here? If crude oil supplies have been tapped out, then there will be limited growth in the number of cargoes regardless of Chinese demand. This could lead to still higher oil prices, which of itself, can begin to cool the global economy. Moreover by the end of 2005, another 40 Vlccs will be delivered, which net of a few scrapings, represent 8% growth of fleet supply. A tapping out of oil supplies, a potential cooling of economic activity in China and elsewhere, and growth in Vlcc supply means continued high rates for a while and then a general retrenchment depending on the timing and degree of any adjustment to the world or to China's economy.
