## January tanker trade data indicates strong compliance
OPEC Cuts Down Tanker Demand Fig. 1: OPEC Crude Oil Exports It appears that the whole world (at least the people that are interested in oil supply and energy prices) is watching OPEC output to see if the member countries are adhering to the crude oil production and export cuts that they promised in meetings in September and November 2016. We are curious too, particularly because of the potential impact this may have on the tanker market. To see if we can determine any trends or draw some early conclusions we analyzed the seaborne crude oil movements out of OPEC in January 2017 and compared this to data from earlier periods in 2015 and 2016.
The 'Algiers Accord', named after the location where OPEC members agreed on September 28, 2016 to cut production, (detailed member allocations were agreed in November 2016) was implemented in January of this year. From a shipping market perspective it is important to note that the agreed OPEC cuts are production cuts, not export cuts, so changes in seaborne trade (or lack thereof) may not fully reflect the degree of compliance with production cuts. Some of the larger oil producers have the ability to switch their domestic fuel mix or utilize oil in (floating) storage to temporarily keep exports elevated while at the same time reducing domestic production.
Saudi Arabia is by far the largest and most important OPEC member and therefore watched very closely. APEX data from Lloyd's List Intelligence shows that Saudi exports declined 233,000 barrels per day (b/d) from December 2016 to January 2017. The decline from September 2016 is more than 500,000 b/d.
Other (smaller) OPEC producers around the Arabian Gulf, like Kuwait, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates have all reduced shipments, in line with their apparent commitments under the production agreement.
Iraq, OPEC's second largest exporter, shipped 3.8 million b/d in January, approximately 134,000 b/d below the December 2016 level. This decline in Iraqi exports is significant, not so much because of the volume (which is relatively small) but because this is the first time since early 2015 that we observe a decline in Iraqi shipments. Iraq has been on a tear in recent years, increasing shipments by a million barrels per day between January 2015 and December 2016. Even though many analysts remain skeptical about Iraqi compliance, it is likely that the recent reduction is a result of the OPEC agreement.
Nigeria, Libya and Iran are not subject to production cuts and while the first two countries managed to ship more crude in January of this year (Libya: +63,000 b/d, Nigeria: +210,000 b/d), volumes from Iran were down 370,000 b/d compared to December 2016. The reduction from Iran may be temporary and traders think that February exports could be higher as Indonesia will take its first shipment. However, other sources indicate that Iran may be experiencing some trouble finding new customers for its oil after ramping up its exports rapidly following the lifting of sanctions in January 2016. Iran is also planning to increase petroleum product exports as it refurbishes its refineries. This would divert crude oil away from exports to the domestic market.
Angola's exports have trended down since early 2016, and the country's state oil company Sonangol appears committed to reduce production as part of the OPEC agreement. However, recent production increases from new deepwater offshore fields should put upward pressure on production in Angola, which surpassed Nigeria as Africa's largest producer in Q1 2016.
Looking at total OPEC shipments in January 2017 (excluding the countries that are exempt from the Algiers Accord), we see a 1.19 million b/d reduction in aggregate, which is in line with the promised production cuts. So far, therefore, OPEC seems to have achieved a high compliance rate with the announced production cuts. Compliance from certain non-OPEC countries, which also committed to production cuts, is more uncertain at this point. We will continue to monitor oil production and movements to see if compliance remains high. Tanker owners hope to see some cheating.
