## Diverging trajectory for two unconventional sources of crude
American shale is back. Production is going from strength to strength on the back of increased drilling activity and significantly reduced breakeven prices compared to 2014/2015. Even at current prices, which are hovering around $50 per barrel, shale oil can be profitably extracted from fields in the United States. As a result, the immediate future of shale oil production looks bright. The situation for Canada's oil sands is quite different. Back in 2014, when oil prices exceeded $100 per barrel, Canadian producers were doing very well and both cash flow and capital spending in the energy sector reached record levels. After oil prices collapsed in late 2014, investments were curtailed. From 2014 to 2016, capital spending in Canada's oil and natural gas sector declined by an estimated $50 billion, or 62%, the largest two-year decline since the Canadian Association of Petroleum Producers (CAPP) started tracking this data in 1947. The numbers for 2017 are expected to show a small pickup in Canadian investment. The diverging paths of these two key non-conventional sources of crude oil can have a significant impact on trade flows and tanker employment in the coming years.
Shale oil is one of the key drivers of U.S. export growth since the crude oil export ban was lifted at the end of 2015. In 2016, U.S. crude oil exports averaged 520,000 barrels per day (b/d), 55,000 b/d (12%) more than in 2015, despite a year-over-year decline in production. Data through April 21 shows that U.S. exports average 766,000 b/d in 2017 to date, a remarkable 246,000 b/d (47%) increase over last year's number. As shale oil production is expected to increase further in the coming years, we expect exports to rise as well. Average export volumes exceeding 1 million b/d are possible later this year and next, especially if oil prices remain above $50 per barrel. Many oil companies, including majors such as ExxonMobil, as well as private equity funds (who reportedly invested $19.8 billion in Q1 2017 alone) are spending money in U.S. shale. Investment in pipelines and coastal terminals is also increasing, further facilitating exports. While Aframaxes and Suezmaxes remain the dominant export vessels, the use of VLCCs - especially to destinations in Asia - is increasing. This week, it was reported that an oil trader loaded a cargo of Bakken crude on a VLCC in the U.S. Gulf destined for Singapore. The imminent startup of the Dakota Access Pipeline will give Bakken producers additional pipeline access to the U.S. Gulf and export markets.
In Canada, oil production increased from 4.28 mb/d in 2014 to 4.71 mb/d in Q1 2017, despite lower oil prices and a significant reduction in investment in the industry. While individual shale oil investments south of the border in the U.S. tend to be relatively small with a quick payoff, Canadian oil sand projects have a long lead time and require large upfront investments, similar to deepwater offshore projects. Some oil sands projects that were started several years ago are only now coming on stream. Once on-line, it is imperative for the producer to maximize output to earn a return. However, the lower oil prices have hurt the relative economics of oil sands, and several oil companies, such Shell, Statoil, Total and ConocoPhillips have reduced their exposure in Canada, mostly selling to Canadian companies such as Cenovus, Canadian National Resources and Suncor. The reduction in investment will start to impact Canadian production in 3-5 years.
Another factor that has discouraged additional investment in the oil sands sector is limited infrastructure. Currently, almost all Canadian crude is exported to the U.S., although small volumes are then re-exported from the U.S. Gulf. Some relief may be in sight however. The Keystone XL pipeline has been revived and the Canadian government has approved some projects and is evaluating several others that would raise export capacity on Canada's east and west coasts.
In the medium to long term the oil production trajectory in the U.S. and Canada is likely to diverge. While shale oil can be developed cheaper and more quickly, it also has a high decline rate. Oil sands production is much slower to come online, but production levels remain stable for many years.
