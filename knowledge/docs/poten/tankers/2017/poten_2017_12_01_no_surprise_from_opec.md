## Oil Producer organization extends production cuts
To nobody's surprise, OPEC extended its oil production cuts on November 30. The original cuts, which were agreed exactly one year ago on November 30, 2016, were for 6 months (January - June 2017) and were subsequently extended for 9 months (through March 2018). The new agreement will run through the end of 2018. The volume of the production adjustments remains the same: 1.2 million barrels per day (mb/d) for OPEC members and approximately 600,000 b/d for the participating non-OPEC countries led by Russia (300,000 b/d). According to the OPEC press release, "the market rebalancing has gathered pace since May, with the OECD stock overhang falling to around 140 million barrels (mb) above the five-year average for October, a drop of almost 140 mb since May. Moreover, crude in floating storage has also fallen significantly over this period. Despite this success, the Conference reiterated that it was vital that stock levels be drawn down to normal levels." The only real news in the OPEC announcement was that (a) Libya and Nigeria are being brought back into the fold and will be subject to production restrictions, and (b) that OPEC will review the current deal in June 2018 to consider "the opportunity for further adjustment actions". OPEC has not (yet) formulated a formal exit strategy, i.e. how to unwind the cuts when they are no longer needed. In the past, OPEC production quotas would gradually unravel as compliance by member states reduced. Time will tell if this agreement will be phased out differently.
Against the backdrop of healthy oil demand growth and reducing stocks (in absolute levels as well as in terms of days forward demand), this agreement seems to be positive for the oil markets and supportive of oil prices. What this deal means for the tanker market depends on a number of factors. As we discussed in a tanker opinion last month (" Tanker Market Hits Peak Supply Growth "), the outlook for tanker supply growth in 2018 is more subdued compared to 2017 and the key driver for shipping rates will be growth in ton-mile demand.
The outlook for global oil demand in 2018 is fairly robust. The IEA expects world oil demand to grow by 1.3 mb/d in 2018, compared to 1.6 mb/d this year. If OPEC and participating non- OPEC countries stick to the agreement and keep their production flat, other (non-OPEC) producers will have to step up to the plate. This opens the door to significant further increases in U.S. production, in particular from tight oil fields. The IEA forecasts that total Non-OPEC supply will grow by 1.44 mb/d from 2017 to 2018. With an expected production increase of 1.08 mb/d, the United States will take care of 75% of the non-OPEC expansion and more than 80% of global demand growth. Other (smaller) growth areas include Canada (+230,000 b/d) and Brazil (+190,000 b/d). Many other producers are stagnant or face a decline in output.
While OPEC production is determined to a large extent by policy and politics, this is not the case for the producers in the United States. For production in the U.S. to increase by more than 1 million b/d, there needs to be a financial incentive as output is driven by the commercial decisions of a large number of individual oil companies. While the breakeven numbers for each of these producers vary widely, analysts estimate that the 12-month forward price for West Texas Intermediate (WTI), the U.S. crude oil benchmark, needs to be between $55 and $60 to encourage them to further ramp up production. These prices translate to $60-$65 for forward Brent and even higher spot prices, since the oil markets are currently in backwardation. If oil prices start to approach $70, it will become increasingly difficult for OPEC members with excess production capacity to keep to their quotas.
Increased OPEC production, driven by robust demand and higher prices, is exactly what the tanker market needs to break out of the depressed rate environment that it is currently trapped in.
