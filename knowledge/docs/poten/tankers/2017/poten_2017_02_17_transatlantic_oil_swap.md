## Product trade developments on transatlantic routes
Historically, the transatlantic product trade has been a significant source of employment for product tankers. The trade is also attractive because of a steady two-way trade so a tanker can typically achieve high utilization rates on these routes.
As the graphs on the right indicate, North American companies export surplus distillates to Europe while the Europeans export gasoline to North America. It is interesting to see how much the trades are dominated by these two commodities. In European countries, tax systems historically advantaged diesel cars for frequent drivers and in 2016, 49.5% of the new car registration involved diesel cars versus 45.8% gasoline powered cars. In the U.S. virtually all cars run on gasoline, diesel cars make up only about 3% of the car fleet. The IEA estimates that OECD Europe uses 1.9 million barrels per day (Mb/d) of gasoline versus 6.2 Mb/d of gasoil and diesel while the U.S. uses 9.4 Mb/d of gasoline but only 3.8 Mb/d of gasoil and diesel. This consumption pattern explains the trade pattern.
Total U.S. gasoline production is more or less in line with total consumption, but in some locations, it is easier to import gasoline than to ship it from domestic refineries.
In the U.S., regional energy statistics are often collected and analyzed by Petroleum Administration Defense Districts (PADD). PADD1 (Atlantic Coast) gasoline consumption has been more or less constant at about 3.2 Mb/d over the last 6 years and demand is not anticipated to increase significantly, primarily due to tightening fuel efficiency standards for U.S. car manufacturers. However, this could change if the automobile industry manages to convince the Trump administration to relax the Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards or not to enforce them.
In Europe, similar developments apply; middle distillate demand has declined steadily for several years before recovering in 2015 and 2016 due to lower oil prices and a slowly recovering economic situation.
North American product imports from Europe and the Med have gradually declined from 42.9 million tons per year in 2006 to an estimated 22.3 million tons in 2016 (see figure 1) as U.S. refineries became more competitive and expanded capacity through additions to existing refineries. The largest product exporters to North America are the UK (4.7 Mt in 2016) and the Netherlands (3.8 Mt).
On the other hand, North American exports to Europe (see figure 2) have increased from 2.8 million tons in 2006 to an estimated 13.8 million tons. The largest product importers in Europe are the U.K. (3.3 Mt in 2016) and France (4.4 Mt).
Analysts generally believe that refining margins will remain under pressure in the coming years and some anticipate that weaker refineries might close. Some of the European refineries are seen as likely candidates for closure or conversions to terminals. The impact of the IMO sulfur regulations taking effect in 2020 on the Heavy Fuel Oil (HFO) market could further affect the competitiveness of simple refineries in Europe. For example, in a recent report, OPEC forecasted another 1.0 Mb/d of refinery capacity closures in Europe by 2021. Refinery closures would increase the distillate shortage in the region and will be supportive for product tanker movements.
As the rate graphs show, freight rates are affected by sudden trade increases and declines, resulting in a temporary shortage or surplus of tonnage. However, the longer term trends are not mirrored by the freight rates as these are more a reflection of the global supply and demand of tonnage. In the earlier period of the graph, the Europe to North America trade commanded a significant premium over the backhaul, but the premium has slowly decreased as the trade gradually became more balanced.
It looks like the transatlantic product trade will remain a significant source of employment for product tankers in the coming years. If predicted refinery closures in Europe materialize, import requirements will receive a further boost, but most likely only after 2018.
