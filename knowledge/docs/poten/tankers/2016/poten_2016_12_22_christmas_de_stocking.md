## Oil from the world's largest Petroleum Reserve is for sale
In recent years, global oil markets have focused on the growth of the Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR) in China and - more recently - India as important drivers for crude oil imports and tanker demand. Relatively little attention has been given to the world's largest inventory of emergency crude oil, the U.S. SPR, stored in huge underground salt caverns along the coastline of the Gulf of Mexico. The U.S. SPR has a design capacity of 713.5 million barrels. The current inventory stands at approximately 695 million barrels, 266 million barrels of which is sweet crude, while the other 429 million barrels are sour crude. So far this year, there has been no movement in the SPR inventory, but this is likely to change in 2017. The Department of Energy recently announced that it will sell crude oil from the SPR as early as in January next year. The U.S. Congress has approved a number of sales and if all planned sales go ahead, the U.S. SPR could be reduced by some 190 million barrels between 2017 and 2025.
The idea of a national oil reserve traces its roots back to World War II, when Secretary of the Interior Harold Ickes advocated the stockpiling of emergency crude oil. Other efforts were made in 1952, 1956 (after the Suez crisis) and in 1970. However, it was the 1973-1974 oil embargo, when several Arab nations cut off oil flows to the United States, that triggered the establishment of the SPR. Exactly 41 years ago today, on December 22, 1975, President Ford signed the Energy Policy and Conservation Act, which mandated the establishment of an emergency reserve of up to one billion barrels of petroleum.
On July 21, 1977, the first oil - some 412,000 barrels of Saudi crude - was delivered to the SPR. The federal government accomplished the early fill of the SPR primarily through purchases on the open market and most of the oil for the reserve was acquired in the late 1970s and early 1980s. In 1994, President Clinton suspended direct purchases as a result of the federal budget deficit and in fiscal years 1996 and 1998, 28 million barrels were sold to reduce the deficit. In 1999, Clinton announced a new plan to replace these sales and resume filling the reserve up to capacity: the Royalty-In-Kind program. Through this program, which ran through 2009, the government accepted physical barrels as royalty payment for oil produced on the federally-owned Outer Continental Shelf. It became the primary means to fill the reserve.
The U.S. government has released oil from the SPR on several occasions. So-called emergency drawdowns have happened only twice: the first time in 1991 at the beginning of Operation Desert Storm to assure adequate global oil supplies during the war in the Persian Gulf; and the second time in September 2005, after Hurricane Katrina damaged and shut down oil production and distribution facilities in the U.S. Gulf. Both times, the release of crude oil from the SPR was part of a coordinated emergency response with the member countries of the International Energy Agency. While emergency drawdowns have been rare, other sales, including test sales (to check the readiness of the SPR), exchange agreements (loans to private companies) and non-emergency sales (like the deficit reduction sales of President Clinton) were much more frequent.
The upcoming sales are all in the non-emergency category: Sales under Section 404 of the Bipartisan Budget Act (BBA) are meant to fund SPR modernization efforts, while BBA Section 403 sales will go against reducing the federal budget deficit. Proceeds from sales under the Cures Act are meant to "modernize and personalize healthcare", and FAST Act sales will be used to fund U.S. infrastructure spending.
Analysts expect that the SPR sales will only have a limited effect on the oil markets although the timing of the sales is likely to pressure U.S. crude balances in the short term. The volumes are small, but the U.S. government is adding oil to the market at the same time that OPEC is trying to remove it.
The oil released from the SPR will likely stay in the United States. Through multiple distribution pipelines, the SPR is linked to many of the U.S. refineries along the U.S. Gulf Coast and these refiners are the most likely buyers of the SPR crude. To the extent that these refiners would otherwise have imported these barrels, the impact on the tanker market will be a small negative, even though the volumes (7-8 million barrels) are relatively small.
