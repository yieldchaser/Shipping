## Summary
Tanker market may receive a lump of coal from China
Clouds have been gathering above the tanker market for some time now. The orderbook for delivery in 2017, particularly for crude oil tankers, is high and the prospects for oil demand growth have been muted. The recently announced OPEC production cuts came on top of that.

## Tanker market may receive a lump of coal from China
Clouds have been gathering above the tanker market for some time now. The orderbook for delivery in 2017, particularly for crude oil tankers, is high and the prospects for oil demand growth have been muted. The recently announced OPEC production cuts came on top of that. In previous years, China has been a reliable source of oil demand growth and hence a strong supporter of the tanker market. According to our data, that trend continued this year with double digit growth in Chinese crude oil imports in 2016 to date. However, there is significant uncertainly about the prospects for Chinese demand growth in 2017.
Using the APEX data from Lloyd's List Intelligence we compared China's seaborne crude oil imports for the first 10 months of 2016 with the numbers for the same period of 2015. China's crude oil imports increased from 6.44 mb/d in 2016 to 7.20 mb/d in 2016, an impressive 760,000 b/d or 12% growth YoY. According to the latest IEA statistics, China's oil demand is up some 260,000 b/d this year (from 11.48 mb/d to 11.74 mb/d), a significant portion of which is coming from "teapot" refineries who were allowed to buy crude oil directly following a policy change last year. The additional import growth is mainly driven by reduced domestic oil production and stockbuilding. China's crude oil production in 2016 fell by 300,000 b/d from 2015, while knowledgeable sources estimate that China added 200,000 - 250,000 b/d to its Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR).
While the overall volume of imports increased by 12% this year, not all crude oil exporters to China benefited to the same degree. In the Middle East (the source of 53% of China's seaborne crude oil) all the main producers exported more, but Iran and Iraq showed the biggest increases in %-terms (16% and 15% respectively). Angola continues to dominate the supplies from West Africa, with The Congo a distant second. Gabon increased its exports by 50%, but still provides only 0.6% of Chinese imports. China imports virtually nothing from Nigeria. Given the distances and volumes involved, Middle Eastern and West African exports are almost exclusively done on VLCCs.
This year, exports from Asia/Australia to China increased by 46% to 1.1 mb/d, representing 15.7% of China's total imports to date (up from a 12% share in 2015). Big increases were recorded from Russia (Kozmino), Malaysia and Indonesia. In 2016, 83% of the Asian exports to China were done on Aframaxes.
Latin America provides about 12% of the crude imported by China. The primary exporters are Venezuela, Brazil and Uruguay and the dominant vessel class is the VLCC.
In conclusion, China has continued to provide significant support to the tanker trades in 2016. Despite this, the tanker market has been under pressure for most of this year.
In 2017, Chinese import demand growth will probably be more limited. Demand growth from the teapots is expected to pull back from the high levels of this year and stockpiling will be reduced as the SPR is filled close to capacity and oil prices are increasing. Market consensus expects import demand growth of 4-5% in 2017, less than half this years' growth rate.
A slowdown in Chinese oil demand growth will be bad news for a tanker market which is already facing multiple challenges next year.
On the supply side the market will need to absorb a significant orderbook next year. Shipyards are scheduled to deliver one VLCC and one Suezmax every week in 2017, leading to capacity growth well above the needs for fleet replacement and ton-mile demand expansion.
These factors will be exacerbated by the announced cuts in OPEC and non- OPEC production, which have already led to a significant rise in oil prices. While the initial impact of higher prices on oil demand may be muted, the production cuts (if implemented) will immediately reduce tanker demand.
In the oil and tanker market, anything can happen, but it appears prudent for the owners of large tankers to batten down the hatches, just in case.
