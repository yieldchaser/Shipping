## Main
On the first day of the National Day holiday every domestic price basis went dark at once - the DCE is shut and neither yuanspot nor the three port spot indices were quoted. Those fields in this report carry their last Sep 30 values and are marked accordingly. The only market actually trading was the seaborne one, and its signal is clear enough: the MMi 61% seaborne index fell to USD 93.00/dmt (-1.27%) and the 65% to 111.75 (-0.22%), with only IOCJ holding at 112.00 among the 12 mainstream seaborne fines; everything else lost USD 1.05-1.60. The distribution of those declines matters more than their size. Low grades fell hardest - SP10-1.94%, RTX Fines -1.75%, Jimblebar Blended Fines -1.73% - mainstream PB Fines lost just 1.11%, and 65% IOCJ did not move at all. This is not a sell-off in high-grade demand; it is a sell-off in holiday liquidity. Low grades depend more on domestic speculative and trade flows, so when China stops, their bid disappears first; high grades sit behind long-term contracts and the base blast furnace blend and hold up best through a break. The high-low spread therefore widened again on day one - the other side of the same coin as the port spot spread reaching 200 yuan/wmt on Sep 30. Offshore markets had in fact moved early: on Sep 30 the DCE rose 0.50% while SGX fell 1.43%. The two boards diverged because the domestic one was pricing pre-holiday sentiment and the offshore one was pricing post-holiday reality. Costs cut the other way: the average imported ore loss narrowed from 6.12 to 2.45 yuan/mt, an improvement of 3.67 yuan from a modest spot rise, a stronger yuan and lower freight combined. Our previous issue wrote that the first print after the holiday could recover ground; it has. But keep it in perspective: of the three drivers, the exchange rate reflects neither ore strength nor mill profitability, and if the yuan gives back ground the improvement goes with it. SMM still expects imported ore to run a modest loss. On inventories, Sep 30 brought the 10-port total to 103.02 million mt (+430,000 mt), but fines drew down (-77,100 mt) while lump (+284,200), pellets (+155,500) and concentrates (+59,900) built. Mills squeezing expensive supplementary feed while protecting the base blend says more than the headline total. On balance, direction during the break is set offshore and no new fundamentals are in play. Three things on the first session back will determine how far the domestic market has to catch down: the actual size of October's first hot metal decline, whether the first round of coke cuts lands, and how much ground offshore markets have lost over the eight days

### Market Commentary · Imported Ore

Futures & spot The domestic market is closed. China's eight-day National Day holiday runs Oct 1–8, the DCE is shut, and neither Qingdao port yuan spot nor the three port spot indices were quoted; those fields are marked “no quotation (holiday)” in this report and carry their last Sep 30 values (DCE 702.5 yuan/mt, PB Fines 665 yuan/wmt, MMi 61% port spot 674 yuan/wmt). Offshore markets kept trading: SGX most-traded settled at USD 93.50/dmt on Sep 30, down 1.43% on the day — the opposite direction to the DCE's +0.50% that same session. On Oct 1 the seaborne indices extended their decline, with the MMi 61% at 93.00 (-1.27%) and the 65% at 111.75 (-0.22%). Basis note: “prices as of Oct 1” applies only to seaborne products; the effective cut-off for yuan spot and port spot is Sep 30.

Drivers The holiday is itself the main driver this issue. SMM states plainly that China is entering an eight-day National Day holiday, liquidity may thin out during the break, and iron ore prices should stay soft and choppy near term. Offshore markets priced that risk first: SGX fell 1.43% on Sep 30 and seaborne fines fell 1–2% on Oct 1. No new bearish fundamentals emerged — hot metal, maintenance and shipment figures all carry over from Sep 30 — but no new bullish ones did either: the first round of coke price cuts remains rumour, to be tested after the holiday.

Demand No new demand data this issue; the Sep 30 prints still stand. Daily average hot metal across 242 mills was 2.3882 million mt (-6,100 mt WoW), the blast furnace operating rate 88.57% (-0.19 ppt) and capacity utilisation 88.15% (-0.18 ppt) — a fourth consecutive weekly decline from the 2.4080 million mt peak on Sep 2, with the last three drops widening from 3,700 to 4,800 to 6,100 mt. SMM's forward guidance still holds: with mills loss-making, some report they will draw up maintenance plans in early October and overall hot metal output may fall markedly. Maintenance losses are 1.6288 million mt this week (Sep 26–Oct 2) and a projected 1.6839 million next (Oct 3–9). The first hot metal print after the holiday is the key test of this cycle.

Inventory & Port stocks were updated to Sep 30. SMM's 10-port total reached 103.02 million mt, up 430,000 mt WoW, but the mix supply diverged: fines drew down to 82.275 million mt (-77,100 mt) while lump rose to 10.8263 million (+284,200), pellets to 1.8894 million (+155,500) and concentrates to 8.0258 million (+59,900). Fines are the mainstay of the blast furnace burden, so a fines drawdown alongside a lump and pellet build looks like mills squeezing expensive supplementary feed while protecting the base blend as they cut output. SMM also flags that arrivals are expected to increase somewhat this week, sustaining a high- inventory, comfortably supplied market. Shipment and arrival figures are unchanged at 35.5121 million mt globally and

29.4625 million mt into China as of Sep 25; freight was last published Sep 29 at USD 40.26/mt for C3 and USD 14.13/mt for C5.
Cost & margin The biggest change this issue. SMM puts the average imported ore loss at 2.45 yuan/mt, down from 6.12 — an improvement of 3.67 yuan driven by three forces pulling the same way: a modest rise in spot prices, a stronger yuan and lower ocean freight. Our Sep 30 issue flagged a tension in that table — it referenced Sep 29 spot and freight, whereas on Sep 30 port spot recovered and freight fell another 4–6%, both of which mechanically repair margins, so the first print after the holiday could recover ground. This is that recovery. One caveat: the currency is the least controllable of the three — it reflects neither ore strength nor mill profitability, and if the yuan gives back ground after the holiday, that part of the improvement disappears with it. SMM's view remains that imported ore will keep running a modest loss.

Given tight domestic supply and a high share of contract volumes, domestic ore typically has less catch-down to do than imported ore.
