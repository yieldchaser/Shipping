A look at propane stocks shows that inventory levels are growing as anticipated, leaving most to conclude that Gulf Coast tightness is the culprit, and recently surging terminal fees offer support for the rationale.  Single-digit transactions are not possible at this point, as a series of 10cpg July deals were concluded, and then followed by an 11cpg July deal which set a new tone.  Limited availability from the terminals (for a variety of reasons, but including recent large-scale storms in/around  the Houston area) has left spot cargoes in the hands of re-sellers, which – when coupled with a strong Arb – has allowed rates to rise solidly in to the double-digits.  As for stock levels themselves, the EIA this week reported an increase of 2.1mmbbls for an overall total of 66.30mmbbls, or about 94% of the levels recorded at the same time last year – but also 134% of two years ago.  Barring any unforeseen events, and if average build rates are achieved through the balance of the summer, total US propane stock levels are now calculated to be on either side of 100mmbbls at the first week of October.  NWE As May comes to a close and the first official day of summer approaches, we will see a second consecutive decrease of total monthly LPG imports into NWE (430,000mts), and while the propane price has climbed slightly over this period, the market is generally seen as comfortably supplied for now.  This could however change if logistical constraints at the Panama Canal continue to unwind, it will be more difficult for Europe to attract tons versus the far east, which has shown some signs of strength in the past couple weeks. It’s for this reason that the forward curve has propane sharply gaining on naphtha in the coming months, and thus, disincentivizing additional buying for feedstock purposes. And while butane has risen sharply in the last month, propane seems stable for the time being.
