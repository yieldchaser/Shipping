# Speed Up on Phase Out

This week we examine the VLCC fleet with regard to the impact of regulatory phase-out of single hull tankers. The VLCC fleet size over the last 10 years has been static as shown below. This stable fleet size is a result of annual scrapping activity more or less equaling newbuilding deliveries until 2004, when scrapping activity declined significantly.

SCRAPPED Even though the VLCC fleet has stagnated numerically, double hull vessels built in the 1990s and 2000s are faster, larger, more fuel-efficient, and operate more days per year than those being scrapped. 1 Newer VLCCs are approximately 20% more productive than the vessels they are replacing. Consequently the fleet can cover greater ton-mile demand with the same number of vessels. Accelerating the Phase-Out In 2003 the IMO accelerated the initial enforcement date for MARPOL- regulated phase-out of single hull tankers to April 5, 2005. Single hull tankers must now cease operating by 2010, unless the tankers pass a Condition Assessment Scheme (CAS) survey. If they pass the survey, then the vessel's required phase-out can be extended according to the CAS survey's findings, but not beyond 2015. In addition, tankers that are either double sided or double bottom are allowed to operate to their 25th year or until 2015, whichever occurs first. The age profile of the current single hull VLCC fleet shows that few face mandatory phase-out before 2010 as 95% of these vessels are less than 20 years of age.

Operating days per year for newbuildings are higher, naturally, than older vessels that require more off-hire time for repairs and maintenance.

Current Marpol regulations will send 119 VLCCs to the scrap yard that will be 20 or less years old in 2010 unless they pass a CAS survey. The new, stricter limits have a major impact on the future vessel supply. In 2004 and so far in 2005, few vessels are being scrapped, converted to FPSOs, permanent floating storage, or other uses. Should this trend continue, newbuilding deliveries will cause the VLCC fleet to swell until 2010 when 164 VLCCs will have to disappear before 2015 (assuming that 8 vessels are scrapped before 2010). This generates a rather unusual fleet development curve, a first in tanker history.

Anticipated Deliveries Removals Anticipated Removals The Erika sinking in late 1999 and the Prestige sinking in late 2003 introduced age preference into the chartering decisions of oil companies, reducing supply below physical availability and helping to cause the greatest charter rate rise in the history of tankers. The new MARPOL provision on single hull phase- out actually could have a greater impact on vessel supply than the Erika and Prestige combined. In the future, many additional VLCCs will need to be ordered to compensate for the tonnage that will be lost by 2015. Such orders, however, will have to come during a time when berth availability, due to demand for other types of vessels, is strained. In summary, current newbuilding orders will increase the size of the VLCC fleet at least through 2009. Many vessels will have to be ordered for delivery between 2010 and 2015, however, to compensate for the required phase- outs, which ultimately should prove worth the risk for those willing to shoulder it.
