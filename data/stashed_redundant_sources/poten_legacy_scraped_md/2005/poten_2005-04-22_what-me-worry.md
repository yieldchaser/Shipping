# What Me Worry?

The two locomotives of world economic activity are China and the United States. China has just reported economic growth for the most recent quarter on an annualized basis of 9.5%. This magnitude of economic growth should increase oil consumption by another 0.5 million bpd, good news for tanker owners. But what about the United States? The U.S. is China's largest marketplace absorbing its ever-growing avalanche of goods. This inextricably ties the two economies together. Moreover growth in the U.S. economy is also an extremely important factor for oil imports and hence continued prosperity in the tanker business. Current concerns over the U.S. economy are centered on rising inflation and short term interest rates.

### Is this transient, too?

### Times of Accelerated Inflation

Yes, inflation has accelerated in recent months (5.5% on an annualized basis), but this is not unusual in itself as there have been other periods of accelerated inflation only to be followed by periods of more subdued inflation. The question is whether this is just another statistical blip or does it presage our entering an extended period of higher inflation? Inflation is affected by the low value of the dollar that increases the cost of imports, other than China where the yuan is tied to the dollar. Greenspan's comments on unpegging the yuan sooner rather than later will increase the price of Chinese imports. The oil market is in contango, which is predictive of continued rising oil prices. Material costs in the United States, while high, are flattening out. All this may extend the period of higher inflation longer than has been experienced. The Narrowing Yield Curve The following chart shows that the spread between short and long term Treasury rates has been narrowing from nearly 4 percentage points in late 2003 to the current 1.6 percentage points.

### Short and Long Term U.S. Treasury Yields

10-Year 30-Day The differential between long and short term rates is narrowing only because the Federal Reserve increased overnight Fed rates to 2.75%. Long term rates are relatively unchanged. One reason for the increase in short term rates is to fight inflation by increasing borrowing costs. This is supposed to dampen demand in order to cool off the economy. This contention worked well historically when inflation reflected industrial capacity and labor availability peaking out, causing prices and labor costs to rise. This hardly fits the U.S. economy today. With manufacturing fleeing our shores and industrial activity below capacity, inflation reflects rising energy costs among other factors. Rising energy costs increase food prices, another component in the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The CPI is also influenced by higher priced imports from all over the world other than China from a weaker dollar. Cheap goods from China reduce the rise in the CPI. Another reason for the Federal Reserve increasing short term rates is to make dollar deposits by foreigners more attractive in order to strengthen the dollar in relation to other currencies. As the dollar strengthens, the cost of imports from nations other than China, where the yuan and dollar are pegged, falls, thus reducing the rise in the CPI.

Are There Indications of an Economic Slowdown? Not yet: the GDP for the United States strengthened in 2003 and does not show signs of abating (constant dollars show the real rate of change in the GDP). The growth between the fourth quarter of 2003 and the fourth quarter of 2004 was a respectable 3.9%.

### Stagnant

The chart on the next page is the ratio of sales to inventory published by the U.S. Census Bureau. The ratio of 1.3 months of inventory to cover a month of sales is very low. Industrial output is being sold, not stored in inventory, which is a good sign. Moreover increases in sales will have to spur inventory growth to keep the ratio from falling further.

### Inventory to Sales Ratio

Other indicators of economic activity are, generally speaking, on the optimistic side. One worrisome trend, however, is the U.S. balance of trade deficit.

The growing deficit reflects higher cost oil imports and greater consumer goods imports from China. High oil prices can affect economic activity, as had occurred during the late 1970s and early 1980s. A revaluation of the Chinese yuan would increase the price of Chinese imports and tend to reduce demand and improve the trade imbalance. But it would also affect China's exports, which could affect their economy, and increase inflation in the United States. Deciding on how this is played out probably explains why China is slow in floating the yuan.

What Me Worry? Our favorite predictor of U.S. recessions is the stock market that has correctly predicted 9 of the past 5 recessions. After falling 900 points in 7 weeks, the Dow Industrials rebounded by 200 points on Thursday on upbeat profit news from U.S. corporations. Underlying dollar strength from increased Fed interest rates and low inventories in relation to sales appear to be countering the combination of high energy prices, a potential yuan floatation, and the widening trade deficit. We don't wish to be a Pollyanna, but we don't see evidence of a slide in economic activity despite worries about inflation and interest rates.
