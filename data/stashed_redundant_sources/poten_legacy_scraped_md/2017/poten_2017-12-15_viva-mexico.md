# Viva Mexico

### Oil reform creates opportunities for tankers

In December 2013, Mexico made the necessary changes to its constitution to allow a widespread reform of the country's energy sector. Until that time, in the oil, gas and power sectors, Mexico was a largely closed and monopoly-driven energy market, lacking in investment and productivity growth. The reform package, introduced by President Enrique Pena Nieto has bolstered long-term growth through increased efficiency and productivity.

Mexico used to be one of the world's major oil producers and exporters, but its position has weakened over the years. According to the IEA, oil production declined by one-third from a peak of 3.8 million barrels per day (mb/d) in 2004 to 2.5 mb/d in 2016. In 2017, Mexico's production continued its steady decline. By October, crude oil output was down to 2.18 mb/d. The declines in Mexican production have been largely the result of underinvestment in the sector by the state-owned monopoly Petroleos Mexicanos (PEMEX). They lacked the resources to slow declines in mature fields or to develop new ones. PEMEX also did not have the technical capacity to bring new production online from deep-water and shale resources. Although 2018 is expected to be another challenging year, the IEA expects that Mexican oil production will turn around in future years as foreign investment in the sector, resulting from the energy reforms, will start to bear fruit. A series of bidding rounds that began in 2015 opened the sector to private investment and is bringing the necessary technology to develop new projects, including tight oil. The IEA projects that Mexican oil production will turn around and can reach 3.4 mb/d in 2040.

The impact of the challenges in Mexico's oil industry on the tanker market has been profound, although not in ways you may expect. Declines in oil production have previously led to cuts in crude oil exports and reduced crude tanker employment, in particular for Aframaxes in the Caribbean and the Atlantic Basin. In recent years exports have stabilized, despite falling production, which implies reductions in domestic crude oil demand. The impact on the tanker market comes from the changes in the destination. The traditional export market for Mexican crude was the United States; back in 2014 71% of Mexico's crude landed in the U.S. These short-haul voyages had a limited impact on crude oil tanker employment. In 2017 to date, the market share of the U.S. in Mexico's crude oil exports has been down to 50%. The growth market has been Asia, which now imports 27% of Mexico's crude exports, up from 9% in 2014. The main beneficiaries of this shift have been the VLCCs, which transport 70% of the Asian volumes, with the remaining 30% going on Suezmaxes. As domestic crude oil production in the U.S. goes from strength to strength, this trend towards diverting more Mexican exports to Asia is expected to continue, which will further support long-haul VLCC movements from the Caribbean to the Pacific.

Despite being a significant crude oil producer and exporter, Mexico is also a net importer of refined oil products because PEMEX failed to invest enough funds in the domestic refining industry. Mexico's refining challenges caused a rapid increase in product imports, predominantly sourced from the U.S. Mexico's petroleum product imports more than doubled over the last 10 years. The two key commodities are gasoline (65%) and diesel (25%). According to the U.S. Energy Information Agency, Mexico is by far the largest export destination for U.S. gasoline. In 2017, more than half (55%) of U.S. gasoline exports end up south of the border. The vast majority of this product is moved via seaborne trade. We are not aware of any product pipelines (other than LPG) between the U.S. and Mexico, and only small volumes of gasoline are transported by rail.

The Mexican oil industry continues to face challenges, but the outlook for the country's crude oil and product trades is supportive for the tanker market.
