# Fixing Forties

### Pipeline outage shuffles crude oil trade in the North Sea

On Monday 11 December, the Forties Pipeline System (FPS) in the North Sea was closed for repairs. The 235-mile pipeline transports about 450,000 barrels of oil per day (b/d) on average, which is about 40% of UK production. It is one of the oldest pipeline systems in the North Sea. It started operating in the Forties field in 1975 and links 85 North Sea oil and gas fields to facilities on the UK mainland, including the Hound Point export terminal and the 201,000 b/d Grangemouth refinery. BP originally operated the Forties oil and gas field, the pipeline and the refinery. It sold its interest in the oil field to Apache in 2003 and Ineos bought the Grangemouth refinery and chemical plant in 2005. Earlier this year, Ineos also bought the FPS from BP for a reported $250 million. The FPS is not expected to reopen until January 2018 and in this Weekly Opinion we will assess the potential impact on the tanker markets of this significant disruption.

To determine the impact of the pipeline closure on the tanker market, we must evaluate how much crude oil is exported from Hound Point (the export terminal for Forties crude in Scotland) and what type of vessels are used. Over the last 4 years (for which we have detailed data), exports have increasingly favored VLCCs. This shift came largely at the expense of Aframaxes. Suezmax tankers are only being used occasionally for exports from Hound Point. Total shipments from Hound Point have averaged about 300 Kb/d for the last three years.

In 2014, 66% of the cargoes loaded in Hound Point were on Aframax tankers as most of the voyages were headed to countries in Europe such as the Netherlands (Rotterdam), Germany (Wilhelmshaven and Brunsbüttel) and the UK (Fawley and Stanlow). Since then, Asian destinations have become much more important, and in 2017 to date they accounted for 67% of the volumes loaded in Hound Point. China and Korea each import more than 30% of these volumes. Exports to China increased after Chinese oil companies acquired interests in several North Sea fields bringing their share of production to about 200,000 b/d.

As a result, VLCCs are now the main size class used for shipments from Hound Point which is now the largest VLCC load port in the UKC area. Other large load locations are Rotterdam and Southwold, used for Ship-to-Ship transfers.

The shift from short haul moves to long haul exports to Asia has been a great development for Hound Point-related ton mile demand, which nearly tripled from 2014 to 2017.

The unexpected closure of the pipeline led to a reduction of about 450 Kb/d of light sweet crude from the global market. This initially caused the price of Brent crude oil to increase by about $1.54 from Friday Dec. 8 to Monday Dec. 11 and WTI, another light sweet crude, increased by $0.63 during this period. The Brent-Dubai spread also increased from $3.50 to $4.25, reflecting the perceived shortage of light, sweet crude versus the heavier Dubai variety, making Middle Eastern crudes more attractive.

It is too early to have definitive statistics but anecdotal evidence suggests that Chinese buyers have increased their purchases from West Africa while Korean buyers have focused more on the Middle Eastern suppliers. U.S. crude oil also became more attractive as the Brent - WTI spread widened further.

Aframax rates in the North Sea increased by 7.5 points on the day that the closure was announced. The impact on other freight rates was less immediate and it is harder to link them to the pipeline repairs. The upcoming Christmas weekend affects rates as well.

Yesterday, Ineos (the operator of the pipeline) announced that they anticipate that the pipeline will be back online in early January. Unless there are unforeseen delays, the impact over the coming weeks will likely be relatively subdued as the market slows down for the holiday season.
