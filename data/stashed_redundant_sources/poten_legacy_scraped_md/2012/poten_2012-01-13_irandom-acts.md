# Iran‐dom Acts

With the tanker market largely tuned into the potential effects of a closure of the Straits of Hormuz, there is a chance that broadly sweeping policies to be enacted by the United States have been somewhat overshadowed. While sanctions with Iran are nothing new, the past few weeks have seen President Obama place increased emphasis on efforts to dissuade foreign central and private banks from dealing with the pariah state.

On December 31, Mr. Obama signed a new round of sanctions into law that have implications for participants in both petroleum and non‐petroleum transactions. To date, the law has been met with mixed feedback from the international community. Restrictions on petroleum‐related activities will likely raise the most concerns for key trade partners as well as the tanker market as oil is Iran's main export.

Shown in the chart below, Iran single‐handedly accounts for over 5% of total worldwide daily crude oil production and is an important supplier not only to countries in the European Union, but also to the global economic powerhouses China, South Korea and India.

% of Worldwide Production Also troubling, the methodology behind enforcing sanctions reads as if it is an inexact science. The decision to impose sanctions will be determined on the basis that "enough" non‐Iranian crude oil is present in the market to warrant importers to "significantly" reduce Iranian purchases. Additionally, Mr. Obama will be able to grant trading partners waivers if deemed appropriate.

While the tone in Washington is clear ‐ punish those who financially support Iran - the law's language is not. On the surface it appears the law is far reaching. However, the bandwidth of its application may ultimately be significantly smaller, especially if international kick‐back persists.

As those well‐versed in the petroleum market are aware, it will be exceedingly difficult to assign causality in price movements to availability of Iranian supplies. Furthermore, the potentially subjective terms under which sanctions will be enforced calls into question its efficacy.

### Pointing Fingers

The majority of Iranian crude oil enters the market on a delivered basis via the National Iranian Tanker Company's (NITC) fleet of vessels: only about 20% of the 2.4 million barrels per day of net exports materialized through reported spot market transactions last year.

The two charts below show the range of discharge locations for reported spot crude oil fixtures for 2002 and 2011. Overall reported spot fixture volumes ex‐Iran have declined about 20% during the ten‐year period from 37 million metric tons to 28 million metric tons.

Based on an analysis of satellite data of NITC's fleet, a significant portion of Iranian exports is shipped to the Su‐Med pipeline in the Red Sea for end user markets in Europe. India has risen as a significant buyer as well. The last 10 years show a 120% increase in volumes moved spot to 5.5 million metric tons a year, or about 110,000 barrels per day. Economic sanctions against Iran by the Europe and India could potentially impact the availability of refined product supply to the United States.

### Shoot First, Get Practical Later

While Washington's stance on Tehran has been clear for quite some time, buy‐in from the international community is a lofty goal. So far the United States has even struggled to galvanize real support from allies simply due to their collective reluctance in severing a critical petroleum lifeline.

The impracticality of garnering international support aside, the tenets on which second tier sanctions will be implemented remain flawed. Expect petitions for waivers to be forthcoming and swiftly granted. Legislators would be wise to follow the tanker market to see just how global petroleum supply and distribution really is.
