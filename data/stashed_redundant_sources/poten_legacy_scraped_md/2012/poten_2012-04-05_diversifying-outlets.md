# Diversifying Outlets

Arguments occasionally spawn diversification. Oil pipeline tariff squabbles with Belarus in 2008 prompted Russia to develop alternative routes that bypassed their neighbor. One result was the country's decision to build the Baltic Pipeline System 2 (BPS2) as shown in the following map. Inaugural flows of Russian oil should begin seeping into European markets over the next few weeks from the pipeline's terminus in the Baltic port of Ust-Luga.

The anticipated initial flows of eventually 200 kBD from this pipeline do not necessarily represent new oil supplies into the Baltic, however. Half this daily rate is slated to be diverted from Russia's deliveries to Primorsk, which is on the Gulf of Finland shore opposite Ust-Luga. Making up the remainder is oil that would have been delivered to Poland's Gdansk port through Belarus via the Druzhba pipeline. The impact on tanker utilization rates of this rearrangement of waterborne Baltic oil flows is being considered by owners.

In 2011, dirty cargo loading on Aframaxes in the Baltic comprised almost three-quarters of the reported spot fixtures over all vessel classes. Nearly 80 percent of these cargos were headed to the nearby UK Continent discharge zone, as shown in the chart below.

The diversion of 100 kBD of oil exports from Primorsk to Ust-Luga should not impact Aframax ton- mile demand appreciably. Shifting the loading of this quantity of oil from Gdansk to a port more distant from European markets, however, might translate in higher ton-mile demand for this vessel sector. Directionally, any lift in Aframax ton-mile demand around the Baltic would bolster this vessel sector's regional utilization rates. Additionally, if plans to lift the BPS2's flows to 600 kBD sometime in 2013 are fulfilled, the amount of Russian oil leaving the Baltic will increase. The increase could come from higher Russian oil production rates, or material diverted from other pipelines or their Black Sea exports.
