# Scrapping To The Rescue

### Increased demolition is needed to rebalance the market

The tanker market has started 2018 in the doldrums. One of the main reasons for the depressed rate environment is the oversupply of existing tonnage. On top of that, there are still a significant number of newbuilding deliveries scheduled this year for all the major vessel segments and because OPEC has vowed to keep its production cuts in place (at least through June), ton-mile demand growth may be limited. That is why many tanker market observers are looking at demolition as a key driver to rebalance the market and restore health to the freight market. The argument is that as tanker earnings regularly dip below operating expenses and more and more charterers discriminate against older (>15 years) vessels, demolition starts to look more attractive, especially with scrap prices on the rise. The looming regulation changes with respect to Ballast Water Treatment Systems and the Sulphur content of bunker fuels and their associated investments also play a role. What do we think that the practical impact will be of scrapping in the short to medium term?

Back in 1789, Benjamin Franklin said that in this world nothing is certain, except death and taxes. In shipping, many companies shield themselves from most taxes, but the other certainty remains: every ship will eventually be scrapped, typically somewhere between 20 and 25 years old. The scrap age of tankers is heavily influenced by the state of the freight market. During the very challenging tanker market of 1980-1986, VLCCs were scrapped at an average age of 11 years, but in the 2003- 2008 super cycle, the average scrapping age for that vessel class reached a high of 29 years (in 2007). Since the boom-bust cycle is most pronounced in the large tanker market, the average scrapping age of VLCCs tends to be more volatile than that for smaller tankers. The annual average scrapping age of smaller product carriers, for example, has never fallen below 20 years and has typically been between 25 and 30 years. So, while there may be some merit in looking at the VLCC fleet of 16 years old and older (116 vessels, 16% of the total fleet), it is rather meaningless to look at product carriers in this age bracket. For product carriers, 20+ or 25+ years old may be a better target to determine the pool of scrapping candidates.

Other factors that can impact tanker demolition are scrap prices and changes in regulations. Scrap prices can be a factor on the margin for owners that are debating whether to sell or scrap, especially when secondhand prices are under pressure and scrap prices are going up. Regulations can be a major factor. The switch from single hull to double hull tonnage ultimately forced all older tankers into retirement. The impending ballast water treatment regulations could have an impact on the life- expectancy of older vessels in the current fleet.

Let's look at VLCCs as an example. There are 116 units built from 1993-2001. Scrapping all these vessels over the next few years would significantly improve the VLCC market, but how likely is that? Despite the low rates, there is still a secondhand market for older VLCCs. A 15-year-old VLCC has a secondhand value of some $23 million and a scrap value of around $18 - $20 million. As demonstrated by recent transactions, owners of well-maintained units of 15-20 years old have been able to sell their vessels for further trading, although the pool of potential buyers is small. If the low freight market persists, secondhand values will likely fall further and scrapping will be the most likely option for the 20 VLCCs that will face a drydock/special survey in 2018/2019 (according to our records). Of the 22 vessels that are already 20+ years old, about a third are controlled by governments in the Middle East. These vessels are mostly employed in captive trades and will probably be scrapped when their newbuilding replacements are delivered. The rest of the 20+ year old fleet is either used for floating storage in Asia or for shuttling crude between the Arabian Gulf and India. Given the low utilization rate of most of these vessels, scrapping them will have a limited impact on the market balances. In short, VLCC scrapping will help, but with 85 deliveries already scheduled for 2018/2019, we also need ton-mile demand growth.
