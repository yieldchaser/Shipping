# Favorable Winds Ahead

### The latest IEA forecast is bullish on tanker trade

The overall tone of "Oil 2018", the latest IEA annual forecast of global oil demand, supply, refining and trade is fairly positive and many of the specific elements that are discussed in the report are rather bullish for the tanker market. A combination of robust global demand growth, refining capacity expansion and changes in tradeflows could help the recovery in the tanker market. Even the IMO mandated changes in marine fuel specifications that will come into effect in 2020 could have a silver lining for the tanker market.

The IEA expects solid increases in oil demand based on a strong world economy. Outside of Europe, whose oil demand is expected to start declining again after 2018, all major regions in the world will show demand growth according to the IEA. Between 2017 and 2023, global oil demand will expand by 6.9 million barrels per day (mb/d). The Asia Pacific region will be by far the fastest growing region, followed by the Middle East. Africa and the Americas will expand at a slower pace. Within Asia, China and India will drive this growth and these countries, who represent 50% of global demand growth in the period through 2023, are both heavily dependent on imports. Demand growth in the Pacific Basin will stimulate long-haul tanker demand, especially since most of the oil production growth is happening in the Atlantic Basin. We will discuss this in more detail below.

The oil production growth in the United States is a bullish factor. The IEA forecasts that more than half of the growth in the world's production capacity between 2018 and 2023 (3.7 mb/d out of 6.4 mb/d) will come from the U.S. Canada, Brazil and Norway are also expanding their production. A lot of different factors will drive how this will play out in terms of tradeflows, but the IEA describes a plausible scenario that would lead to expanding U.S. crude oil exports to Europe and Asia. The expectation is that almost all the incremental oil that will be produced in Canada (about 1 mb/d over five years) will be exported (via pipeline and/or rail) to the U.S. because Canadian producers are facing an uphill battle getting export pipelines to either the east or west coast of their country approved. The increased imports of the heavy Canadian crudes will satisfy most of the incremental demand from refiners in the U.S. Gulf area. On the margin, it will push out some of the heavy grades that are currently imported from Latin America and the Middle East. However, the main consequence of these developments in North America is that most of the incremental Light Tight Oil (LTO) that is produced in the U.S. will hit the export markets. Most of this will be exported to Asia, a long-haul trade that is increasingly using VLCCs. The IEA expects that China will become the largest importer of U.S. crude oil, since U.S. LTO is well suited for refineries that are integrated with petrochemical operations and the booming chemicals sector in Asia requires more feedstocks.

The IMO bunker fuel changes are also expected to drive growth in demand for US LTO. The expectation is that, in the absence of readily available low- sulphur fuel oil, shipowners will switch to various diesel blends. This will create both a deficit of middle distillates and a surplus of high-sulphur fuel oil. Refiners will try to address this by using more light and low-sulphur crudes such as U.S. LTO. This will be particularly attractive for European refiners, which are already short middle distillates.

Then there is the refined product side of the market. The IEA expects the Middle East to lead in incremental refined product exports, followed by the U.S. and China. The IMO change will also lead to more diesel imports into the bunkering hub of Singapore.

Most of the changes discussed above are adding to ton-mile demand. This will help balance the tanker market over time and provide some much- needed support for owners.
