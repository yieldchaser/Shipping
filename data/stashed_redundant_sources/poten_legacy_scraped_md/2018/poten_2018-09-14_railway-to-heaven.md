# Railway To Heaven

### Is crude by rail making a comeback in North America?

Large oceangoing tankers are generally considered the most cost-efficient way to move crude oil over long distances across the world. Pipelines are a quick, reliable and cost effective alternative although by their nature, they are less flexible. Crude by rail (CBR) is a third option for long haul transportation of oil, more expensive than pipelines but providing more flexibility for overland transportation. Due to the significant existing railroad infrastructure in North America, CBR offers significant geographical flexibility as well as responsiveness to changes in demand. As a result, crude by rail was a significant factor in the distribution of crude oil in the U.S. during the period 2013-2015, when shale oil production was ramping up and U.S. crude oil exports were still restricted to Canada. After the U.S. crude oil export ban was lifted, the Brent-WTI spread narrowed which made it cheaper for coastal refiners to import foreign crudes by tanker. Total CBR movements in the U.S. (domestic and from Canada), dropped from a peak of 1.14 million b/d in October 2014 to 273,000 b/d in September 2017. As the Brent-WTI spread has expanded again, CBR movements have started to increase, primarily from the Bakken region to the U.S. East Coast.

Crude by rail is almost as old as the oil industry itself. In August 1859, Colonel Edwin Drake struck oil in Titusville, Pennsylvania. The first railroad to reach Pennsylvania's oil region arrived a little more than three years later in November 1862. The Oil Creek Railroad was approximately 27 miles long and moved about 30,000 barrels per month from Titusville to Corry, Pennsylvania. This is a far cry from the unit trains that operate in North America today. Unit trains are long trains (usually at least 50 and sometimes more than 100 cars) consisting of a single commodity (in this case crude oil). A unit train might carry 85,000 barrels of oil and can be loaded or unloaded in 24 hours. Over the years, the railroads have invested significant sums to enhance the safety of moving crude oil. They have improved the infrastructure, equipment and accident mitigation and response functions.

In the United States, the largest volumes of CBR originate from the Bakken formation in North Dakota, since this region lacks pipeline capacity. In June 2018, rail movements from Petroleum Administration for Defense District (PADD) 2, where Bakken is located, accounted for 85% of total intra-U.S. movements of crude by rail. The main destinations for this crude are PADD 5 (the U.S. West Coast) and PADD 1 (the U.S. East Coast). After a long period of decline, rail and Jones Act volumes have started to pick up again. As we indicated previously, the main reason for this shift is the expanding Brent-WTI spread. Domestic infrastructure bottlenecks are the key driver behind the current price differential between domestic and foreign crudes. However, the challenges are temporary. Oil producers and midstream companies are investing heavily in pipelines and export terminals.

The situation in Canada is slightly different. Rail movements in Canada (both domestic and exports to the U.S.) are trending higher as well. However, in Canada this could be part of a longer-term trend. Canada is facing a significant shortage of pipeline capacity, but there is only limited new infrastructure coming online in the coming years. The IEA forecasts that Canadian exports by rail will grow to 250,000 b/d this year and then to 390,000 b/d in 2019. In 2020, the expansion of Enbridge's Mainline pipeline system is expected to reduce rail exports to around 170,000 b/d.
