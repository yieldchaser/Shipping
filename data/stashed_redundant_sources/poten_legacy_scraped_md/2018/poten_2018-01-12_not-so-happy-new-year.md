# Not So Happy New Year

### What is going on in the tanker market?

Shipowners typically look forward to the winter. As the weather gets colder, the tanker market starts to heat up. There are a variety of factors that contribute to this seasonal improvement in the markets. Colder weather in the northern hemisphere boosts energy demand, while winter storms in the north Atlantic, ice in the Baltic and fog in the Black sea reduce fleet productivity in the winter months. As can be seen in the chart of VLCC rates (which is normally fairly representative of all tanker markets), in a "normal" year, the first and in particular the fourth quarter show the highest earnings. As the fourth quarter of 2017 started, it appeared to follow the normal seasonal pattern, but rather than strengthening through November and December, rates plateaued and turned down towards the end of the year and remained depressed into the new year. What caused this counter seasonal slump in tanker rates, and (how long) will it last?

The first area to examine in more detail is tanker demand. According to the IEA, global oil demand in Q4 2017 was a robust 98.3 mb/d, the same level as in Q3 and 1.3 mb/d higher than in Q4 2016. More important for tanker demand, however, is OPEC production, and this has moved in the opposite direction. In Q4 2016 Middle East OPEC produced 25.6 mb/d. One year later, this number was down to an estimated 24.5 mb/d, a reduction of 1.1 mb/d. So, while global oil demand was up 1.3%, Middle East OPEC production was down 4.3%.

If Middle East OPEC has reduced production (and exports), but oil demand remains strong, where do refiners source the additional crude from? A significant portion comes from the United States due to two concurrent developments: 1) growing production and exports from domestic shale producers, and 2) crude oil inventory drawdowns. According to U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) data, U.S. crude oil production increased by 850,000 b/d from 8.79 mb/d in October 2016 to 9.64 mb/d in October 2017 (latest available data). On a net basis, most of this extra production is exported: U.S. crude oil exports more than doubled from an average of 485,000 b/d in 2016 to 978,000 b/d in 2017. In Q4 of 2017 U.S. crude exports reached an average of 1.47 mb/d! U.S. crude oil stocks have also been drawn down. U.S. inventories (commercial and government) at the end of December 2017 stood at 1,088 million barrels, 85.8 million barrels below the level of year end 2016. Inventory drawdowns accelerated in the last quarter of 2017, averaging 16.8 million barrels per month (550,000 b/d).

When we focus on VLCCs for a moment, it becomes clear how the above changes impact tanker demand. The average monthly number of VLCC movements decreased by 18 cargoes between Q4 2016 and Q4 2017. The volume of VLCC cargoes originating from the Middle East was down by 24. Most of this reduction was directed towards the U.S. Gulf (-10), but Asia (-5) and NW Europe (-4) also received less crude from the Arabian Gulf. On the other hand, exports from the U.S. Gulf increased dramatically (+7.3). Overall, there was a noticeable reduction in monthly VLCC traffic, with a depressing effect on rates.

What about the supply side of the (tanker) equation? Fleet growth was also a factor. Since January 2017, the VLCC fleet has grown by 37 vessels (+5.3%).

In conclusion, the VLCC market remained under heavy downward pressure despite healthy global oil demand growth, because an expanding fleet faced a combination of OPEC production cuts and inventory drawdowns. A continuation of these headwinds could make 2018 another challenging year. OPEC decided to maintain its production cuts at least until June and another 50 or so VLCCs are expected to hit the market this year.

However, we caution against too much pessimism. The oil and tanker markets are prone to unexpected events. Market psychology is an important driver and owners should be careful not to make a gloomy market outlook a self- fulfilling prophesy.
