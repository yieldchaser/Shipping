# If You Build it, They Will Come

In 2006, the Panama Canal Authority (ACP) announced a $5.25 billion expansion proposal. It was a period of global economic growth and expanding shipping markets. Now, just three years later, the project finds itself in a very different environment. The expansion plans to create a third canal capable of handling post‐Panamax container vessels, up to 366 meters in length with a 49 meter beam, and a draft that would allow Capesize bulkers and Suezmax passages. The shipping shortcut joining the two largest oceans is destined for expansion by 2014; the question now is whether or not customers will be looking to take advantage.

The Panama Canal opened and closed its locks to over 14,000 ships in 2008, carrying over 200 million tons of cargo and yielding a toll revenue of over $1.3 billion. According to the ACP, tanker traffic accounted for 1,916 of the transits or roughly 13% of the total.

The chart below provides an overview of the toll revenues over the past three fiscal years (October to September). The upward growth trend helps justify the expansion. Even in crumbling economic conditions, the Canal saw an increase in transits of 0.1% in the first quarter of fiscal year 2009 (fourth quarter of calendar year 2008).

Reefer Other Although on‐time and on‐budget so far, some details and hurdles surrounding the expansion still remain to be tackled. For example, the ACP is undergoing the selection process for contractors to design and build the locks - the most expensive and arguably the most important part of the project.

According to the 2006 proposal, the peak construction period (2009‐2011) is pegged to receive $2.3 billion from external sources. In other words, almost half of the $5.25 billion budget will be soaked up over the next two years; most will likely go to cover the expenses associated with lock construction. Although the exact cost is unclear, it is certain that the ACP will be relying heavily on expanded toll revenues to recover these project expenses.

In 2006, the ACP had proposed a 3.5% annual toll increase to speed up debt repayment and triple canal contributions to the Panamanian Treasury by 2015. According to the ACP, this plan would repay investment costs in less than ten years and loans in only eight. However, such increases could also be to the Canal's detriment as new users in 2014 may be deterred if passage fees become too high or alternatively if traffic delays are too burdensome after expansion.

### The Canal of Dreams

As shipowners and charterers alike are in cost cutting mode, the ACP should look to do the same. In a low freight rate and slackened demand environment it is unlikely that shipowners and charterers will feel pressured to pay an expensive toll just to take the most direct route.

In a move to accommodate the current weakening market, the ACP Board of Directors has made a statement about temporarily reducing tolls for transit, beginning June 1, 2009 and ending September 30, 2009. With construction costs looming ahead, it is unclear if discounts to customers beyond September 2009 can be sustained.

The next few years will be crucial for the Canal with respect to tolls and number of transits. While the expansion brings with it the ability to double current capacity, either increased tolls or vessel traffic may ultimately be a deterrent. The theoretical reduction in ton‐mile demand that will result from the expansion may have less of an impact in practice.
