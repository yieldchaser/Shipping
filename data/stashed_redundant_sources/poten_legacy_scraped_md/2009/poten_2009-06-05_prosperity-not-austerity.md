# Prosperity - Not Austerity

After making a quiet entrance into President Obama's cabinet, Steven Chu, the new US Energy Secretary has found himself in the limelight. Perhaps less by choice and more because of a call by media and lawmakers, Chu has gone from a Nobel Prize winning physicist, to a man with the power to shape and mold the future of US energy policy and arguably the world. While it is yet to be determined if Chu's basket of ideas parallel President Obama's, his comments of late have provided insight into his disconnection from oil.

While in attendance at the Reuters Global Energy Summit in Washington, Secretary Chu made it clear his sentiment on the impact of oil on the US and global economy. In short, he believes that the slight increase in oil prices over the past month is a sign of economic recovery; however, he warns that any additional price rise would hamper the recovery - a thin line to walk.

Awash with new policy ideas, Secretary Chu finds himself in between an alternative energy horizon and reliable, efficient oil. In addition to linking the price of oil to economic recovery, Chu safely believes that the price is the result of market psychology and the prospect for future supply and demand. ### Flower Power

After oil peaked in July 2008 + $147/bbl, the sleeping green giant was awakened by new advocates for fuel efficient cars, hydrogen power, fuel cells and any other means of transport which could stave off prices at the pump. As the dust settled, policy makers found themselves in a new battleground; energy was called to the plate. Since then, the Obama administration has pitched a full count of new energy investments, such as the six below included in the American Recovery Act.

### Green Job Training

Only in the past month have rough plans been hewn out for the public - which includes $60 billion in the near term and $150 billion over the next ten years to be invested into green technology. However, a quick scan of the energy portion of the American Recovery Act leaves members of the petroleum industry empty handed as funds have been diverted for the strict use of creating green jobs and retooling the US to become a renewable powerhouse and "exporter of renewable energy."

"So we have a choice to make. We can remain one of the World's leading importers of foreign oil, or we can make the investments that would allow us to become the world's leading exporter of renewable energy..." - US President Barack Obama, March 19, 2009 As the Washington crowd grinds out new energy policy and works to allocate taxpayer dollars, Secretary Chu has to take a stance.

### The Green Curtain

While Washington has yet to determine the ultimate direction of their actions, the scheduled timeline for serious policy decisions seems short. On one hand, President Obama has declared energy independence from foreign oil and unveiled a massive green spending package. On the other hand, Midwest policy makers are hesitant to fight for cap and trade because of potential impacts on industry, OPEC is called upon to increase production to combat rising oil prices and President Obama meets with the Saudi King to talk oil.

After looking at the tabled expenditures, the American taxpayer is rightfully confused. Contradictory rhetoric about high crude oil prices being a good economic sign and the need to lower oil prices to help the economy only highlight the mixed sentiment in Washington. The focus on green energy serves to expose the lobbyist influences driving policy and the short‐sighted laws that are likely to be passed.

With competing agendas in Washington about where priorities should lie with respect to stirring the economy, it looks as though a functional long‐term energy plan is a distant dream. The inability of lawmakers to recognize that markets offer the best environments for technology to thrive is somewhat disheartening. The tough pill for Washington to swallow is that the best relief to the American wallet is to stick to what is cheap and efficient: oil. But with an unlimited checkbook mentality the Administration is unlikely to demonstrate self‐restraint.
