# Barrels Bound East

The rapid growth of China's oil demand has forced a transition from self‐sufficiency enjoyed in the early 1990s toward dependence on imports for about half of its crude oil requirement. Managing their growing import dependence has become a central concern of policy planners in Beijing and an important factor in the development of present and future tanker trade routes.

As shown in the chart below, Chinese crude oil production has remained flat in recent years as its demand has risen sharply. Offshore developments are in the near future, but have yet to come to market. The IEA expects China to experience 5.1 percent (410 kbd) oil demand growth in 2009 and a further 4.1 percent growth (340 kbd) in 2010.

### Domestic Production

Consumption In the first half of 2009, as total crude oil consumption fell 3.2 mbd compared with 2008 levels, resilience in the Chinese economy helped stave off a complete collapse in the global economy. A $586 billion stimulus package China launched last year to counteract the impact of the economic crisis was focused on boosting domestic consumption of goods and services. The National Development and Reform Commission of China recently released statistics reporting that the economy grew at an 8.9 percent annualized rate in the third quarter of 2009.

China's economic development has been a clear factor determining future oil demand for some time, but recent upstream acquisitions have demonstrated the country's interest in taking the reins on security of supply. China has capitalized on asset prices partially deflated by the economic crisis to acquire stakes in a wide variety of oil and gas projects; estimates of the total spent on energy related mergers and acquisition this year vary around $15 billion. China has also entered into about $50 billion of loan‐for‐oil agreements with many producers since 2008. With a dearth of liquidity in the market, it seems more international eyes are turning east.

### Have Trade, Will Travel

Though some future Chinese imports will be received through pipelines, tanker owners have reason to welcome China's recent energy investments as they will ultimately result in incremental increases in ton‐mile demand. Chinese sponsored exports have already helped transform the West Africa to Far East voyage from a sleepy backhaul into a primary money‐ making voyage and Venezuela's increased exports to China have supported Caribbean to Far East rates. As shown in the chart below, recent years have seen an increase in reported liftings headed to China. While reported fixtures only capture a portion of market activity, they do reflect the general trend. China's import dependence is likely to impact tanker markets in both predictable and perhaps unpredictable ways.

### AG ‐ China

AG ‐ US Gulf WAF ‐ China WAF ‐ USAC/US Gulf About half of Chinese crude oil imports come from the Middle East and cargoes bound for China are expected to continue as a major factor in supporting rates out of the Arabian Gulf. Another producer expected to see increased volumes headed for China is Brazil. In May, the Chinese Development Bank loaned Petrobras $10 billion in exchange for access to 200 kbd of crude oil from Brazil's sub‐salt resources due to come online in 2013.

### Wild Card

Perhaps the largest wildcard with respect to the shipping markets will be China's interest in developing a domestic fleet for the transportation of imported barrels. If China takes the reins on shipping, the market for crude oil tankers could become fragmented.

A desire to diversify its vast holdings of American dollars has surely played a role in driving the upstream shopping spree, but the need to secure access to future supply has also been a central motivator. As China could potentially have a large number of newbuilding tankers on hand, growth of a large domestic fleet of tankers might be a reality sooner rather than later.
