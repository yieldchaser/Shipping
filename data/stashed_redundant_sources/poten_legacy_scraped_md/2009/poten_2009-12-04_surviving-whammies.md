# Surviving Whammies

US refiners are being forced to deal with dismal margins brought about by a lack of demand for their prime products - gasoline and diesel. Consumption of gasoline is roughly 7 percent less than the go‐go days of mid‐2007 - and once the growth in ethanol use is considered, the need for refiners' hydrocarbon component has contracted even more. Diesel use has evaporated by some one million barrels per day (mbd) or over 20 percent from its highs of late 2007.

Margins have suffered from the collision between fairly strong crude oil prices and refiners' lack of leverage to raise product prices in the face of weak demand. But after living in a cyclic industry for many years, most refiners are adept at dealing with business pull‐backs. Past recessions have generally seen some refining capacity close while other capacity is either expanded or added. In keeping with the law‐of‐the‐jungle, economically strong refineries become stronger while weak ones get closed and, of course, life goes on. But this recession somehow seems different. With green house gas (GHG) cap and trade regulations - as well as the associated costs - still being forged on the anvil of political wrangling, strong US refiners may pause before rushing to offset any closed capacity.

### The Coup de grâce?

US rules on limiting domestic GHG emissions are still wending their way through the legislative process. But judging by the volume of complaints emanating from segments of the refining industry, one might conclude that the rules proposed by the country's august legislators are less than fair and equitable to all parties affected. These rules presently propose that parties selling transportation and other fuels derived from crude oil must buy allowances for the GHGs resulting from the fuel's burning. Both importers and domestic refiners must abide by these rules. However, an additional onus - in the form of a levy on the GHGs generated in manufacturing the fuel - is proposed only for domestic refineries and not for imports. The prevailing industry sentiment is that any additional levy over and above what product importers face might push some marginally profitable US refining capacity into retirement as it would give imports a significant advantage.

### GHG Legislation Forming, Tanker Owners Take Warning

The seemingly endless line of storm clouds gathering around US refiners does not appear to bode well for people trying to make a living carrying crude oil to the shores of this nation. While domestic refining capacity in a few niche markets may weather the pending GHG allocation storm, some of those located in markets with easy access to imports may become pressured financially and eventually succumb. Conventional reasoning might see shrinking US refining capacity yielding expectations of greater product imports. But if the mandated leap in biofuels production does indeed come to pass, any growth in imports may be muted. Ship owners will likely call on their agility to balance their fleets against shifting US demand for both dirty and clean tonnage.

Surviving a paralyzing or lethal blow - commonly referred to as a whammy - is usually a mixture of planning, skill and just plain luck. Make no mistake though: the coming US GHG regulations represent a whammy that will propel the concept of conducting business‐as‐usual into antiquity. Only through intense planning, skill and a perhaps a dose of luck will agile and adaptive businesses survive in the future's GHG emission constricted paradigm. Here's to hoping Washington realizes the long‐term impacts of regulations on American business before it's too late.
