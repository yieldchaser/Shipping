# Walking The Plank

As we have been painfully aware for several months, piracy off the coast of Somalia has been on the rise since 2007 and it poses an increasing effect to the shipping industry. With the Somalian government too shaky to take effective measures, the onus has fallen upon other governments and the shipping industry itself. But questions remain: how is piracy to be combated? Who will shoulder the costs? And how will the market reorient itself around this escalation of crime?

Neither piracy nor its presence in the Gulf of Aden is new, but the main cause for alarm about the situation in Somalia is the exponential growth: there were two attempted attacks in 2004, but 42 in 2007. And while the Somalian government is located in the capital, Mogadishu, the vast majority of the pirates are based in the breakaway region of Puntland. As a result, the Somalian government lacks the resources and the influence to mount a campaign against the pirates, or solve the socioeconomic problems that make piracy so appealing.

### The Ton-Mile Effect

One result of the heavy pirate presence in the Gulf of Aden is a growing preference among shipowners to re‐route through the Cape of Good Hope. While this move effectively reduces the chances of pirate attack, it is both extremely costly and time‐ consuming. While a standard AG‐USG voyage costs $25,000 per day, re‐routing ups the rate to $37,100 per day. In addition, the growth of insurance premiums has skyrocketed in assonance with the increased levels of piracy: premiums are reported to risen tenfold in 2008 alone!

### Who Pays the Price?

The route through the Cape of Good Hope is being treated as a stop‐gap; but it might become more permanent as pirates get more violent with their captives. As a result, the pirates have capitalized on the situation to consistently raise their ransom demands, preying on most shipowners' fears for the lives of their crew, as well as their ignorance of the average ransom price. The situation has also become more fraught, if only in the eyes of the public, as pirates and the region become associated with terrorism. Yesterday, Egyptian authorities arrested 26 Al‐Qaeda linked suspects in connection with a plot to detonate explosives in the Suez Canal. The payment of ransom becomes strained as the moral ambiguity grows. If paying a ransom becomes linked at all to funding terrorism, the shipowner is at an extreme disadvantage in negotiating for human life.

### What Next?

Clearly, the situation calls for government intervention, and fast: the Somalian government has issued a plea for funds to create its own coast guard, and the United States already spear‐heads the Combined Task Force 151 (CTF‐151). The CTF‐151 includes vessels from the United Kingdom, Australia, France, Germany, Italy, Pakistan and Japan as well as aircrafts that aid in patrolling the Gulf of Aden. But this large naval presence has only moderately inhibited the pirates. Now what?

The first option is to organize shipping into a safe lane; the existence of a heavily secured lane would make it easier for international forces in the area to monitor shipping and respond to distress calls. However, if the naval forces fail to provide complete security, the lane has the effect of herding all the potential targets together in an accessible location. The second is to provide a coastguard for Somalia; but in the absence of a long‐term and reliable government to either maintain a coastguard or prosecute pirates, the existence of a weak coastguard would only result in free training for future pirates and further loss of money. The final option is to stop paying ransoms, a solution suggested by those who argue that a lack of incentive is key to safer seas for all concerned. However, the Somalian pirates might adopt the practices of their Indonesian colleagues and create phantom ships (a stolen ship that is re‐registered and then has its cargo 'stolen') or switch to stealing cargo. Each option would result in a tactical shift for the pirates-something that the shipping industry is reluctant to induce, in the event that it creates a more sophisticated breed of Somalian pirate.

As unwelcome as the news is, the solution lies in fixing Somalia's political situation; only with a stable government in place can the pirates be ousted from their haven. Until then, the presence of warships cannot suffice for the expenses incurred from ship re‐ routing. An industry‐wide refusal to pay ransom would be difficult to implement- especially when human life is involved. The alpha and omega of this situation is Somalia's internal stability: and only when this is addressed can this glut of piracy end.
