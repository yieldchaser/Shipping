# China's Bright Horizon

China's growing reliance on seaborne crude oil imports will set the tone of the tanker market for the coming decade. As shown in the chart below, the size of China's reported spot program has increased five‐fold over the past ten years; rising from an equivalent of 11 VLCCs in 2000 to one of more than 55 VLCCs last year. Chinese charterers have accounted for about 30% of reported VLCC spot fixture activity year‐to‐date, as compared with less than 5% a decade ago.

China National Petroleum Corporation (CNPC) expects a nearly 10% rise in the country's imported oil requirements in 2010. Increased imports, the proportion of which reportedly crossed the 50% threshold last year, are thought to have raised energy security concerns of policymakers in Beijing. These concerns have motivated recent attempts to diversify suppliers for crude oil, not unlike other large‐scale importers, and ensure that 40% of the country's oil is imported on Chinese‐owned ships by 2015.

### The Arabian Gulf and Beyond

Second only to the United States in oil consumption, demand for transportation fuels from China's growing middle class has been a factor in worldwide crude oil demand for years. The International Energy Agency expects China's oil demand to continue expanding at a rate of 4.7% in 2010, reaching 8.9 million barrels per day. If demand growth continues along its current trajectory and reaches expected levels of 11.5 million barrels per day by 2015, it could lead to significant increases in ton‐mile demand. For illustrative purposes, assuming that these incremental volumes are sourced from the Arabian Gulf, it could create demand for an additional 80 VLCCs to meet Chinese demand by 2015. In fact, these barrels are likely to be sourced from farther away, resulting in even higher ton‐mile demand.

The Arabian Gulf has accounted for more than 70% of Chinese imports since 2005. Establishing relationships with a wider array of suppliers has therefore been a focus of China's foreign policy in recent years. Depressed global demand in the wake of the financial crisis last year provided China with several opportunities to secure access to crude oil and other commodities through mergers, acquisitions and loan‐for‐oil agreements. These strengthened relationships with future suppliers will likely contribute to the development of new tanker trade routes in coming years, particularly from Atlantic Basin suppliers such as Angola and Brazil.

### Refining its Role

Also likely to impact future trade routes are the Chinese government's efforts to discourage the import of refined petroleum products, most prominently by supporting rapid growth in China's domestic refining sector. The construction of large, complex refineries within China, some built in cooperation with foreign multinationals, has helped expand Chinese crude oil demand in recent years and this trend is expected to continue, as shown in the chart below.

The stockpiling of crude oil and refined products in China has also played a role in supplying demand for ships throughout the market slowdown last year. While changes in Chinese inventories are thought to have contributed significantly to recent Chinese consumption, there remains a great deal of uncertainty regarding inventory practices in China. Beijing acknowledges ongoing construction of both crude oil and refined product storage facilities, which will allow more flexibility in controlling domestic prices and planning for emergencies.

### The Expanding Chinese Fleet

China's expanding middle class, strategic stockpiling, and complex refining capacity ensure that it will continue to be a large ship, crude oil story. Recent efforts to expand ship‐owning may ultimately lead to structural changes in the tanker market. A stimulus package supporting shipping interests following the economic crisis has focused on enlarging the Chinese controlled fleet. With Chinese charterers and ship owners currently controlling less than 10% of the VLCC fleet, the government has pursued its goal through a variety of means. China's Shipping companies are also looking beyond the Great Wall to international oil and trading companies as charterers of their vessels.

The economic crisis helped China secure access to much of the natural resources it will need to support its economy and many of the ships it will need to transport them. With future barrels secured and the Chinese‐controlled fleet expanding rapidly, Chinese companies are likely to focus next on developing long‐term relationships with the international shipping and oil community.
