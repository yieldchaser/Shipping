# Bulls & Bears Ring in the New Year

As 2010 comes to a close, a broad overview of equity markets leads many to believe an economic recovery from the lows experienced in 2008 is at hand. Debate as to whether the market is a leading or lagging indicator of real economic growth aside, the positive returns in the major indices cannot be ignored. Year to date gains of 9.8% 12.8% and 16.1% in the Dow Jones Industrial Average, Standard and Poor's 500, and Nasdaq, respectively, all bear testament to a stock market continuing to rebound from the troughs of the financial crisis. These gains however must be viewed in the context of current monetary policy, rising commodities prices, and a host of data to be truly relevant to the casual observer. As the graph below demonstrates, ship owners and tanker market participants may be wise to look closer to home in their own sector for clues as to whether the rebound in equities is truly indicative of a world economy on the mend.

VLCC Earnings (AG ‐ USG) VLCC Earnings (AG ‐ Far East) S&P 500 Year End Source: Poten, Standard & Poors Much like the past several years, 2010 brought with it unprecedented forays into the financial markets on the part of sovereign governments and central banks alike. The "burden of proof of solvency" ping‐ponged through European sovereign debt markets bringing a level of volatility, the likes and scope of which the world had not seen, to the currency markets. The United States Federal Reserve played its own part in the ongoing saga with the announcement of additional quantitative easing in efforts to help sustain the fragile economic recovery at hand. Stimulus events had an undeniably positive impact on equity and commodity markets as both rallied with each successive stimulus announcement. These events however, stood in stark contrast to the palpable fear that gripped investors the afternoon of May 6 th as the "Flash Crash" jarred bullish sentiment in the wake of a 1014 point decline in the DOW in a matter of moments. Amidst the continued commotion that characterized the economic climate of 2010 however, tanker rates remained subdued.

Given the multitude of factors affecting market sentiment it can be easy to get lost in every manner of analysis, news‐reporting and asset‐class correlation. However, a closer look at tanker earnings reveals an interesting relationship with equity markets as well as domestic economic growth. Looking at a ten year chart of VLCC earnings against the S&P500 index and the U.S. Gross Domestic Product, we can evaluate just how shipping earnings stacked up against equity performance and economic growth rates. By no means perfectly correlated, it is still clear to see the strong similarities between the different markets.

VLCC Earnings (AG ‐ USG) U.S. GDP (Millions) Source: Poten, U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis In both graphs the most striking correlation can be seen in the drop off in tanker earnings coinciding with both a weakening of equity markets and a decline in U.S. GDP for the two year period beginning mid 2007. The initial reaction might be to point to the equity market decline and label its movement the leading indicator for ship earnings. This observation, however, would fail to recognize that shipping earnings for the decade peaked nearly three years prior (mid 2004) and had been experiencing a continued decline as equities plateaued in 2007. Lending further credence to shipping rates' forward indication of economic recovery, a look at earnings versus GDP paints a somewhat compelling picture. Tanker earnings anticipated the steepening curve (accelerated growth) of GDP from 2002 through 2005 and responded notching highs for the decade in mid 2004. As tanker earnings fell for the next several years, the flattening of the GDP curve indicated a deceleration of GDP growth in conjunction. Remarkably, tanker earnings plummeted at nearly the same time the United States experienced a GDP decline in 2008 in what can only be described as the most striking correlation between GDP and tanker earnings in the last decade.

Looking ahead, one would be hard‐pressed to explain to most tanker owners that the equity markets are painting a picture of healthy economic growth at a time when rates remain comparatively low on a historical basis. The data implied by the charts can lead to the assumption that GDP growth (and acceleration) is anticipated in the form of rising tanker earnings. On this basis, with earnings still well below both decade averages and decade highs, it may come as no surprise to note that GDP growth continues to be stagnant in the United States. For equities and tanker earnings, the data would indicate that the current disparity will not remain for long, with either equity markets poised for contraction or tanker markets set for a significant rise in earnings (or some combination of both). The continued quantitative easing on behalf of the U.S. Federal Reserve, however, makes it difficult to be bearish on equities in the current environment. The resulting rise in inflationary pressure felt by ship owners in the form of higher oil and bunker prices as a result of this policy will necessitate an environment of rising rates if the industry is to maintain healthy levels of profitability.
