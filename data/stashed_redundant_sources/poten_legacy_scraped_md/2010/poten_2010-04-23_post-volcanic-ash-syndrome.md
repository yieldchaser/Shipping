# Post Volcanic Ash Syndrome

As market dynamics unleashed along with columns of smoke and ash from the Eyjafjallajokull volcano continue to work their way through the international economy, the full effect of the week's events has yet to fully reveal itself. It is already clear that the cessation of airplane traffic across Europe sharply reduced demand for jet fuel in the region, with consequences that filtered through crude oil and refined product markets this week. The tanker market impact of the transportation chaos, however, will become clearer only in the days to come, as refiners, traders, charterers and owners continue to digest this week's developments.

Europe is an important market for jet fuel, typically consuming about 1.2 million barrels per day of the refined petroleum product. The disruption of tens of thousands of flights since last week has cut European demand for jet fuel and swelled European stockpiles of the product. The sudden disappearance of airline demand led the price of jet fuel in the region to drop sharply, as shown in the chart below. Despite the presence of jet fuel supplies already in storage, traders scrambled to take advantage of opportunities stemming from the discounted supplies created by the extraordinary conditions in the market.

Jet fuel cargoes are often delivered to pipelines that carry supplies directly to airports and storage at these sites tends to be limited. With flights grounded and little additional onshore storage available, the logistics of delivering jet fuel to customers in Northwest Europe were severely complicated, prompting some airlines to declare force majeure and stop accepting delivery. Lufthansa, for example, declared force majeure on jet fuel deliveries on Tuesday and the Frankfort airport reportedly cancelled deliveries from a pipeline linking it to the nearby Rotterdam refinery. British Airways said that it was working with their suppliers to manage deliveries while the operator of the Central European Pipeline System (CEPS) said that it would reduce its intake of jet fuel starting Tuesday.

Vessels carrying jet fuel cargoes which were unable to discharge in Northwest Europe were forced to remain at port on demurrage, or negotiate short‐term extensions on special storage rates.

### "Majeure" Challenges

With some of the fuel's main customers unable to accept delivery, European refiners must confront the challenge of what to do with jet fuel supplies intended for the continent's cancelled flights. Area refiners producing unneeded kerosene have two main options: they can blend the excess fuel into middle distillates or change their configurations to reduce jet fuel production. Because jet fuel is a grade of kerosene with a boiling point between those of naphtha and gasoil, refiners have a degree of flexibility in deciding which product to favor in their output adjustments. Refineries' decision‐making on the issue will likely be influenced by how long it takes for logistical operations at airports to recover from the ash‐related disruptions as well as any indications of demand they receive from the market.

If refiners choose to blend their excess jet fuel into distillates, they will likely introduce cargoes into an already over‐supplied market, potentially putting downward pressure on oil prices and clean market freight rates in the region. Such large scale blending would be very expensive for refiners but would also allow them to avoid taking loses on the entirety of their excess jet kerosene production.

### Floating Inquiries

A contango pricing structure in European jet fuel to gasoil swap markets was widened by the sharp drop in near‐term jet fuel demand following the flight stoppage. The premium for jet fuel in Northwest Europe over gasoil futures on the ICE exchange narrowed from $45 per metric ton on April 15 to $35 per metric ton on April 19, as shown in the following chart. This narrowing fueled speculation that excess supplies would find their way into floating storage. Jet fuel's water content limits its shelf life in storage and is thought to hinder the attractiveness of this play, but the opportunity did spur an uptick in floating storage inquiries in region. At least one trader is reported to have chartered an LR2 for storage of jet fuel on a two to three month charter in the La Havre area and several other storage fixtures with unidentified cargoes in region have been reported.

### Tankers Awaiting

Going forward, unwinding the logjam surrounding delivery of jet fuel to airports in Europe is likely to keep ships out of the market and could lend some support to clean carrier freight rates. Some increase in floating storage fixtures as a result of opportunities created by the jet fuel demand disruptions in Europe this week is likely, though which cargoes are most likely to be stored on those fixtures depends mostly on what regional refiners choose to do with their jet fuel.

Floating storage inquiries for jet fuel in Northwest Europe this week were inspired mostly by an extraordinary event with specific logistical consequences. It is nonetheless interesting to note that such a short‐term, narrow trading opportunity spurred a marked increase in inquires. Such a willingness to quickly turn to tanker brokers when onshore space gets tight may be the legacy of the unprecedented levels of floating storage experienced in last year's market. The existence of previously‐agreed contract clauses and persistently low clean freight rates have helped solidify the status of tankers as a viable option for short‐term storage opportunities.
