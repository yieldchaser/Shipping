# Let the Finger Pointing Begin!

The effort to stop the gushing oil well 5,000 feet below sea level in the Gulf of Mexico has moved into a critical next stage with the arrival of a four‐story containment dome designed to capture the oil at the site. The 100‐ton concrete and steel dome is being guided by cameras as robots lower it directly above the well currently spewing oil into the U.S. Gulf. The dome will then be connected to a vessel at the surface, which, if successful, officials hope will capture as much as 85% of the oil coming from the site by Sunday.

As oil has started to make landfall in some areas of the Gulf Coast , as shown in the map below, the political reaction to the spill has moved into its next phase as well. The ultimate legacy of the incident may depend partially on the success or failure of the ongoing containment effort, but it is already clear that Deepwater Horizon has become a watershed incident likely to stall already‐sputtering efforts to pass comprehensive energy legislation.

### Managing Expectations

Despite the expectations of many, the Deepwater Horizon spill has, to date, had only a moderate impact on freight rates in the tanker market. Specially‐crafted clauses have been inserted into charterparties for voyages through the area, as owners naturally seek a premium for taking the risks associated with bringing their vessels into the US Gulf. Most important waterways have been able to remain open, though the threat of possible delays reportedly forced the diversion of at least one crude oil tanker headed to the region earlier this week. In the weeks to come, the focus is likely to shift to clean‐ up efforts and the task of cleaning hulls of ships which have been forced to traverse the oil.

Over the past week, reactions to the spill have moved beyond initial shock and concern for the region and have started to reflect existing political divisions surrounding energy policy. BP is reported to be painting a worst‐case scenario after being called to Washington for closed‐door briefings for lawmakers this week. There have been calls by some lawmakers to raise the liability limit from the $75 million level set by the Oil Pollution Act of 1990 to as much as $10 billion. Special interest groups have run television ads calling for offshore drilling plans to be amended in light of the spill and the Minerals Management Service, responsible for regulating offshore drilling, has already come under increased scrutiny for its ties to energy companies.

What role the reaction to the BP spill will play in the efforts to pass new energy legislation is not yet fully clear, but it is most likely to slow overall progress on the effort. Efforts by Senators John Kerry, Lindsey Graham, and Joseph Lieberman to craft a compromise version of the energy bill, which had already been complicated by the fight over immigration in recent weeks, is likely to be further slowed by the need to address concerns raised by the spill. Senator Graham, a Republican from South Carolina whose support for the new bill was seen as critical, has called for a pause in consideration of the issue and said that the proposal had "no chance" of passing in the current climate.

### Slowing Momentum

The clearest indication of the spill's immediate impact is the 30‐day moratorium on new exploration licenses announced by President Obama almost immediately following the accident. It is highly likely that the spill will delay those efforts for far longer than 30 days as it will take years for all of the investigations likely to stem from the incident to run their course. The spill has already raised questions about regulation of the deepwater drilling that was expected to play a significant role in the plans to sell domestic exploration licenses announced last month.

Whatever tightening of regulation that ultimately results from the spill in the Gulf, the fate of the Deepwater Horizon will likely also reinforce an existing trend of large oil companies shying away from working with less‐experienced contractors for such complicated drilling operations. Such developments would slow the continuing recovery of oil production in the US Gulf from outages of recent years.
