# Mira Mira on the Wall: Will Tanker Rates Rise or Fall?

While Venezuelans await Sunday's referendum to see whether or not their president, Hugo Chavez, will continue his six-year term, the oil tanker industry and the broader economic community await the impact the vote will have on the oil market. Chavez, who was elected in 2000, is faced with losing the presidency after his political opponents obtained two million signatures to force a referendum vote. Among the accusations against Chavez are his left-leaning social policies, mismanaging the nation's natural resources and contributing to a high unemployment rate. The opposition proposes to allow foreign investment into Venezuela to beef up its oil industry.

### Reliant on Exports

The resource-rich South American nation is the world's fifth largest exporter of crude oil, with much of it going to the United States. With about 1.4 million barrels per day shipping to the U.S., Venezuela supplies about 15% of U.S. imports, not including crude oil sent to the Caribbean and refined for the U.S. market. Last year of 2.3 million barrels per day (bpd) of crude oil produced, 1.1 million bpd were exported to the United States. The US is also a large importer of refined products from Venezuela. Upheaval Again This is not the first time that Venezuelans have tried to remove President Chavez from office. Since he took office, he has faced down two coups in April 2002 and December 2002. Chavez has shown that his politics do not always lend themselves to prime utilization of the nation's resources. In 2003, he fired more than 15,000 oil workers, many of whom were trained to work refinery equipment, from state-owned oil company PdVSA after they organized a strike against him, reducing product exports next to nothing at the beginning of this year. Tanker Rates Rise on Chaos Spot freight rates rise on instability. In the same way Iraq's stop and go oil flow drove rates in the VLCC market up, Venezuela's political situation is cause for increase in WS rates in the Handymax and Aframax markets. Trans-Atlantic shipments ex-UKC have replaced Caribbean voyages for clean products, driving Handymax rates up. Aframax rates have historically increased on top of Handymax rates in the frenzy of trying to fix a ship in an uncertain market. The expectation is that the Aframax market, since that ship size is most used to transport crude oil from South America and the Caribbean to northern points, would suffer if Chavez is overthrown and the interim government cannot manage the nation's oil industry. This of course remains to be seen. In the past, Middle East cargoes have replaced Caribbean crude oil exports. In the meantime, rates this time last year were almost a full 100 WS points lower than they were this week (WS 130 August last year to WS 212 this week) for an Aframax voyage. Daily earnings for those voyages doubled from $12,500 to $27,700, respectively. But just look at the graph on the next page for evidence of spiking rates in times of uncertainty. It shows that when Chavez fired the oil workers in early 2003, Handymax rates shot up. The incident also jerked the Aframax market where rates rose from WS 165 for daily earnings of $16,755 in January 2003 to WS 323 in February for daily earnings of $27,730 for a Caribbean to US voyage.

### Tanker Replacement

The tanker market will most certainly feel the drop if 2.3 million barrels per day falls away from the market and will look to the Persian Gulf to replace it. With that, expect a rise in VLCC use and higher freight rates for these longer voyages. With oil consumption at historical highs, the United States will scramble to find replacement crude if their neighbor to the south stops pumping oil. Aframax voyages from the Caribbean to the US Gulf dropped off by 100 between 2002 and 2003. Each Aframax carries 500,000 barrels of oil. If this were to continue to happen, 50 million barrels of crude oil will need to be replaced from somewhere. VLCCs need apply.
