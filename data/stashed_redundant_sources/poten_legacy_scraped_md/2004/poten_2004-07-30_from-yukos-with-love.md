# From Yukos With Love

Russia's near stoppage this week of 1.8 million bpd of oil production caused a slight panic in the oil market. Crude oil futures rose to a 21-year high of $43 dollars per barrel yesterday on the Russian government's announcement that it had curtailed Yukos, the country's largest oil producer, from producing and selling oil. Press reports said the Russian Government would not let the company operate because it owed huge amounts of money in back taxes. The government yesterday told Yukos it could no longer sell its property, which was understood as its oil as well as fixed assets. The Kremlin later clarified the statement and said the company could continue producing oil while its other assets remain frozen. Although crude oil prices fell marginally, they ended today back over $43 per barrel. Investors sense that the problems with Yukos defy such a simple solution. Russia Second Largest Exporter Russia is the second largest exporter of crude oil behind Saudi Arabia. Yukos is one of the main exporters sending half of its 1.8 million bpd production to destinations outside Russia. One of the key drivers of the strength of the tanker market has been the increase in Russian crude exports. If the market were to lose a significant amount of Russian exports, the tanker market would certainly change. The world has grown to rely on Russia after their exports began to increase in 1991, as seen in the graph on the next page. Saudi Arabian capacity is currently about 9 million bpd though it has in the past produced up to 10 million barrels of oil per day to stabilize crude prices in times of need. It would be logical then that the giant Middle Eastern producer would fill the gap should Russian crude exports fall off.

### The VLCC Impact

Let's say that Yukos had been forced to halt oil production. If it were a total cessation for a short period of time, replacement crude could come from inventories. For longer stoppages, there appears to be two alternatives. Bearing in mind that a larger quantity of Russian oil comes from the Black Sea to the Med than from the Baltic to UKC, a logical solution would be to ship more crude through the Iraq-Turkey and the Sumed pipelines until they approach maximum capacity. Aframaxes and Suezmaxes will find some alternative employment on the Mediterranean terminals of the pipelines. VLCCs will find employment moving Middle East crude, which would be substantially Saudi Arabian, to the Red Sea terminal of the Sumed pipeline. It is problematic whether there will be enough cargoes to make up for the loss of Black Sea crude. Any remaining deficit will have to be made up by whatever limited spare capacity there may be in North and West Africa and the Caribbean. As these are probably close to capacity, some VLCCs would be needed to carry cargoes from the Middle East to Europe. This would place more upward pressure on VLCC rates. For Aframax and Suezmax, change in rates depends on how much Russian crude can be replaced by the two pipelines. For Suezmaxes trading Black Sea to Med, vessel earnings peaked at $117,000 per day at the start of this year and are currently about $43,000 per day; for Baltic to UKC, earnings peaked at the same level of $117,000 per day and are currently $59,000 per day. For Aframaxes trading Black Sea to Med, earnings peaked in January at $59,000 per day and are now $34,000 per day; for Baltic to UKC, earnings peaked in January at $79,000 per day and are now $33,000 per day. However the winter season was plagued with Bosphorus Strait weather problems that caused vessels to pile up, increasing transit time, which boosted rates. Where they would go after some cessation of Russian exports is hard to say, but initially they might climb in reaction to another disruption in trade and then fall when the world learns where the replacement crude is coming from. Which Charterers Would Suffer? In a sense, Russia would be shooting itself in the foot by ceasing some of its exports since oil is a major US dollar revenue earner for the nation. ### Financial Woes, High Demand

Yukos' financial troubles come at a time when oil demand is particularly high and violence in Iraq threatens output in that Middle Eastern country. Attacks against oil workers in Saudi Arabia leave the rest of the world a bit cautious about relying solely on that country's oil supply. The Russian government prosecutors who are working to seize Yukos' assets perhaps have not come to realize the high demand for Russian oil and the effect of cessation on production would have on the world economy. Yukos produces 20% of Russia's nearly 9 million bpd and supplies 2% of the world's oil. With the crude oil supply chain already stretched pretty thin, this would not be a good time to lose this volume of crude.
