# Product Tanker Rates Take Off

### Floating storage is tightening product tanker availability

Rates for product tankers have been rising since early March, both for the smaller MR's as well as the larger LR1 and LR2 tankers. While this seems slightly counterintuitive, it coincided with a steep drop-off in refined product demand. As a result of Covid-19, which caused lockdowns in large parts of the global economy, demand for jet fuel, gasoline and, to a lesser extent, diesel has dropped precipitously. So, it was not an increase in trading volume that caused markets to rise. Logistical bottlenecks, trading inefficiencies, port congestions, in addition to contango trades, were all factors that contributed to market tightening. In recent days, product tanker rates have increased almost exponentially. This was most likely due to a rapid increase in floating storage, which is limiting the availability of vessels on the spot market. This week, we have observed rates on various trade routes that we have never seen before. Records are being broken and there is no end in sight.

For the refined product supply chain to work smoothly, it is important that all the links in the chain are strong, that there is certain slack in the system and that markets behave in a somewhat predictable fashion. Under normal circumstances, the global oil markets are very efficient and flexible. Regional surpluses and shortages can be effectively dealt with and if there are problems in one port, you can reroute your ship to another port. A large part of the product tanker industry is built specifically to handle these regional imbalances.

However, now we are dealing with a completely different situation. There is a surplus everywhere, all at the same time. Although refineries are cutting back, they are still making too much product, so there is a huge product overhang. Storage tanks are filling up rapidly. For trading companies that are trying to take advantage of the contango pricing, it is difficult to find on-land storage, so they are increasingly looking for floating storage. Even regular trades, to the extend they are still happening, are disrupted, because vessels experience extended wait times in both load and discharge ports (if they can discharge at all). Even though most ports remain open, because they are deemed essential operations, congestion is rampant. Many ports are working with a reduced workforce and for the people that are still working, safety is paramount.

As you would expect the different ports have different rules. Almost everywhere, pilots need to wear personal protective equipment (PPE) when they board a vessel, and in some countries, vessel crews have to wear PPE as well whilst a non- crew member is on board. Many ports have instituted a mandatory period of self-isolation (typically 14 days), meaning nobody can board or leave the vessel until 14 days have passed since the vessel left the previous port. While this may have a limited impact on vessels that are on long-haul voyages, it creates significant delays for many smaller product carriers that are typically engaged on short-haul voyages that may only be a few days long. As long as the world is in the grip of Covid-19, it is likely that many of these restrictions will remain in place for an extended period of time, and even after that, they will only be relaxed gradually.

Shipowners seem to be big winners here, at least in the short-term. However, even owners face many challenges. Apart from the operational and logistical problems discussed above, they also have significant financial exposure. Vessel owners are typically only paid after completion of a voyage, and that is taking a lot longer these days. While the vessel is waiting to discharge, owners typically charge demurrage, which may also not be collected for months. In this market, it is even more important that owners carefully check the creditworthiness of their clients. The collapse of Singapore's Hin Leong Trading/Ocean Tankers is a stark reminder that some players in the energy space will not survive. Conservative owners will gravitate towards the largest, most reputable charterers, making it more difficult (and expensive) for others to access tonnage.
