# Seafarers Delivering Christmas

### The global maritime workforce deserves vaccine priority

The global shipping industry, through its supporting organizations, has come together to show its appreciation for the estimated 1.6 million seafarers in the world. Seafarers are the unsung heroes of the maritime business, a critical industry that carries around 90% of global trade, but seafarers are mostly taken for granted. In 2020, in the middle of a global pandemic, they have continued to keep international trade going while facing significant adversity. Throughout the year, shipping organizations and maritime executives have highlighted the plight of the seafarers to draw attention to the crew-change crisis caused by the Covid-19 pandemic.

On 24 September 2020, on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly, Captain Hedi Marzougui, pleaded the case for the seafarers, some which had been at sea for a year or more. At the time some 400,000 seafarers from around the globe were stranded on ships because borders were closed and flights were few and far between. This complicated efforts to bring in replacement crews, forcing the manning agents and shipowners to keep extending the contracts of the seafarers on board. The situation has improved in recent months, but hundreds of thousands of seafarers continue to work well beyond the usual periods of service at sea. A few days ago, the International Labour Organization (ILO) a specialized agency of the United Nations, adopted a resolution to address the dire situation of seafarers trapped at sea because of the Covid-19 Pandemic. It refers to the "immense risk that seafarer fatigue represents for the physical and mental health of the individual seafarers and for the safety of navigation, security and the protection of the marine environment." Among other things, the ILO urges member states to remove obstacles to crew changes, designate seafarers as "key workers" and consider (temporary) measures including waivers, exemptions or other changes to visa or documentary requirements.

Shipping is a truly global business. There are an estimated 50,000 merchant ships trading internationally, moving every type of cargo, including containers, dry bulk commodities, crude oil, refined petroleum products, LNG and LPG. The number of crew members on board a vessel varies with the size of the vessel, its equipment and the cargo it is designed to carry. Each company can operate their ships slightly differently, but all vessels must have the personnel to operate 24x7 regardless of size. Most crews of larger international vessels have between 20 and 30 members, divided between officers and ratings. The Captain is in charge. He is assisted by several deck officers, who are in charge of navigation and cargo handling and engineering officers, who run the engine department and the auxiliary equipment. called ratings who assist the officers. They can include oilers, stewards, cooks, electricians and bosuns.

Seafarers come from all over the world. China, the Philippines, Indonesia, the Russian Federation, India and Ukraine are estimated to be the largest supply countries for all seafarers (officers and ratings). The Philippines is the biggest supplier of ratings, while China supplies the most officers.

To keep the global economy afloat it is critical that merchant vessels continue to move goods around the world, even during extremely challenging periods like global pandemics. To do that successfully, we need to take care of the seafarers that work onboard these vessels. Hugo de Stoop, CEO of tanker owner Euronav, has been a strong advocate for seafarers worldwide throughout the pandemic. "If you stop shipping, you stop the world," according to De Stoop. "It's time we recognized the people working on the ships and the sacrifices they are making. They are the best ambassadors we have."

I could not agree more. We should all support seafarers worldwide by designating them "key workers" and ensure that they will receive priority vaccinations.
