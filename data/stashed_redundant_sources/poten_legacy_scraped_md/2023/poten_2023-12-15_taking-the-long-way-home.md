# Taking The Long Way Home

### Trans Mountain Expansion Will Transform Pacific Trades

In the second quarter of 2024, the Trans Mountain expansion project is expected be finalized. That is, if further delays can be avoided. The expansion will add about 590 thousand barrels of oil per day (b/d) of capacity to the Trans Mountain Pipeline, increasing it to 890 thousand b/d. Most of the additional volumes will likely be exported through the Westridge marine terminal in Vancouver. This terminal is being expanded to three berths, but the port can only handle tankers up to Aframax size. This means that Westridge will load around one Aframax per day if the pipeline operates at full capacity. As Vancouver is relatively far from mainstream Aframax trading areas, it may be a challenge to ensure the timely availability of sufficient tonnage in the area. This week we will examine the Aframax trades in the region and where tonnage might be drawn from.

Aframax tankers involved in dirty trades (crude oil and dirty products) are typically involved in regional trades as their economics are more suited to short-haul trades, where the scale advantages are less critical. The main global trading areas for these ships are in the US Gulf/Caribbean, around Europe and the Mediterranean and in the Middle East and Asia. Figure 1 shows the current positions of Aframaxes in dirty trades in the Northern Pacific Ocean. The map clearly shows the concentration of Aframax tankers along the coast of Asia as well as in the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean.

Analysis of Vortexa Aframax dirty trade data focusing on the Pacific (East of Suez) shows that exports from the Russian Far East to East Asia was the most common trade route in 2023 with 389 voyages. This is followed by Intra AG voyages (270) and cross Red Sea voyages (259). Trade between China, Korea and Japan contributed 206 voyages. Repositioning vessels to Canada's West Coast will not be straightforward. Ballasting from China to Vancouver takes around 17 days, while the voyage from the AG will take about 34 days, so this would require some planning.

Another source of tonnage could be tankers operating in the Gulf of Mexico, one of the main employment areas for Aframaxes. These ships could ballast through the Panama Canal and reach Vancouver in about 18 days. Unfortunately, there are currently significant delays as the Canal Authority is restricting transits due to low water levels in the Gatun Lake. This has significantly reduced Canal transits for oil tankers as other vessel classes use up the capacity. It is unclear how long this situation will persist, but it indicates that the US Gulf cannot be relied on as a consistent source of tonnage. There are also some Aframax tankers that operate on the U.S. West Coast, mainly involved in lightering operations around the Pacific Lightering Area. Export voyages from Ecuador to the US West Coast as well as to the lightering area off the Coast of Panama (PanPac) also employ a number of Aframaxes.

A complicating factor is going to be the availability of acceptable tankers for exports from Vancouver. Due to port restrictions, Aframax tankers are the most common vessel class used for Russian crude oil exports. Since the G-7 imposed sanctions on Russian oil trades, a significant portion of the Aframax fleet has become suspect. Figure 2 shows a sanctions risk classification from Vortexa/Windward. The rating is based on historical trading activities of tankers. Involvement in Iranian and Venezuelan exports (prior to the lifting of sanctions) or shipments from Russia after sanctions went into effect. Ships that carried Russian crude might be acceptable if it was sold below the price cap, but this is hard to verify. ESPO crude, exported from the Russian Far East trades at a premium to Urals and almost consistently above the price cap.

In summary, it is not going to be easy to integrate the Trans Mountain exports into the existing spot trading patterns, due to the number of ships required and the long distances from traditional trading areas. Over time, as the trade develops and charterers gain experience, these export voyages may attract a dedicated fleet of Aframaxes.
