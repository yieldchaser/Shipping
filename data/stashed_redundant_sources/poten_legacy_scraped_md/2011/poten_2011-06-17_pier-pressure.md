# Pier Pressure

The proposed project to expand Pier 400 - Berth 408 in the port of Los Angeles has engendered a fair bit of media coverage recently and begged questions as to the potential impact on the tanker market and the surrounding environment. Although it will not be the first berth capable of accommodating Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs) on the United States West Coast, the commencement of the project could yield material impacts on the overall traffic patterns, demurrage and turn around time for vessels moving into the Port of Los Angeles. However at present, it is difficult to see the imminent need for expanded berth capacity given the tepid economic climate. Regardless, the increasing role of imported crude oil in meeting US West Coast demand raises awareness about the potentially fragile supply scenario over the long‐term.

Crude oil production on the United States West Coast, also referred to as Petroleum Administration Defense District V (PADD V), has been on the decline since its peak of 3 million barrels per day in the late 1980s. Present production is in the range of 1.2 million barrels per day, evenly split between Alaska and California. While stagnant from a capacity perspective, refiner net crude oil inputs are in the range of 2.4 million barrels per day, suggesting that imports of crude oil are nearly on par with production volumes, as shown in the chart below.

### Crude Oil Imports

While the petroleum handling capacity of the proposed Pier 400 project is impressive - a stated throughput of up to 200 VLCCs per year - the actual volume of international tanker calls will likely be significantly less. Of the imported barrels for the entire U.S. West Coast, only 300,000 barrels per day, or one full VLCC cargo every six days, are sourced in the Arabian Gulf. On a practical basis, this equates to approximately 60 VLCC calls per year, most of which are lightered into smaller vessels offshore. Since California accounts for approximately 70% of PADD V's total crude oil imports the number of port calls needed declines further.

Other major suppliers such as Canada, Russia and Ecuador (see, "How the West Was Won" Jan‐11) are unlikely to see the largest crude carriers carry their exports. ### Diesel Death Drama

At present, vessels calling California ports burn 1.5%S max MDO or 0.5%S max MGO within 24 nm as regulated by the California Air Resource Board (CARB). When the North American Emission Control Area (ECA) commences in August 2012, it will extend to 200 nm off the coast requiring ships to burn IFO 1.0%S max for the incremental distance.* The change in fuel requirements will complicate and make more costly the operations of shore side discharge, potentially furthering the case for offshore lightering or even the development of an offshore terminal.

Although the case could be made that the United States is in need of port expansions and upgraded facilities, the slackened demand environment makes it increasingly difficult to bring large scale, capital intensive projects to market. Let us turn to the Texas Offshore Port System project, TOPS, for perspective. Some thought TOPS would have the potential to bring the lightering trade in the US Gulf to extinction, however lack of capacity commitments and funding ultimately brought the project to an impasse (See, "Lighter Lightering?" Aug‐08).

As domestic crude oil production dwindles, California will become increasingly dependent on petroleum imports, but sources of this crude oil will dictate the vessels employed. The existing trans‐Panama pipeline already adds supply diversity and undermines the case for increased VLCC trade. The current demand environment suggests that existing import infrastructure is likely sufficient for the foreseeable.

What is perhaps most interesting about this 15 year in the making project is that it seems to be driven by the long‐term premise of increasing California imports with little consideration for the existing tanker trades and efficiencies that already serve this market. Perhaps proponents of the project would be better served to investigate the existing supply means and whether the oil market is in fact clamoring for its development.
