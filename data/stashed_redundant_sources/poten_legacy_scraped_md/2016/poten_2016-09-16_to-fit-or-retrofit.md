# To Fit Or Retrofit

### How is the expanded Panama Canal being utilized so far?

Almost three months after the inauguration of the new locks of the Panama Canal we will try to capture some early indications as to its implications for and utilization by the large tanker market. Some of these early developments may have been expected, while others were less predictable.

On June 26, the first Neopanamax ship, the container vessel Cosco Shipping Panama transited the expanded Panama Canal, followed the next day by the first commercial passage of a Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) vessel. One month later, on July 26, the first Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) tanker, the Shell-chartered Maran Gas Apolonia made its transit through the new locks. Since its inauguration, the Panama Canal Authority (ACP) completed more than 160 successful transits through the new locks, which translates to an average of about two transits per day. As expected, container vessels have been the most frequent users of the new locks, followed by LPG vessels. Going forward, it is expected that LNG tankers will become more frequent users of the canal as U.S. LNG export capacity continues to ramp up.

How about large oil tankers? The MT Aegean Unity, a 2016 built Suezmax tanker under commercial management of Mjolner Shipping was the first (and so far, only) crude oil tanker to move a cargo of crude oil through the Canal from the Pacific to the Atlantic. The transit was completed on August 19. What makes this first crude oil move through the Canal extra special is that Mjolner, after discharging the vessel in the Bahamas, was able to pick up another cargo in the area and did another laden transit on September 5 th . Since then, a few more large tankers have indicated an intention to utilize the expanded locks and are on their way to the Canal.

How frequently crude oil and product tankers will transit the Canal in the future will ultimately depend to a large extend on the arbitrage and trading opportunities that can be developed by oil majors and traders. In the short term however, there is another factor that plays a role in the utilization by large tankers of the new Panama Canal: the availability of chocks and bollards to secure the ship's dock and tow lines. Essentially, the chocks and accompanying heavy bitts intended for towing vessels permitted to transit the new locks should be delivered with an increased safe working load of 90 tons and the supporting structures have to be designed to transfer the respective design load. Ships transiting the new locks need this, because the ACP is using tug boats rather than locomotives to assist vessels. These new standards were published by the ACP well in advance, but - at the moment - it is uncertain how many vessels are compliant. A preliminary review of the Aframax and Suezmax fleets in Q88 gives an indication of the number of vessels that have chocks with the required Safe Working Load of at least 90 tons and/or have a Panama Canal Net Tonnage listed (Fig. 2). Vessels delivered in 2016 and beyond will likely have the appropriate specifications; older vessels may need retrofits. While the parts themselves are relatively inexpensive, it is necessary to drydock the vessel to install them and the total cost of the retrofit, including out of service time, can add up to $150,000. This is not an expensive modification in an industry that is facing regular regulation changes that forces owners to spend multiples of these amounts for compliance. However, the industry is going through a particularly lean period at the moment and some owners may opt to wait for a scheduled drydock to make the required changes. In the meantime, the use of the Panama Canal by Aframax and Suezmax sized tankers maybe somewhat restricted due to the lack of suitable vessels.

Over the long term we do not expect this to be an issue. The capacity of the new locks is at least 12 transits per day (24 hour period), but the ACP started with one or two transits and is gradually ramping up the number of vessel movements to full capacity. Most forecasters expect that the increased capacity of the Canal will be largely utilized by container vessels and gas tankers (LPG and increasingly LNG), with a sprinkling of dry cargo ships, such as bulk carriers and car carriers. Add cruise vessels, some of which have already reserved spots more than a year in advance, and the room for oil tankers appears limited.
