# Down But Not Out

### Oil demand from North West Europe still relevant for tankers

Over the last 10-15 years the focus in world oil markets has been on Asian demand in general and the rapid growth in Chinese oil consumption in particular. On the supply side of the equation, the Middle East continued to be the focal point as the largest supply source, although other areas like Africa and Russia did become more important. More recently, production and exports from the United States have been getting a lot of the spotlight. One region that has not received a lot of attention in recent years is Northwest Europe. Once a major driver of global oil and tanker demand, Europe has gradually become a less significant factor. However, as we like to highlight in today's Tanker Opinion, Northwest Europe is still very relevant.

Back in 1970, the combined oil demand of the members of the European Union represented 27.9% of global oil demand. Its market share has been gradually declining since then. By 2018, the EU only represented 13.3% of global oil demand. Absolute oil demand in the European Union peaked in 1979 at 15.6 million barrels per day (mb/d). Oil demand plunged in the 1980s after the global economic recession, which was triggered by a rapid increase in oil prices after the second oil crisis. Oil demand fell to 12.6 mb/d in 1983, before gradually recovering to 15.1 mb/d in 2006. The financial crisis of 2008 triggered another drop in oil demand which fell back to 1983 levels. Oil demand has improved since, but we don't expect EU oil demand to recover to the levels of 10 years ago.

Most of the EU countries have mature economies that have become significantly less energy intensive over the years. Global energy intensity (defined as total energy consumption per unit of GDP) has been declining since the 1990s and the European Union is the region with the lowest energy intensity in the world. Continued focus on energy efficiency and renewables, combined with a limited population growth means that oil is unlikely to make a significant comeback in Europe.

In terms of tanker demand, however, Europe remains relevant. European refiners still churn out 12 million barrels of products and most of the crude oil that feeds these facilities needs to be imported. While oil demand and refinery runs have gradually declined over the last 10 years, so has regional oil production. North Sea oil output peaked in 2000 at just over 6.4 mb/d. Last year, it had declined to about 3 mb/d.

In the past, Northwest Europe (the UK Continent area) imported significant volumes of crude oil from the Middle East on VLCCs. However, over the years, the use of VLCCs on the trades to Europe has reduced. Out of a grand total of 327 average monthly VLCC voyages in 2018, less than 10 were destined for Northwest Europe. Suezmaxes are used more frequently. In 2018, a little over 20% of all global Suezmax voyages were destined for Northwest Europe. Almost all these voyages came from the Atlantic Basin. Out of the 90 average monthly Suezmax voyages in 2018, 36 originated in the North Sea, 24 in West Africa and 16 in the Mediterranean. Most of the remaining voyages came from the Americas. Aframax tankers continue to play a dominant role in European imports, moving crude from the North Sea, the Baltic as well as the Mediterranean and the U.S. In 2018, an average of 163 Aframaxes called in Northwest Europe and 119 (74%) of these voyages originated in the UKC/Baltic region. Another 23 Aframax voyages per month came from the Mediterranean and 20 from the Americas.

Since the ban on U.S. exports was lifted in December 2015, the U.S. has become a significant supplier of crude oil to Europe, initially using primarily Aframaxes and Suezmaxes. Recently, U.S. exporters have started to use VLCCs to move cargoes into Europe. As port and terminal infrastructure in the U.S. Gulf continues to improve, we expect that more VLCCs will be used to move U.S. tight oil to refiners in Europe, in particular after IMO 2020 becomes effective.

In short, Europe will continue to support tanker demand for smaller and medium sized vessels and, since it has the infrastructure to accommodate them, economics could favor using more VLCCs for transatlantic voyages.
