# No Blast From The Past

### While the movements in the oil price may resemble the

mid-1980s, the current tanker market is very different. The collapse in oil prices that we are experiencing now is not unprecedented. As a matter of fact, something very similar happened in the oil markets in 1985 and 1986. Within a period of 8 months, the Brent crude oil price went from $30 per barrel to $10 per barrel. What was the situation in the tanker market back in the mid-1980s? Is history repeating itself in the tanker market as well? Various oil market reporters and investment bank research analysts have pointed out that last years' events leading up to the oil price decline are very similar to what happened back in 1985: relatively stable, historically high oil prices, lackluster overall demand growth and expanding non-OPEC production. On November 27, 2014 OPEC decided not to cut production even though oil prices were in free-fall. This was very similar to Saudi Arabia's decision in late 1985 to give up its role as swing supplier and prioritize market share over price. Can we draw similar parallels in the tanker market? In 1985, doom and gloom reigned in the tanker market. Tanker owners were still reeling from the combination of the unprecedented 1970s ordering binge, followed by a significant and unexpected decline in ton-mile demand as a result of rapidly expanding short-haul crude supplies (first Alaska and later the North Sea). In the first half of the 1980s, freight rates were frequently below operating expenses, owners stopped ordering and scrapped vessels at a record rate. In 1985 alone, an astounding 15% of the VLCC fleet (18.9 million dwt) was sold for demolition, and 50 million dwt of tankers still remained in lay-up. Please note that this was by no means an "old" fleet as many of the VLCCs sold for scrap were less than 10 years old!

The decision by OPEC to flood the world with crude oil in the mid-1980s started the healing process for the tanker market due to a strong correlation between Middle East crude oil production and VLCC rates (see figure 2). As the market psychology changed, rates, and in particular, prices quickly jumped. The price of a 5-year-old VLCC, for example, almost quadrupled from 1985 to 1987 (from $10 million to $37.5 million). The current tanker market is at a different point in the cycle than it was in 1985. The excess supply in the market as a result of the sudden end of the shipping "super-cycle" in 2008/2009 has largely been absorbed.

The tanker market is now fairly balanced and rates have been in an upswing since late 2013. As a result, the market is reacting very differently to increasing volumes of crude oil in world markets. While increasing rates have made tanker shipping fairly profitable again, nagging concerns about the long-term health of the world economy in general and Chinese oil demand in particular have created doubt about the sustainability of the market recovery. Against this backdrop, tanker contracting has (thus far) not been as robust as the rate environment would suggest. If shipowners continue to show restraint and limit the contracting of additional capacity, the outlook for the tanker industry could remain favorable for several years to come.
