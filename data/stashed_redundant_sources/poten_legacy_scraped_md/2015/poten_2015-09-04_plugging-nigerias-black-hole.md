# Plugging Nigerias Black Hole

### New Nigerian Government overhauls oil industry

When Nigeria gained independence from Great Britain in 1960, the country was expected to develop into an African powerhouse. It had a large population and abundant natural resources, including significant oil reserves. However, despite (or because of) its oil wealth, Nigeria has struggled to reach its full potential. JODI data shows that Nigeria produced 2.2 million bbls/day (b/d) of crude oil in 2014. The Economist estimates that about 85% of government revenues come from the oil industry, therefore, the oil price decline of the last 12 months has significantly affected the government finances. One way of plugging the gap is to reduce leakages in the system resulting from corruption and siphoning off of crude oil. Mr. Mohammadu Buhari, the new president who was elected in March ran on a platform that included fighting corruption in general and especially around the oil industry.

One of the first steps taken by the new government was to replace the top management of the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation (NNPC). The new Group Managing Director, Ibe Kachikwu, former Executive Vice Chairman of ExxonMobil Africa, has promised greater transparency and has restructured the top management layers of the company. He promised to clamp down on graft and theft of oil, review contracts with JV partners and production sharing agreements, and provide transparency on contracts to the public.

Some initial changes have been announced already. NNPC cancelled unfavourable crude swap agreements totalling about 210 Kb/d with several offshore refineries.

On July 15 th , the Nigerian government announced that 113 tankers, mainly VLCCs, are no longer allowed to trade in Nigeria. So far, they have provided no reason for the ban, but the general thinking is that the ban was also related to a crackdown on corruption and theft. However, the list of tankers is confusing, as some vessels are listed twice and it seems that some of the tankers on the list have not called Nigerian ports in years.

Pipeline theft is so prevalent that the NNPC announced they intend to employ drones to monitor pipelines to prevent siphoning of crude. The government stated that in one year they identified between 3,400 and 4,000 attacks on its various pipelines. Just last week, Shell declared force majeure on crude export contracts as a result of shutting down two crude pipelines: the Trans Niger Pipeline (TNP) and the Nembe Creek Trunk Line (NCTL). The TNP was closed due to a leak resulting from a failed illegal connection while the NCTL was closed to remove crude theft points. On September 2 nd , Shell announced that the pipelines were now operational again.

In recent years, Nigerian exports have been redirected away from the U.S. where plentiful local light sweet production has displaced imports. Imports of Nigerian crude declined from 1.0 Mb/d in 2010 to 0.1 Mb/d in 2014. A part of this decrease has been absorbed by Europe, which increased imports from 450 Kb/d to 950 Kb/d, while the remainder went to Asia, mainly China and India.

Nigeria remains an African powerhouse with significant oil production and export potential. As a result of growing product demand and an inefficient refining industry, Nigeria has also become a sizeable importer of petroleum products, increasing its importance for the product tanker market. While many of the reforms in Nigeria will take time to take effect, they are expected to ultimately lead to more crude oil production and exports and the impact on the crude oil tanker market should be positive. The impact on the refined product market, however is less clear.
