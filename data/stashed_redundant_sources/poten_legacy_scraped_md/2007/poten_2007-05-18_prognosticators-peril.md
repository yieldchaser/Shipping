# Prognosticator's Peril

Having been blessed with the consistent ability to turn a black, messy fluid into gold, refiners have enjoyed strong margins in recent times. Although the refined products market has exhibited remarkable price resilience, some refiners are rethinking their plans to expand their Midas touch. Congressional testimony given this week by the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) underscored the reluctance of US refiners to commit to tentative expansion plans announced last year. A litany of reasons were put forth to explain this expansion slowdown. Shown below is a recent history of US gasoline production and refinery utilization. Given today's average utilization levels of about 90%, there is still some room for expansion.

### Thinking Long-Term

The confluence of many factors obstructs the view of future refinery profitability. Economic returns from expansion projects are conceptualized based on estimates of construction costs and expectations of refinery margins.

With construction costs for energy projects rising world-wide, it is not surprising that US-based projects are running up against viability check points during their early stages. Propelling costs ever higher are shortages in materials, delays in the manufacture of critical equipment and a lack of skilled engineering and construction labor. Rising costs directly erode anticipated returns.

While the current strongly positive refining margins are certainly nice to experience and must be a refreshing respite from the hangover of negatives ones incurred earlier this decade, refiners have long realized that margins are cyclical. However, current political sentiment does not recognize these cycles and expectations for future margins are clouded by this threat.

As ship owners and charterers well know, planning, evaluating and executing significant investments is a perilous task in any market. Commitment to a major refinery expansion in the current investment environment is hindered by a cacophony of commentary on high gasoline prices and the production of more alternative transportation fuels. Perhaps it is time to help refiners polish their crystal ball by clarifying and quantifying the role taxes and alternative fuels will play with respect to the nation's refining mix.
