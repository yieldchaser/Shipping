# Spread is the Word

There is no doubt that the tanker market has become increasingly complex in the past several years. Changing trade patterns, increased demand for ships, high profile incidents, and increased regulations have all served to affect the day-to-day spot market rates in the various ways. The VLCC market in particular has been affected by changes in the chartering preferences of spot market players. In the East, the VLCC market has been considered two-tiered for a few years, but it could soon become even more complicated. The chart below shows the relative spot market Worldscale (WS) rates for VLCCs trading from the Arabian Gulf to the Far East. Since the start of 2008 there has been a striking discount for non-double hull vessels, but as recently as November of last year it was far smaller.

### Incidental Effects

The reaction of the market to the Hebei Spirit incident in December was not as unanimous as one may expect. While the South Korean maritime authority may be looking to set an example for the region by accelerating the phase-out of non double-hull usage, on a practical basis non-double hull ships continue to trade.

What is perhaps most interesting is that some large charterers have actually increased the use of non-double hull vessels as programs have continued to grow in size. By comparison charterers that have adopted policies banning the use of non-double hull vessels are often tasked with finding a suitable vessel among a limited pool of available ships. Regional imbalances in ship availability are what often lead to sizeable swings in freight rates and create risk exposure for these companies on a purely economic basis. This is certainly the case today.

### Facts & Figures

The cost-benefit relationship is particularly relevant to the market today as the potential freight savings can make the non-double hull ships quite appealing.

At today's spot market rates, there is a 45 WS point discount for a non-double hull VLCC on the Arabian Gulf - South Korea trade. This translates into freight savings of about $2.0 million for one voyage. If an oil company charters two vessels per month this can translate into freight savings of nearly $50 million per year. All of a sudden this begins to feel like real money.

Quality starts with the vessel operator and the people manning the ship. Not all ships are created and manned equally. Blanket bans are not usually good for any industry and the tanker market is no exception.
