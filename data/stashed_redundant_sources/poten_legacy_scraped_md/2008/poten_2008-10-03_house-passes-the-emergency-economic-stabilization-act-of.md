# House Passes the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008

The following is a reprint of a noteworthy piece published by the law firm Dewey & LeBoeuf LLP.

On October 3, 2008, the United States House of Representatives passed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008 (the "Stabilization Act" or "Act").

1 The Senate had already passed the Stabilization Act on October 1, 2008. The Stabilization Act is only part of a much larger bill that contains various tax and other provisions. The Stabilization Act aims to provide stability to the US financial system by eliminating uncertainty regarding the value of mortgage-related financial instruments, restoring confidence among financial institutions regarding this aspect of their mutual viability as trading partners and reopening the market for the credit and other financial transactions that the US economy depends on. The principal difference between this version of the Act and that which originally failed to pass the House is the addition of a temporary increase in the amount of deposit insurance and the addition of a large number of unrelated tax provisions. This Client Alert discusses only the principal elements of the Stabilization Act that relate to the current financial crisis.

Overview . Fundamentally, the Stabilization Act: Authorizes the Secretary of the Treasury to establish programs to either purchase or guarantee mortgage-related assets; Defines the institutions and asset types eligible to participate in these programs; Provides for up to $700 billion to be made available for these programs, but only in tranches, subject to various approval procedures; 1 The full text of the legislation may be found at http://financialservices.house.gov/essa/essabill.pdf and the House Financial Services Committee's section-by-section summary may be found at http://financialservices.house.gov/essa/eesabill_section-by-section.pdf .

Creates an Office of Financial Stability to conduct these programs; Establishes oversight and transparency procedures for the Treasury's dealings with its acquisition and pricing efforts; Requires Treasury to assist consumers in obtaining mortgage relief by offering to modify the terms of mortgages Treasury ends up owning; Constrains executive compensation at institutions that receive assistance under the Stabilization Act; Provides a very limited rule allowing "Applicable Financial Institutions" to treat gains and losses from the sale or exchange of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac preferred stock as ordinary income or loss; Orders that the financial regulatory system in the US be studied further and that recommendations for improvement be made; Increases until December 31, 2009 the coverage of deposits in FDIC- insured banks and in credit unions from $100,000 to $250,000; Permits some immediate relief on mark-to-market accounting; and Extends from January 1, 2010 until January 1, 2013 the exclusion from taxation of income from gains that can result from the discharge of indebtedness with respect to a principal residence.

Eligible Institutions . The Stabilization Act applies to institutions established and regulated under the laws of the United States and the states, commonwealths and territories. The word "established" means that US branches and agencies of international institutions are covered, so long as these branches and agencies are regulated in the US and have "significant operations in" the US. The Act states that these institutions include, but are not limited to, banks, savings associations, credit unions, securities brokers and dealers, and insurance companies. Although the Act uses the term "financial institution," it does not specify what kinds of activities are "financial" or what constitutes "regulation."

Eligible Assets . Eligible assets consist of all types of mortgages and securities, obligations and instruments "based on or related to" all types of mortgages, so long as the mortgages, securities, obligations or instruments were "originated or issued" on or before March 14, 2008 ("Troubled Assets"). The Act contains additional authority for the purchase of any other financial instrument if the purchase is determined to be necessary to promote financial market stability.

Approaches to Dealing with Troubled Assets . The Treasury is given authority to "purchase, and to make and fund commitments to purchase," Troubled Assets through a program called the Troubled Asset Relief Program, or "TARP." In addition, if it uses its purchase authority, it must also establish a program to make available guarantees of up to 100 percent of the value of any Troubled Assets. As part of guaranteeing assets, Treasury must charge the institution being assisted a premium "in an amount that the Secretary determines necessary to meet the purposes of [the] Act and to provide sufficient reserves" to meet anticipated (actuarially determined) claims and protect taxpayers. The authority of the Treasury and the Office of Financial Stability is broad and would appear to offer the possibility of creating numerous innovative structures to protect both financial institutions and shareholders. The Stabilization Act provides authority for the Treasury to manage assets it acquires. This authority appears to be applicable to both the purchase and the guarantee programs and should assist in making innovative structures possible. It is unclear what the effect of Section 106(d) might be on the ability to craft its proposals. That section requires Treasury to transfer the revenues and proceeds of Troubled Assets it acquires to the Treasury's general fund for the reduction of the public debt.

Amounts Available . Initially, the Treasury's purchase authority is limited to $250 billion. This limitation and the other purchase authority limits set forth in the Act are reduced by an amount equal to the difference between the outstanding guarantees and the balance in the fund that contains the "fees" (presumably, the premiums) collect under the guarantee program. The reference to the balance presumably means that premiums and other amounts to be collected in the future cannot be counted until received.The Treasury's purchase authority can be increased to $350 billion by the President at any time and up to $700 billion by the President acting with Congress. Congress may also block the exercise of purchase authority in excess of $350 billion. Interestingly, the dollar limitation on the Treasury's purchase authority is phrased in terms of amounts "outstanding at any one time," which perhaps means that Treasury's authority is like a revolving line of credit.

Transparency and Supervision . The Comptroller General will have oversight over the program established by the Treasury and must issue reports at least every 60 days. Annual audits are required, as is an effective system of internal control, such as that required by Sarbanes-Oxley. The Treasury must also separately make monthly reports to Congress. Periodic reports must also be made each time another $50 billion in assets have been purchased. Judicial review of the Treasury's actions has been expressly preserved. An inspector general will be appointed to supervise the Treasury's asset purchase program, and a Congressional oversight panel will be established. The Treasury must publish details of purchases, sales and other dispositions of Troubled Assets within two business days of their occurrence.

Homeowner Assistance . Federal agencies that control the servicing of mortgages are required to establish programs to aid consumers and engage in mortgage modifications. These provisions do not seem to apply to the Treasury as holder of mortgages under either the purchase or the guarantee program, but similar obligations are imposed on the Treasury by a separate section of the Act.

Executive Compensation . New executive compensation and corporate governance standards will apply to financial institutions that sell Troubled Assets directly to the Treasury and issue to the Treasury in the transaction meaningful equity or debt positions. These standards will include (i) restrictions on compensation arrangements that incentivize senior executive officers to take "unnecessary and excessive risks;" (ii) a claw-back on bonuses paid to senior executive officers based on financial performance that are later proven to have been materially different; and (iii) a prohibition on "golden parachute payments" to senior executive officers.

Financial institutions that sell through auction Troubled Assets exceeding $300 million will be prohibited from entering into new employment agreements with senior executive officers that provide golden parachutes. Financial institutions that sell more than $300 million of Troubled Assets will not be able deduct annually for tax purposes more than $500,000 of compensation payable to an executive officer. Financial institutions will be subject to a tax deduction limitation and executive officers subject to a 20 percent excise tax if payments based on the officer's termination of employment exceed three times the officer's average annual compensation.

It is possible that the use of the word "sell" in the relevant provisions of the Stabilization Act means that those financial institutions that participate only in the guarantee program, rather than the purchase program, will not be subject to these constraints on executive compensation. However, we note that the word "purchase" is part of the definition of Troubled Assets.

Lastly, for services performed after December 31, 2008, commonly-used deferred compensation arrangements between US fund managers and their offshore investment vehicles could be subject to current taxation.

Ordinary Income Treatment Relating to the Sale of Preferred Stock of Freddie Macand Fannie Mae . This benefit is only available to banks (including international banks), bank holding companies, mutual savings banks, cooperative banks, domestic building and loan associations, savings and loan institutions, savings and loan holding companies, small business investment companies and state chartered business development corporations ("Applicable Financial Institutions"). Generally, the Applicable Financial Institution must have held the stock on September 6, 2008 or sold it between January 1, 2008 and that date, and have been an Applicable Financial Institution on the date of the sale.

Additional Deposit Insurance Coverage . From the date on which the President signs the Act through December 31, 2009, coverage for insured deposits will be increased from $100,000 to $250,000. This increase does not appear to affect the existing limit of $250,000 for certain retirement accounts.

Studies . Studies regarding margin rules, the general structure of the US financial system and mark-to-market accounting are to be conducted.

Mark-to-Market Accounting . The Securities and Exchange Commission is given authority to suspend any requirement to mark assets to market for any issuer or with respect to any class or category of transactions if it determines such steps necessary, appropriate or in the public interest and consistent with investor protection. It is unclear what the effect of such a suspension would be on the comparability of accounting across periods and the kinds of disclosure that might be made in periodic reports. Interpretive advice as to such matters may well be part of any future determinations or interpretations by the Commission. The SEC Office of the Chief Accountant and FASB Staff have already issued "Clarifications on Fair Value Accounting" that may provide some relief to market participants. Those clarifications refer to earlier releases that provide guidance on how to make fair value determinations transparent to investors.

Discharge of Indebtedness Income . The extension of this exclusion for income that arises from debt cancellation should be of use in protecting homeowners whose principal residence has declined substantially in value.

* * * * * We expect that the Act will be signed immediately into law by the President. Funds could be available for use by the Treasury thereafter, although the Treasury must within two days of exercising powers under the Stabilization Act publish program guidelines addressing matters like mechanisms for identifying, valuing and purchasing Troubled Assets. The Treasury is no doubt hard at work preparing these guidelines.

The Stabilization Act raises a variety of issues relating to corporate finance, executive compensation, corporate governance and tax, and this will no doubt be true of the Treasury guidelines, as well. We intend to consider these topics in detail in the coming days and weeks and to publish in one or more client alerts our thoughts in regards thereto.
