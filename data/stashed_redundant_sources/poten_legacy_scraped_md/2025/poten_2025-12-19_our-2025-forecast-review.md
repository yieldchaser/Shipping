# Our 2025 Forecast Review

### Looking back at our projections for 2025

A few years ago (against our better judgement), we started an annual forecasting tradition. At the beginning of every year, we make a number of projections about developments in the tanker market and check them at the end of the year. Against that backdrop, on January 10 th , 2025, we discussed several trends/developments that we expected to happen in the tanker market in 2025 ("Predicting The Unpredictable"). As we are approaching the end of December, it is time to look back at our predictions and see how we did. We'll discuss them in order.

1. Trump's tariffs will change tanker trade flows.

This did not happen, at least not to the extent we expected. President Trump's "Liberation Day" announcements created significant confusion and uncertainty back in April, but almost nine months later, global trade flows barely budged as a result. Many of the initial tariffs were negotiated down and/or postponed, limiting their impact on the oil trades. While some of the U.S. trade deals incorporated increased energy purchases from the U.S., the overall volumes were not big enough to have a significant, lasting impact. This is not to say that the return of President Trump to the White House did not have an impact on the oil and tanker markets. Increasing sanctions on Russia (Lukoil and Rosneft), the attack on Iran's nuclear facilities, the USTR inspired port fees on China and (most recently) the Venezuela blockade are just a few examples of how the new U.S. administration was never far from the Tradewinds headlines.

2. Despite a ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine, the sanctions on Russia will remain in place.

This prediction was partially correct, but mostly wrong. We expected that President Trump would be able to deliver on one of his main campaign promises during 2025: End the fighting in Ukraine. However, while we still have a few days to go before the end of the year, and discussions are ongoing, a ceasefire does not look imminent. On the other hand, sanctions remained in place (and were tightened during the year) and China and India continued to be the key buyers of Russian oil.

3. China's economy will show signs of recovery, but the tanker market will hardly notice it The Chinese economy in 2025 was not significantly different from 2024. The challenges in the property sector persisted and China's economy stagnated amid significant trade tensions with the United States. Demand for transportation fuels continued to decline. However, Chinese crude oil imports remained robust among heavy stock building by Chinese refiners. This did provide support to the tanker market, although imports from Iran, Russia and Venezuela primarily utilize the dark fleet.

### 4.   Iranian exports will decline from their recent highs

We were wrong about this. Iranian exports did not decline in 2025. As a matter of fact, they increased (see Chart 1), despite a return of Mr. Trump to the White House and a resumption of his "maximum pressure" campaign. China imports virtually all (90%) of Iran's crude despite U.S. sanctions. Over the years, the Iranians have perfected the art of sanctions evasion through the use of dark fleet vessels and ship-to-ship transfers (often off Malysia and more recently off Indonesia as well). The U.S. has increased the pressure by sanctioning more vessels and even certain terminals in China, but the flow of oil continues. The Iranians need the revenues, and the Chinese teapots benefit from cheap oil.

VLCCs will be the best performing crude tanker segment in 2025 This prediction was right on the mark. VLCCs outperformed the smaller crude tanker segments as well as the product tankers by a significant margin in 2025, reestablishing themselves as the top earners in the tanker market. All crude tanker segments had a good year in 2025 (see chart 2), but VLCCs did particularly well. The key reasons for the VLCC (out) performance were clear. First of all, OPEC+ started to roll back their production cuts and the increases in exports disproportionately benefited the VLCCs. A boost in the long-haul Atlantic to Pacific crude oil trades also contributed. Brazil, Guyana and the U.S. Gulf all boosted their exports. Another key driver was the lack of fleet growth. One additional VLCC in 2024 was followed by only six deliveries in 2025, setting the stage for a remarkable freight market rally.
