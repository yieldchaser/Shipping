# Make America Build Ships Again

### What are the implications of USTR for the tanker market?

On March 28, we wrote a Tanker Opinion ( " It's Complicated " ) about the proposed actions of the Office of the United States Trade Representative ("USTR") to address China's alleged "dominance in the global maritime, logistics and shipbuilding sectors". Following a period of consultation and a two-day public hearing, the USTR published a revised notice on April 17, with actions that will be implemented in October 2025, after a 180-day grace period. The revised notice differs substantially from the initial proposals, in part to address objections raised during the consultation period. In this week's Tanker Opinion, we'll address the changes and the implications for the tanker market. Please note that Poten & Partners will host a webinar on this topic on Wednesday, April 30, entitled "Confusion Surrounds Shipping Rules Intended to Boost U.S. Shipbuilding". You can sign up for the webinar here .

Relative to the original proposals, the revised actions seem to be much more lenient toward oil and product tankers, except for Chinese owners and operators. They will have to pay a port fee of $50 per net ton beginning on October 14, 2025. The fee will increase by $30 per year over the next three years (reaching $140 per net ton on April 17, 2028. These fees are highly punitive. By 2028, a Chinese owned MR will have to pay almost $2 million per port call, while the fees for a VLCC exceed $15 million per call (see table 1). It will be little comfort to the Chinese owners that these fees will only be charged up to five times per year per vessel. Chinese owners will not be competitive under these rules.

Chinese built vessels that are not Chinese owned or operated are subject to lower fees, starting on 14 October 2025 at $18 per net ton and increasing by $5 each year to reach $33 per net ton in April 2028 (see table 2). However, while Chinese owners/operators have to pay, no matter what, non-Chinese owners of Chinese built vessels have been granted a number of exemptions. Among others, the fees don't apply if the vessel: Is at least 75% U.S. owned; arrives at a U.S. port empty or in ballast; is smaller than 80,000 dwt, travelled less than 2,000 nautical miles; or is enrolled in certain U.S. MARAD programs.

At the moment, it appears that the immediate impact of the revised USTR rules on the crude oil and product tanker market is limited. The above-mentioned exemptions mean that U.S. crude oil exports are not impacted as export tankers typically arrive in ballast. The 2,000-mile short-sea exemption covers U.S. Gulf lightering and makes sure that import voyages from Canada, Mexico and most of the Caribbean are not affected either. Product trades will continue unimpeded because they are mostly done on vessels below the deadweight threshold.

Obviously, there will be implications for longer-haul imports into the United States from the Middle East, West Africa, South America and the North Sea for example. However, most charterers and shipowners should be able to redeploy their fleet to avoid calling the U.S. with Chinese built tonnage.

The changes from the earlier draft to the current USTR rules have largely been improvements for the oil tanker market. The fees, where applicable, are no longer cumulative, and there are no fees based on the fleet composition of owners (i.e. fees applied if companies own some Chinese ships or have Chinese ships on order). However, there are some potential problems, mostly because of imprecise or expansive definitions (what exactly is a Chinese owner/operator?). Also, it seems that other shipping sectors (car carriers, LNG, LPG and containers) will be more affected, and it is therefore very possible that the proposed USTR action will change again.

The current USTR ruling does take aim at Chinese shipping companies but does little to address the lack of competitive shipbuilding capacity in the United States (except for LNG carriers). Requirements to use U.S. built tankers for an increasing percentage of exports were removed from the proposal.

While the USTR rules for tankers seem to have been watered down, there is bipartisan legislation that will be introduced next week in the U.S. Congress (both House and Senate). This "SHIPS for America Act" seems to incorporate some of the components that were removed from the USTR action, focusing on the expansion of the U.S. Flagged fleet and providing U.S. shipbuilding and port incentives. However, this is a topic for a future Opinion.
