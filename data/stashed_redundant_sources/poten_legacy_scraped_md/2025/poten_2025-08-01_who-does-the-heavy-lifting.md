# Who Does The Heavy Lifting

### Canada and Venezuela reshuffle heavy crude flows

Crude oil flows in and around the United States are changing, affecting imports and exports around North America. Some of the changes are driven by the shifts in supply and demand for certain types of crude oil, heavy crude in particular. In this Weekly Tanker Opinion, we will discuss developments in Canada, Mexico and Venezuela in particular and how they are having an impact on the oil and tanker markets.

Not all crudes are the same. The oil industry uses many specifications to differentiate between grades of crude oil. One way to do this is by sulfur content; sweet crude has a low sulfur content (< 0.5%), while sour crude has a high sulfur content (> 0.5%). Sulfur content is a key factor impacting the value, processing requirements, and environmental impact of the crude. The other differentiator is light versus heavy. Light crude oil is less dense, contains fewer impurities, and is more valuable and easier to refine than heavy crude oil, which is denser, more viscous, and contains more impurities. In simple terms, heavy crudes require more intensive processing to produce high value fuels, but they are cheaper to buy. The primary distinction between light and heavy crudes is measured by API gravity: light crude typically has a higher API gravity (usually above 35°), while heavy crude has an API gravity below 25°. Traditionally, the United States has had access to ample supplies of heavy crudes from nearby sources, such as Canada, Mexico and Venezuela, and as a result many refineries, especially those in the Midwest (US PADD2) and the US Gulf (PADD3) invested in upgrading capacity, such as cokers and hydrocrackers from the mid-1980s through the early 2000s. As a result of using more heavier (cheaper) grades, the average API gravity of crude oil used in US refineries gradually declined from 32.5 in 1985 to 30.2 in 2005. The advent of shale oil (which is very light) changed the dynamics in the U.S. refining industry. Over the last 20 years, the API gravity of crude oil refined in the U.S. increased from 30.2 to 33.2 as refiners started to use more light domestic shale oil, which was competitively priced due to its ample availability.

Geopolitical and other factors, such as geology and logistics, have also played a role in the changing U.S. crude oil landscape. Traditionally, Mexico maintained a strong position in the U.S. with significant exports of Maya crude to U.S. Gulf refiners. However, in recent years, Mexican production has started to decline, and the addition of new domestic refining capacity has further limited export availability of the heavier Mexican grades. The situation in Venezuela, one of the other major exporters of heavy crudes to the U.S. has become problematic due to mismanagement and U.S. sanctions. Venezuelan exports to the U.S. stopped completely from 2019 until 2023. Even after Chevron started producing and exporting again in 2023, volumes were only a fraction of the pre-2019 imports. The current situation is quite uncertain with the status of foreign oil licenses continuously changing.

Canada has become the dominant supplier of heavy crudes to the U.S. Production increased dramatically over the last 10-15 years and, until 2024, when the Trans Mountain Expansion pipeline was completed, virtually all the additional output was exported to the U.S. Most of the crude (> 2.0 Mb/d) is delivered to refineries in PADD2, while 400-500,000 b/d goes to U.S. refineries on the U.S. Gulf Coast. Prior to the opening of the TMX expansion, an estimated 200,000 b/d of Canadian crude was exported to China via the U.S. Gulf. These exports stopped after the TMX started up. Now, similar volumes of Canadian crudes are exported to China via Canada's West Coast.

What impact do all these changes have on the tanker market? Many of the flows around the U.S. are Aframax trades. Short-haul crude supplies from Mexico and Venezuela typically move on Aframaxes. The exports of Canadian crude to China via U.S. Gulf terminals was predominantly a VLCC trade, which makes sense given the distances involved. However, this trade has dried up since the TMX expansion opened up in May 2024. Port restrictions in Vancouver mandate that all exports from Canada's West Coast are done on Aframaxes, even the long-haul trades to China. Ironically, this means that while Canada now has "direct" access to China, charterers can no longer take advantage of the economies of scale of VLCCs.
