# More Pressure On Putin

### G7 countries are discussing significant additional sanctions

One month ago, there was cautious optimism about a possible resolution to the conflict in Ukraine. President Putin and President Trump met in Alaska on August 15, and while the summit did not produce any formal agreements or jointly signed declarations, both leaders described the meeting as productive. However, there has been no progress since. President Putin and President Zelenskyy have not met and the Russian attacks on Ukraine have continued unabated. This has led to increased frustration among the members of the seven largest advanced economies, the G7, which are now actively considering a new round of sanctions targeting Russia. This latest push is led by the United States as President Trump said that his patience with Vladimir Putin was "running out fast". In this Tanker Opinion we will discuss which new sanctions are under consideration and how they may impact the oil and tanker markets.

The key message from the U.S. is that to be successful, all G7 members have to commit to the new sanctions. Reportedly, the Trump administration is promising that U.S. will match European tariffs on India and China, if the Europeans act. Apart from the United States, the Group of Seven includes Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan and the United Kingdom. The EU participates as a "non-enumerated member". The proposals from the U.S. Treasury Department have a number of different elements. First of all, the U.S. is urging its allies in the G7 to impose secondary tariffs of 50% - 100% on China and India as long as they continue to buy large volumes of Russian oil. The U.S. already started this process by doubling its tariffs on India from 25% to 50% on August 27. The U.S. proposal also includes moves to create a legal pathway for seizing immobilized Russian sovereign assets, potentially redirecting these funds to support Ukraine's defense. Most of the roughly $300 billion in frozen Russian assets are held in Europe. At the moment, the profits that are generated from these Russian assets are used to provide loans to Ukraine. Other proposals target the financial and dual-use technology sectors that are connected to Russia's military and energy complex. It has been reported that the sanctions targeting Russia's dark fleet will also be expanded, although the details have not yet been disclosed.

The proposal from the U.S. follows additional measures from the EU and the UK. A recent EU package (the 18th since the onset of the war) further restricts Russian energy, lowers the oil price cap for Russian crude from $60 to $47.6 per barrel, tightens asset freezes, and increases trade restrictions on dual- use technology and financial services. One of the key elements of this package was the ban on importing refined petroleum products made from Russian crude even if delivered from via a third country, affecting Indian refiners in particular.

The UK also announced new restrictions today, adding 70 ships to its list of sanctioned dark fleet tankers, among other measures.

If the measures that are currently being discussed among the G7 members are implemented and strictly enforced, this will create significant pressure on India and China to reduce their purchases of Russian oil. For India, a trade war with the U.S. and the EU will be very costly and probably outweigh the benefits of having access to discounted Russian barrels. Unlike China, India does not import any Russian oil via pipelines. Prior to the invasion of Ukraine, India imported virtually no crude from Russia. However, India will not want to be seen as giving in to Western pressure and will resist immediate reductions. Two weeks ago, during the Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit held in Tianjin, China, the presidents of Russia, China and India met in a show of solidarity and resistance to the U.S.-led international order.

It is uncertain whether President Putin can be pressured to come to the negotiating table. However, a significant reduction in export revenues can - over time - hamper his ability to continue to wage war. In the meantime, the oil and tanker markets are keeping a keen eye on these developments. Significant supply disruptions will push up oil prices and could disrupt and reshuffle trade flows. As an ever larger portion of the tanker fleet is being sanctioned, mainstream tanker owners should benefit from increased ton-mile demand, not only for Aframaxes and Suezmaxes, but other segments as well.
