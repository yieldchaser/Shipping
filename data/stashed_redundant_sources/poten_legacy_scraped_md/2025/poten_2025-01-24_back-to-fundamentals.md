# Back To Fundamentals

### Underlying oil supply/demand drivers seem supportive

In recent years, the narrative in the oil and tanker markets has focused mostly on geopolitical events. The conflicts in Europe and the Middle East have upended global trade flows. Furthermore, the proliferation of sanctions against Russia and other rogue regimes (including Iran and Venezuela) has accelerated the growth of the "dark fleet", which operates outside the mainstream tanker markets. These geopolitical developments have been an important driver of oil prices and freight rates. With the election of Donald Trump and his ambitious plans to reshape the domestic and global energy landscape and address the conflicts in the world, there is no reason to suspect that the importance of geopolitics will diminish in the near term. However, we should not underestimate the importance of the underlying supply/demand fundamentals in the oil market. Ultimately, these will be just as important (or even more) in driving our markets in the long term. In today's Tanker Opinion we will focus on the fundamentals and try to separate the signal from the noise.

In terms of oil supply and demand, 2024 was a rather disappointing year. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), global oil demand increased by 0.92 Mb/d last year, a significant slowdown from the 2.36 Mb/d expansion in 2023. The main reason for the slowdown in global demand growth was Asia, and in particular China. Asian oil demand grew by 1.72 Mb/d in 2023 and only by 0.49 Mb/d last year. This decline in Asian demand growth accounted for all of the global slowdown. The changes in oil demand in the OECD countries and the non-OECD countries outside of Asia were minor in comparison. Fortunately, the outlook for 2025 is more positive (see Chart 1). Both the EIA and the IEA (International Energy Agency) show a pickup in growth relative to 2024. The EIA is slightly more positive as it expects 240 Kb/d growth in North America (primarily in the United States) while the IEA projects no growth in this region. There are some smaller differences in the other regions of the world, so the EIA expects total demand growth of +1.33 Mb/d for 2025, while the IEA forecasts +1.0 Mb/d of demand expansion. As has been the case in the last twenty years, Asia represents the majority of the global expansion in oil demand. However, in recent years, the emphasis is shifting from China to India. Absolute demand growth in both countries is similar; approximately 200 Kb/d for 2024 and 2025 (expected). However, the growth rates are very different. Based on 16.62 Mb/d of oil demand, 200 Kb/d means around 1.2% growth for China. India's oil demand is only 5.6 Mb/d (2024), so 200 Kb/d represents 3.6% growth. Unfortunately, given the proximity of India to the Middle East, the implications for ton-mile demand of a switch from China to India are not favorable for the tanker market.

So, oil demand growth is anticipated to pick up from 2024, mostly in Asia. Where is the additional oil expected to come from? A comparison of the EIA and the IEA forecasts shows broad agreement as well. Most of the supply growth will come from North America (U.S. and Canada) and to a lesser extent Latin America (Brazil and Guyana). With respect to OPEC, however there is a difference in methodology: The EIA forecasts OPEC production and they expect the cartel to increase output by some 400 Kb/d, reversing some of their earlier production cutbacks. The IEA on the other hand doesn't give projections for OPEC output. They calculate a "Call on OPEC", i.e. how much oil OPEC output is needed to balance supply and demand. As oil supply growth in 2025 is expected to outstrip oil demand growth, the IEA calculates that OPEC needs to cut output by 500 Kb/d. Whether this will actually happen is another question since OPEC has been losing market share for several years already. One of the wildcards in the global oil market is Russia. Despite elaborate Western sanctions imposed on Russia since the invasion of Ukraine, oil production and exports have remained remarkably stable. However, a recent tightening of the sanctions regime, in particular by the U.S., could change the situation in Russia.

Overall, based on global oil demand and supply fundamentals, the 2025 outlook for tanker ton-mile demand seems modestly positive. However, the risk that these fundamentals could be completely overwhelmed by geopolitical events in the coming year is quite high.
