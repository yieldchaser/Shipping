# The Tankship Tromedy

One of the more interesting books dedicated to understanding the tanker business was recently published and is entitled, "The Tankship Tromedy." The author, Mr. Jack Devanney, draws on 30 years of working experience in the tanker industry to be able to define it as a Tromedy - both a tragedy and comedy. This book provides an in depth analysis of spills that have taken place in the past and provides suggestions for prevention in the future.

The Tromedy, the entire system in which the ship is designed, constructed, owned and employed, shares responsibility for incidents at sea. Where many are quick to blame the crew for incidents, including some high profile jailings of captains, Mr. Devanney points to something much larger. Analyses of the incidents that have occurred in the industry illustrate how standards, set by class, owners and charterers have been the key drivers in some of the most disastrous incidents in history. His perspective is especially interesting given that most public knowledge about these events is primarily learned through media channels. Only Human? In the early pages of the book Mr. Devanney makes reference to the International Maritime Organization's (IMO) assessments that 90% of all maritime pollution incidents are due to human error. He goes on to point out that, "blaming spills or other tanker problems on 'human error' is a barren truism. Tankers are created and operated by humans." Looking at the genesis for the problems that lead to incidents gives insight into what we can do in the future as an industry to avoid these tragedies.

### Starts with Design

Mr. Devanney explores numerous areas for change and development. Improved ship design and maintenance are the first steps toward reducing the incidence of spills. It is not possible in this short space to review all the aspects of this work, but some of the areas addressed include: • Increasing steel weight for more robust ships • Standardizing the coatings for all vessels, both cargo and ballast tanks • Inerting ballast tanks • Building ships with twin screws for back-up for engine failure

### Age Doesn't Matter

One of the more counter intuitive conclusions is that the age of the vessels should have no bearing on whether or not it should continue to trade. Mr. Devanney's point is that a vessel, regardless of age, must be properly maintained. A poorly maintained younger ship can pose more of a threat than a well-maintained older ship. To assume a vessel life at construction of a set amount of years, say 25, intrinsically sets the ownership up for failure by lowering expectations. Ships should not be designed with a specific operating life in mind rather they should be built with the idea that they will operate as long as they are well maintained. Conclusion "The Tankship Tromedy" presents a candid look at the design and construction of tankers: the pitfalls, the conflicts and the successes. It is not light reading, but members of the industry, regardless of affiliation, would stand to benefit from such a read. Shipping experience has shown that it is far better for the industry to take the lead than for the political process to impose its will.
