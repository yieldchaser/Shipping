# In or Out?

When looking at the mounting tanker orderbook, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) phase-out regulation for single-hull tonnage serves as a natural check for a potential oversupply of ships in the future. Owners, charterers and analysts use this phase-out schedule in developing predictions about the future supply of tonnage in the world market. The question facing the tanker market is most likely one that the IMO and other regulatory bodies do not want to address: will these ships really stop trading?

Playing by the Rules Non-double hull vessels that were delivered in 1978 and 1979 must phase-out by their anniversary this year. By the end of 2010, the regulations state that ships built in 1984 or later must exit the fleet. However, the Condition Assessment Scheme (CAS) was established to extend the usable life of certain qualified ships until 2015, or the 25 years of life. The number of ships that are set to be phased-out by 2010 is relatively gradual and in line with historic scrapping activity. If younger non-double hull ships pass the tests from the CAS the bulk of the scrapping activity will be postponed until after 2010. Due to the relative age of the current fleet many of these ships will not be due to scrap until 2015. The answer to whether or not states are going to comply with the IMO is a combination of economics and politics. For several countries, it is in their best economic interest to continue to accept single-hull tonnage past the phase-out date. Of the total non-double hull fixtures in the past year, the majority trade from the Arabian Gulf to South Asia, intra-Mediterranean and intra-Southeast Asia. Whether or not the governments of the countries loading and discharging cargo decide to comply with IMO regulations remains up to speculation. What is interesting, is that our database actually records several fixtures on vessels that should have phased-out. Conclusion Higher demand for crude and products combined with the projected overall increase in ton-mile demand creates upward pressure on rates in the near term. The balance to the increases in the fleet that are expected in the next few years will be managed by increasing demand for tonnage, not the IMO phase-out schedule. In the long run, the phase-out can be credited with supporting rates, but mostly post-2015. It will be even more interesting to see over the next several years if vessels that should have phased-out continue trading.
