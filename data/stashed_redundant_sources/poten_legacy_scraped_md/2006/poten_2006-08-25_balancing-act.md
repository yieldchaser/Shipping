# Balancing Act

The recent indications made by the Federal Reserve hinted at the idea that the U.S. economy may be cooling off. Among the leading indicators that point to a potential slowdown in the economy are the decline in new home sales and orders for durable goods- cars, household appliances etc. Recently at an industry conference, Exxon Mobil Chairman and CEO Rex Tillerson commented that, "[Oil] prices at these levels are not healthy for economies anywhere." This begs the question: how much will high oil prices impact global economic growth?

U.S. Change in GDP The chart on the previous page shows the quarter-on-quarter change in GDP experienced by the U.S. versus crude oil prices, and an outlook for 2007. To date high energy prices have not impacted economic growth in the United States. The Chicken or the Egg?

Does energy consumption strengthen the economy or does a stronger economy mean that there is more capacity to consume energy? It is probably a combination of both. Stronger economies and higher GDP per capita mean that the average consumer is likely to have more disposable income to buy durables such as cars and their complement- gasoline.

### Gasoline Demand - 2005

Gasoline Demand - 2006 The graph above shows the total U.S. auto sales versus gasoline consumption for 2005 and 2006. Auto sales represent one part of durable goods sales and have been on the decline since the start of the year. What is interesting is that gasoline consumption levels for this year are relatively in line with last year, but auto sales are considerably lower. With the price for gasoline at historically high levels it appears that the demand will not change in the short run. In order to compensate for higher prices at the pump, people are not buying new cars.

### It's a Delicate Balance

While demand for crude oil and economic growth are all good things, the price implications can sometimes prove to be unsustainable. Perhaps there is some gasoline demand 'correction' in the future, but it does not appear to be in the near term. What is more likely is that these conditions will not result in less consumption of gasoline, but rather less consumption of everything else. In the longer term, the U.S. economy will slow substantially and the overall energy demand with it.
