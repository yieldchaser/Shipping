# A Game Changer In Africa

### The impact of this new refinery in Nigeria will be significant

On August 25, 2021, the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation (NNPC) announced that it will supply 300,000 barrels per day (b/d) of crude oil to the new 650,000 b/d Dangote oil refinery, which is under construction in the Lekki Free Zone on Nigeria's coast, approximately 30 miles east of the commercial capital of Lagos. When it is commissioned, the Dangote refinery, Africa's largest, will dramatically change the crude oil and refined product balances in Nigeria and the wider West Africa region. This will also have a major impact on the tanker market.

The Dangote refinery is the brainchild of Nigerian businessman Aliko Dangote, the wealthiest person in Africa. Mr. Dangote is the Chairman and CEO of Dangote Group, an industrial conglomerate involved in many different businesses. Three of the largest Dangote companies, Dangote Cement, Dangote Sugar Refinery and NASCON Allied Industries (salt & seasonings) are publicly listed on the Nigerian Stock Exchange. Early plans for the refinery were unveiled in 2013, but major construction began only in 2017. The company estimates to start commissioning the refinery in early 2022, but industry sources think that the start-up could be pushed back by up to a year due to delays caused by Covid-19.

According to Dangote's website, the 650,000 b/d refinery will be able to meet 100% of Nigeria's refined product needs and will have a surplus available for export. The company claims that the plant will yield 327,000 b/d of gasoline, 244,000 b/d of gasoil/diesel, 56,000 b/d of jet fuel/kerosene, as well as 290,000 mt/year of propane/LPG when fully operational.

S&P Global Platts reported that the crude distillation unit has been designed to process up to 12 crudes at one time but has been engineered to process three Nigerian crude grades - Escravos, Bonny Light and Forcados. The supply agreement announced earlier this week indicates a strong commitment from both the NNPC and Dangote to deliver on these promises. According to the NNPC, the agreement will give the NNPC the right to 20% of the production of the refinery. At the same time, the supply agreement will guarantee NNPC a market for at least 300,000 b/d of its crude.

Once the Dangote refinery is fully operational, Nigeria's crude oil and product flows will change dramatically. At the moment, Nigeria exports almost all its crude oil because none of its domestic refineries are currently operational. As the first chart shows, Nigeria exports its crude to a diverse group of countries, both in the Atlantic Basin and the Pacific. Unlike some other OPEC producers which favour terms deals, Nigeria sells most of its crude on the spot market. In 2019, Nigeria exported about 2.0 Mb/d, with about 900,000 b/d (45%) going to Europe and a further 178,000 b/d to the U.S. East Coast. The rest was exported to Asia (545,000 b/d) while smaller volumes went to the U.S. West Coast. Production problems in Nigeria and the pandemic have reduced exports by some 25%, which impacted all export destinations. Based on the export destinations, it is no surprise that Suezmaxes are the vessel of choice, with a market share of approximately 75% over the last 5 years, with VLCCs representing 25%. A significant decline in Nigerian exports resulting from the startup of Dangote would therefore primarily hurt the Suezmax segment.

On the product side, the impact will be felt on both the LR1 and the MR/Handysize segments. In recent years (2019-2021), Nigeria imported on average some 470,000 b/d of refined products of which 350,000 b/d was gasoline. This import trade will likely disappear when the new refinery starts operating. While Dangote may generate some export business, the net effect on the product tanker trades will be negative.

In conclusion, while the new refinery being built in Nigeria could be good for the country, the simultaneous demise of crude exports and product imports will be a loss for the Atlantic Suezmax and LR1/MR product tanker trades.
