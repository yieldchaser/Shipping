# IMO Dcs 2025: Is Shipping’s Decarbonisation Progress Fast Enough?

*Published on 30 October 2026*

Each year, the Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC) reviews a summary report that the IMO Secretariat prepares from verified data submitted by shipowners for the previous reporting year. The report covering 2025 data will be considered during the upcoming MEPC 85, scheduled for 30 November to 3 December 2026. The session will also be critical for discussions on the draft amendments incorporating the IMO Net-Zero Framework, ahead of the possible resumption of the extraordinary MEPC session on 4 December, when its adoption is due to be considered.

Drewry’s Energy Transition team closely tracks this verified data to assess the industry’s decarbonisation progress.

Annual fuel consumption was around 221 million tonnes in 2025, of which alternative fuels accounted for around 18.3 million tonnes. The use of alternative fuels[1] has been rising steadily, from 5.9% of the shipping energy demand[2] in 2019 to 9.5% in 2025.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

Biofuels maintained their strong momentum, with consumption doubling over the year. Their share of shipping’s alternative fuel demand increased to around 12% compared to 6.2% in 2024.

The lead alternative fuel, LNG, has remained stable YoY in absolute consumption terms, with total LNG consumption remaining just short of 15 million tonnes. Methanol’s consumption has grown steadily, but its share among all alternative fuels remains stable at 0.3% on an energy basis. Ammonia is yet to make its debut in the IMO DCS reports, and the stakeholders anticipate seeing a new column for ammonia in next year’s data.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

Despite continued growth in shipping activity, the industry reduced its overall CO₂ emissions by 1.3% in 2025. This came despite a 2% rise in vessel capacity and a 1% uptick in total distance sailed. Average emissions per vessel reduced 4%, while emissions per nautical mile contracted 2.3%. Increased biofuel consumption, which reached 2.5 million tonnes, and generally lower sailing speeds were among key drivers for this reduction.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

Despite the broader slowdown in fleet speeds, following the introduction of EEXI and CII, container ships and tankers recorded an increase in average speeds in 2025. This indicates robust demand and higher vessel utilisation, with longer sailing distances around the COGH, potentially contributing to the increase.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

In 2025, CO₂ emissions per vessel declined significantly, while CO₂ emissions per unit of distance (nautical mile) continued their gradual downward trend among vessels reporting to the IMO DCS.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

Shipping’s AER (supply-based carbon intensity[3]) also continued to improve in 2025, falling by a further 1.6% to reach 33.1% below 2008 levels. This aligns with the decline in CO₂ emissions during the year, despite growth in fleet capacity and distance sailed. If the shipping industry continues this reduction at the same rate, the IMO’s 40% carbon-intensity reduction target for 2030 seems achievable.

Source: IMO DCS

With 2025 marking the third year of CII implementation, vessels were expected to strengthen their energy-efficiency measures to maintain or achieve compliance. This is reflected in a 4% increase in the share of compliant vessels[4] among those required to report CII ratings to the IMO DCS. At the same time, the share of vessels not reporting CII ratings declined from 8.9% to 3.6%. Combined with the improving AER trend discussed above, this indicates greater familiarity among ship operators with CII calculations and the measures needed to improve compliance.

Note: Vessels that did not report their CII ratings are assumed to be non-compliant.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

Among the major cargo carrying sectors, a comparison of the 2025 CII data with the previous year’s ratings indicates that LNG carriers are struggling to maintain compliance under the regulation, with the sector’s share of compliant vessels declining 1.6% in 2025. LNG trade remains particularly exposed to geopolitical conflicts, which can alter trading patterns and consequently affect the sector’s GHG-emission performance. In 2025, shifting trade requirements increased demand for less-efficient LNG carriers, adding further pressure on the sector’s CII performance.

Note: The above calculation includes vessels that did not reported their CII ratings to IMO DCS as non-compliant.

Source: IMO DCS,Drewry Maritime Research

As the industry inches closer to the postponed MEPC ES2 (Extraordinary Session 2) planned for 4 December 2026 to further discuss the fate of the drafted ‘IMO Net Zero Framework’, Drewry’s analysis of the 2025 IMO DCS data shows some encouraging signs of progress. Despite vessel capacity improving 2% and distance sailed rising 1%, shipping managed to reduce its overall CO₂ emissions by 1.3% in 2025 (as compared to 2024). Shipping’s AER also improved further, taking supply-based carbon intensity to 33.1%, below 2008 levels, gradually closing the gap towards the IMO’s 40% reduction target for 2030.

Alternative fuels continued to gain ground, accounting for around 9.5% of shipping’s energy demand in 2025. Biofuel blends remained the key growth story, with consumption more than doubling during the year to around 2.5 million tonnes. At the same time, the third year of CII implementation showed improving compliance, with the share of compliant vessels increasing by 4%.

Overall, the 2025 numbers suggest shipping is moving in the right direction on efficiency and carbon intensity, but the pace remains gradual. With further consideration of the IMO’s mid-term measures expected at the resumed MEPC ES2 on 4 December 2026, an outcome with a price gap reduction mechanism could significantly impact the pace at which shipping moves from incremental efficiency improvements towards deeper emissions reductions.

Notes:

[1] Conventional fuels are fossil-based marine fuels that are traditionally used for ship propulsion.Alternative fuels are fuels other than conventional fossil fuels that offer reduced GHG emissions.

[2] For this analysis, shipping’s energy demand is calculated in terms to VLSFOeq. This approach converts different fuels into an equivalent amount of VLSFO based on the energy they provide.

[3] “Supply-based carbon intensity” considers the carbon emissions with respect to aggregate capacity of available vessels.

[4] For this analysis, A-, B- and C-rated vessels are considered CII-compliant, whereas vessels with D and E ratings are considered non-compliant. While these non-compliant vessels are still allowed to sail for now, they are required to comply in the upcoming years. Moreover, the vessels that did not report their CII ratings on IMO DCS are also considered as non-compliant.
