### SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK

Total shipment volume growth has fallen from 4.6% in June to 4.0% at the end of November, which is still higher than the supply growth of 3.0%. Despite fundamentals being positive by this measure, markets have fallen as growth in West of Suez shipments has dropped from 6% in July to 2.5% in November, and there is no congestion in loading or discharge ports. Further, vessel sailing speeds remain low but have trended upward since the beginning of August. 

Our market viewpoints always start by analyzing shipment volume growth against supply growth, as the other factors are more uncertain. Since Q4 last year, our view has been that shipment volume growth would drop below supply growth during the first half of 2025, so downside risk would escalate during the second half of 2025. We still seem on track for that development, which is a little worrying considering where earnings are now. On the other hand, as shipment volume growth remains positive it could mean that markets will rebound strongly after the Chinese New Year next year if sailing distances, congestion, and sailing speeds go in owners favor. 

Nonetheless, the outlook remains bearish, and we do not think that will change before Q4 2025 at the earliest, and Q2 2026 at the latest.

## PERIOD RATES AND ASSET VALUES

Basis the correlation between earnings and asset values since 2010, we believe the following adjustments have to be made in order to bring the relationship back to normal. 

Handysize: 1-year time charter rates either have to rise above 15.000 USD pd, or the 10-year-old value has to fall to around 17 million USD. 
Supramax: 1-year time charter rates either have to rise to the high-teens USD pd, or the 10-year-old value has to fall to around 20 million USD. 
Kamsarmax: 1-year time charter rates either have to rise to the high-teens USD pd, or the 10-year-old value has to fall to around 20 million USD. 
Capesize: 1-year time charter rates either have to rise towards 25.000 USD pd, or the 10-year-old value has to fall to around 35 million USD.

We expect values to drop over the coming months. However, we also note a lot of potential buying interest, so we do expect some support to values as long as earnings rebound after the Chinese New Year.

## MACRO ECONOMIC OUTLOOK

In our previous monthly update from early November, we went through our viewpoints on China's stimulus measures. 

Quote:

*China's currency trades under a so-called 'managed float', which means the central bank is heavily involved in trying to keep it trading around a desired level. This process sometimes obviously involves buying Yuan against the USD, and vice versa. The USDCNY is therefore an important part of the domestic liquidity situation. Recent stimulus measures in China will naturally involve expanding the domestic money supply to fund infrastructure projects as well as trying to revive the property sector. That, along with weaker domestic economic growth rates means there should be heavy downward pressure on the Yuan.

To counteract this downward pressure, the central bank has to buy increasing amounts of Yuan to keep the currency stable, with the flipside of this being that it nullifies other liquidity-increasing factors. That is why there is a very high correlation between the 9-month lead of the year-on-year change of the USDCNY and China's year-on-year growth of dry bulk imports. The chart displaying this relationship is shown in the middle left below. Over the last year, the Yuan has strengthened relative to the USD, which strongly suggests China's dry bulk import growth will continue to fall over the coming 9 months.

In our view, China's government has to allow the Yuan to weaken considerably going forward for the stimulus measures to succeed. The other thing to watch in this regard is obviously the actual financing growth domestically. Reserve requirement and interest rate cuts have not led to higher financing growth this year, which in our view goes back to the still too-strong currency. Seasonally, monthly financing is always the highest during Q1, especially in January. It will therefore be very interesting to see how high (or low) those numbers are at the beginning of next year.*

Unquote

One month later, China's credit growth rate has dropped further, and the USDCNY is not suggesting that import growth will pick up within the next 9 months. 
Steel industry experts in China remain skeptical of the outlook for demand in 2025. A recent report from Platts outlined the situation well, which reads that the issuance of local government special bonds is up 81% on the same period last year. However, despite this massive increase in bond issuance, infrastructure investment increased by just 4.3% through January-October vs the same period in 2023. The reason for the low growth is that most of the bonds have been used to pay down or swap local government debt, rather than funding new projects. The consensus for next year is that flat steel products demand will be relatively good as infrastructure related to clean energy will continue to expand, whereas long steel products will remain under pressure primarily due to prevailing weakness in the property sector. 

In the World ex-China, manufacturing is not showing much strength. JP Morgans's latest Manufacturing PMI report had the global reading at 50, stabilizing after four months of contraction. More than half of the World's countries included in the report had a reading below 50, with the Eurozone in particular being weak. 

The below charts paint a bearish picture going forward. 

The top left chart shows the total dry bulk imports of Japan, Korea, Taiwan, and Europe, which have remained at average monthly import volumes of 65 million tons since the start of 2023, compared to 75 million tons in 2021 and 2022. No upturn has materialized this year despite the global economy being on an upswing, which is highly unusual. 

The top right chart shows an index we make tracking China's economic growth. The index is composed of property market data, fixed asset investments, power generation, and freight volumes handled in ports. This index is highly correlated to the year-on-year growth of total dry bulk demand growth. This year, the index has been negative whereas dry bulk demand growth has grown by about 4%. The last time there was such a mismatch between these two data series was in 2014. 

The middle left chart shows China's bulk commodity import growth vs the year-on-year change of the USDCNY. We explained this dynamic in our previous monthly report. The USDCNY year-on-year lead suggests China's import growth will continue to fall over the next 9 months. 

The middle right chart displays the year-on-year change of the Baltic Dry Index against the 18-month change of coal, gas, and oil prices (so comparing December 2024 prices to June 2023 prices). When energy prices have cheapened compared to the previous 18 months, it usually boosts economic growth and in turn leads to a positive year-on-year change in the Baltic Dry Index. The converse is also true. The 18-month change in energy prices usually leads by about 18 months, and the suggestion going forward is that the economic cycle will peak in the middle of 2025. 

The bottom left chart shows China's dry bulk import growth against OECDs index of cyclical changes in economic growth for the G-20 countries in aggregate. China's bulk commodity import growth is shifted 1 year forward in order to display that it leads the economic cycle. This year's falling import growth suggests the economic cycle could weaken through next year. 

The bottom right chart shows China's daily imported iron ore consumption. Average consumption has been 2.81 million tons per day in 2024, against 2.91 million tons per day in 2023. This is a drop of 3.2% year-on-year. 

All the above considered, it is very hard to be anything else than bearish for market prospects in 2025.
