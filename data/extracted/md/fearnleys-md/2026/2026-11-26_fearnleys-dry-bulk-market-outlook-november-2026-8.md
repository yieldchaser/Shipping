### SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK

**Overall outlook**

We expect 2027 to be a weaker year, with average earnings somewhat below the 2024–2026 average. This is a cyclical bearish call, not a structural one. Over 2027–2029 as a whole, we expect average earnings broadly in line with 2024–2026. A recovery looks possible during 2028, and 2H 2028 and 2029 stand out as the most likely next bullish period. We expect newbuilding prices to stay stable to firm, and we do not expect a major drawdown in second-hand values.

**Macro environment**

Over the last five years, global GDP and industrial production have each grown by about 3% a year. Industrial production growth was about 5% in emerging economies and 1.5% in developed economies. Over the same period:

•	Dry bulk shipment volumes grew by about 3% a year.
•	Ton-time demand grew by 4.8% a year.
•	The fleet grew by 3.1% a year.

We estimate fleet growth at around 3.8% a year over the next three years. It is likely to peak in 2027 at around 4–4.5%, which adds supply pressure just as demand is slowing.

The OECD expects global GDP growth to stay around 3%, which gives dry bulk demand a reasonable growth base. Export growth will come mainly from the Atlantic basin while demand stays in the East, so ton-time growth should keep outpacing GDP and industrial production.

Next year’s macro headwinds remain:

•	**Higher interest rates:** fewer central banks are cutting, more are hiking, and bond yields are higher.
•	**Higher oil prices:** oil price changes have historically led BDI year-on-year changes by about 12 months. The higher prices this year thus suggest a lower BDI in 2027

**China** is the main upside risk, but perhaps not before 2028. Its OECD growth indicator and credit growth are both very weak, but conditions were similar in late 2022, about nine months before the dry bulk upcycle that started in Q4 2023.

Disruptions at Hormuz, Suez, the Black Sea and Panama, relatively slower steaming, along with congestion in West Africa, have added to ton-days. How long that support lasts is a key uncertainty, as it depends on geopolitical developments.

**Iron ore**

The near-term iron ore outlook remains weak:

•	Chinese iron ore consumption is below last year's level.
•	Port and mill inventories have averaged 265 Mt in 2026, up 9.5% year on year.
•	Steel mill profitability is close to record lows.

Global iron ore shipment growth (six-month moving average) has fallen from a peak in Q1 to −1.5% in October. The main causes are the end of Chinese restocking, lower Chinese consumption and weak steel markets outside China. Still, there are some positives to point to. Iron ore prices could be in a bottoming process, and South Atlantic iron ore loadings are up 7.6% year to date, with Simandou shipments increasing faster than expected. Inventories of Brazilian iron ore in China remain quite low. To turn bullish again on seaborne volume growth, we need to see three things: stronger Chinese credit growth, falling inventories and better mill margins. We expect these to emerge in 2H 2027, which sets up a more positive 2028.

**Bauxite**

Bauxite remains a strong long-term growth story, but the near-term market looks oversupplied. As iron ore weakened, bauxite has carried the Capesize market through the second half of this year:

•	Guinea loadings are up 30.8% year to date and 71% over the last four weeks.
•	Bauxite accounts for about 73% of the increase in South Atlantic iron ore and bauxite volumes this year.

Chinese imports, however, have run well ahead of underlying aluminium demand:

•	Imports could end up around 240 million tons this year
•	China’s domestic bauxite production is likely to end up around 57 million tons this year.
•	The estimated bauxite requirement in 2026 is 245 Mt.

Bauxite inventories in China are around 92 days of consumption. This leaves little need for further strong import growth. We therefore expect Bauxite growth to be lower in 2027 than this year, as we expect some inventory drawing.  We are more constructive for 2028.

**Coal**

Global coal trade is up only slightly this year, with all the added volumes being shipped on Panamaxes/Kamsarmaxes.

Still, the trade mix has shifted toward longer hauls, so average coal voyage durations are up by 3.5% this year compared to 2025:

•	Exporters: Indonesia is down 7.5%, Australia is up 2.9%, and the Atlantic long-haul suppliers are growing strongly. Russia is up 8.8%, the US 11.9%, South Africa 10.6% and Colombia 10.9%.
•	Importers: China is down 4.6%, and India is down 9.2%. Southeast Asia is up 6.4%, Japan/Korea/Taiwan is up 3.9%, and Europe is down 1.6%.

The five-year coal shipment cycle and the price cycle both point to rising prices and volumes for another 1.5–2 years. Coal is the one commodity group where we are bullish for both 2027 and 2028.

**Grains**

The IGC expects grain trade to weaken in 2026/27:

•	Total grains are forecast at 450 Mt, down from 467 Mt in 2025/26 (−3.6%).
•	Including soybeans, trade is forecast at 640 Mt (−2.1%).
•	Wheat is expected to fall 7%, partly offset by maize and soybeans.

2026 has been strong so far, with grain loadings (25–100k dwt) up 6.4% in January–October and heading for a record year. ECSAM loadings are at their highest level since at least 2013.

Momentum has turned, though. Discharge growth (three-month average) was −5.7% globally in October. China was at −4.0% and the world ex-China at −6.4%, the weakest reading since November 2020.

Higher grain prices are the main drag on imports outside China. Our grain price index is up from 102 to 137 year on year. The 4.5-year price cycle and China's 2.75-year import cycle both point to weak trade in 2027 and a recovery in 2028.

**Minor bulks**

Minor bulk growth has slowed significantly, with 12-month rolling growth at around 1.8%. So far this has not hurt the Ultramax and Handysize markets, mainly because of higher average voyage durations:

•	Supramax/Ultramax volumes are up only 2.4% in January–October.
•	Tonne-days are up 7.7% over the same period.
•	Average voyage duration is at a record 38.6 days.
•	Far East exports are up 15.1%, and Pacific-to-Atlantic flows are up 16.4%.

Volume weakness is likely to catch up with these segments eventually. With interest rates and energy costs high, we see little reason to expect a strong rebound in minor bulk volumes in 2027.

**Capesize / Newcastlemax**

Demand growth has averaged around 4% a year over the last decade. South Atlantic volumes are up 16.8% year to date and 26% over the last four weeks, which should support the market into year-end. Beyond that, the balance gets tougher.

The delivery schedule implies fleet growth of between 5% and 6% in both 2027 and 2028. Combined with a weaker demand outlook, we expect earnings to stay below the 2026 average through 2027 and most of 2028.

There is upside potential in 2028, however. Deliveries could slip into 2029, and demand growth could move back toward 8%.

The longer-term picture is much stronger. Yard constraints limit fleet growth to about 2% a year in 2029 and 2030. Combined with potentially stronger demand, this makes the setup much more bullish from 2H 2028 into 2029 and beyond.

**Kamsarmax / Ultramax / Handysize**

Demand growth has averaged about 5% a year over the last decade, swinging between about 10% at cycle peaks and about 0% at troughs. It has fallen sharply in recent months, and we expect that to continue.

Combined fleet growth is estimated at around 4% in 2027, falling to 3% in 2028 and currently 1.8% in 2029. Supply is tightest in Handysize, where we see net fleet growth of about 1.6% in 2027 and close to zero in 2028. Panamax/Kamsarmax growth is higher at about 3.6% in 2027, with an orderbook of about 13% of the fleet.

We expect lower average earnings in 2027 and are more constructive on 2028–2029, provided orderbooks for those years do not grow too much.

## PERIOD RATES AND ASSET VALUES

Period rates have fallen over the last two months. Every segment peaked in the week of 23 September and has eased since. This time Panamax and Kamsarmax have fallen furthest, slightly more than Capesize and Newcastlemax. Geared rates are only modestly lower, and Handysize is unchanged.

**Capesize / Newcastlemax.** Over the last two months, our Capesize 1-year TC assessment fell from $36,600 to $31,900 (-13%), and our Newcastlemax assessment from $49,700 to $43,900 (-12%). Both peaked on 23 September ($37,100 and $50,400) and are now about 13–14% lower. Rates are also below where they were in mid-August.  Second-hand values have kept rising even as rates eased. Since mid-September, 5-year-old Japanese-built Capes are up $4.5m to $77.5m and Chinese-built up $2.5m to $72.5m. Newcastlemax newbuilding prices are unchanged at $80m.

**Panamax / Kamsarmax.** Over the last two months, Kamsarmax 1-year TC fell from $22,750 to $19,600 (-14%), and Panamax from $20,250 to $17,200 (-15%). Both peaked on 23 September ($23,250 and $20,750) and are now about 16–17% lower. Firm fixtures are thin. Owners' ideas for 1 year range from $19,000 to $20,500, and 5–7 months is at $21,000–22,500, above 1 year. Second-hand values are broadly stable. 5-year-old Kamsarmaxes rose by $0.5–1m in early October and have been flat since, and newbuilding prices are up $0.5m to $39m.

**Ultramax / Supramax / Handysize.** Over the last two months, Ultramax 1-year TC fell from $20,000 to $19,000 (-5%) and Supramax from $18,000 to $16,750 (-7%). Handysize is unchanged at $15,500. Ultramax and Supramax are about 10% below their 23 September peaks, and Handysize about 3% below. Second-hand values have been flat since September. Ultramax newbuilding prices are up $0.5m to $36.5m.

### Asset Values vs Commodity Prices

The recent sharp drop in industrial metals prices suggests second-hand values will correct in Q1 next year. The correlation between the price changes of copper and the price changes of second-hand values is at times very close. Should the series track each other closely over the next months, 10-year old second-hand values will correct by around 20% from current levels.

## MACRO ECONOMIC OUTLOOK

This page gives brief comments on what the charts on the next page tell us.  Shipment volume growth has fallen considerably over the last months, which is in accordance with what we expected earlier this year given how the macro indicators looked. Macro indicators continue to suggest weakness going forward

Top left chart:

China's credit growth remains weak, which suggests the BDI year-on-year change will continue trending down over the next months. There are increasing rumours about a new round of stimulus measures being launched. The total social financing figures in January next year will be a key data point in this regard, as January is the month of the year when the most credit is issued.

Top right chart:

Except for a decoupling in 2024, the change in bond yields in China and the US has been a good leading indicator for dry bulk demand growth. This year, it has so far called the demand growth development perfectly, as growth has tumbled sharply following the peak in Q2.

Middle left chart:

Lower crude oil prices are bullish for economic growth, and vice versa. The chart displays that since 2017, there has been a tight correlation between lower year-on-year oil prices and a higher year-on-year BDI, with the oil price change leading by 1 year.

The low oil prices of last year have suggested that the BDI would remain positive year-on-year throughout 2026, which has been the case. However, the increase in oil prices since late February suggest the market will be lower for most of 2027.

Middle right chart:

The OECD diffusion index momentum keeps falling, and suggest the year on year change of the BDI will turn less and less positive over the coming six months.

Bottom left chart:

The OECD diffusion index correlation with the BDI is very high, as usual. The OECD index reading is comparable to previous cycle peaks. The index seems to be rolling over, as the reading has fallen from 18 between January and April to 13 in October.

Bottom right chart:

Shipment volume growth has, as usual, tracked the development of the US dollar index (a very good proxy for global liquidity conditions). The dollar has strengthened over the last months, which suggest no imminent turnaround in the weak shipment volume growth rates seen in the last 4-5 months.
