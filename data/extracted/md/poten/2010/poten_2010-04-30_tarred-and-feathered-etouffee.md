# Tarred and Feathered Etouffee

The oil spill which began with an explosion on an oil rig in the U.S. Gulf of Mexico has developed into a rapidly‐deteriorating situation threatening local wildlife and national commerce and is also likely to have longer‐term implications for recently‐announced plans to increase offshore drilling. Unlike past spills of contained volumes, the continuing flow from the site could take up to 90 days to quell, and adds additional pressure and uncertainty to the situation. The spill has slowed traffic on critical trade routes for petroleum, among other goods, and raised a number of logistical issues likely to have wide‐ranging consequences for tanker markets in the weeks to come.

An April 20th explosion on the Deepwater Horizon oil rig in the U.S. Gulf killed 11 workers and caused 5,000 barrels per day of crude oil to flow from a well 5,000 feet below sea level into surrounding waters, as shown in the map below. A massive collaboration among the Coast Guard, National Guard, and area volunteers is underway to contain the effects of the spill, which one specialist has labeled the worst offshore platform incident in 40 years. Increasing southeasterly winds forecast through Saturday night are expected to push the oil slick near the coasts of Mississippi tomorrow, possibly threatening Alabama and Florida in the days to follow.

### Location, Location, Location

The volumes of crude oil gushing from the well have inspired widespread comparisons to the 1989 Exxon Valdez oil spill in Alaska, but its location dictates that this spill will have a more significant impact on the spot tanker market. The map below shows the location and distribution of other oil spills that shaped regulation and reputations in oil and tanker industries. Unlike other memorable oil spills, the long‐term implications of the most recent spill are likely to be felt higher up in the supply chain.

### Historical Oil Spills

While the Exxon Valdez spill complicated operations at just one distant port, the Deepwater Horizon spill is occurring in a heavily‐trafficked area responsible for about 30 percent of US domestic oil production. Ships have been instructed to avoid contact with the heavy black floating oil at all costs and maintain a safe speed while moving through lighter oil on the surface when forced to traverse affected areas. Both owners and charterers are starting to make attempts to further protect themselves through additional clauses or wording while the United States Coast Guard works with area port authorities to formulate plans for decontamination.

As the spill threatens to possibly disrupt operations at several important oil ports along the coasts of Alabama and Mississippi, there is a great deal of uncertainty surrounding how the market will respond to what is still a very fluid situation. The spill is not expected to impact operations at the Louisiana Offshore Oil Platform (LOOP), the landing site for more than 1 million barrels per day of crude oil imports and officials at Capline's St. James crude oil terminal are reportedly assessing the situation and planning to issue a statement in coming days. The Southwest Pass connecting the Mississippi River with the US Gulf is expected remain outside of the slick for at least the next 72 hours, according to the most recent trajectories. Barge activity on the inter‐coastal waterway has slowed while owners wait to see how the situation develops.

Delayed and diverted voyages will undoubtedly be a factor in determining tanker freight rates in the region and beyond as attempts to stop the spill continue to develop. Voyages will be extended as vessels are forced to go around the spill, with freight rates likely to rise while owners and charterers haggle over who is responsible for extra costs. Weather‐related delays late in the week increased demand of vessels for lightering to the Gulf. These vessels were seized upon by lightering companies, partially in anticipation of spill‐related complications, helping boost freight rates for Aframaxes on the Caribbean to US Gulf voyage.

The disruption of normal trading patterns in the U.S. Gulf is likely to also have other, more unpredictable consequences for both oil and tanker markets over the medium‐term. If prompt supply of distillates in the area is affected significantly, it could create attractive arbitrage opportunities. Additionally, lowered volumes of crude oil being delivered to area refineries could contribute to a drawdown in considerable inventories.

One thing is for certain, incidents such as this do nothing for the plight of oil companies trying to defend safe practices and respect the public domain. If history has taught us anything, it is that the public has a photographic memory when it comes to oil spills. Expect to hear of this event for decades to come.
