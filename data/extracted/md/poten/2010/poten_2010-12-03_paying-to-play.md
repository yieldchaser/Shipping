# Paying To Play

While the relative strengths of the leading world economies will be the ultimate driver of sustained trade growth, it is the relative strength of currencies which are being discussed as potential drivers of the tanker asset market in the near term. Debate surrounding currency pressures have become a topic of increasing significance between the United States and China. Shipbuilding in particular finds itself at the forefront of this entanglement in countries like South Korea, where businesses must regularly manage the inflow of foreign currency with the domestic outflow.

In the United States, the Federal Reserve Bank has set in sight objectives similar to the Chinese by fostering a low interest rate environment and seeking to increase the monetary supply through quantitative easing. The resultant weak dollar environment sets the stage for inflationary pressure in the near term. With interest rates at historically low levels, pragmatic investors would have much incentive to seek out capitally intensive projects to the extent that they can source financing. This is one explanation for the resurgence of newbuilding activity seen in the tanker market over the course of 2010, and it is has been further suggested that forthcoming inflation will contribute to investors' gains by allowing them to secure assets at lower nominal values today.

While on the surface, inflationary pressure could extend the value of the shipowners dollar, there is a big 'if' associated with this logic. Over the past two years, investors have become sorely familiar with the notion that prices do not always increase, but staying cognizant of this reality is easier said than done.

The challenge with assets such as ships is that supply and demand fundamentals will be far stronger determinants of the overall return on investment over time. At some point in the future, the new capacity will deliver into the market, expanding supply and adding downward pressure to freight rates and prices overall in the absence of material gains in demand.

### What's in a dollar?

It is likely that the shipyards themselves will bear the brunt of this currency risk, seeking to balance getting paid in dollars and running their business in local currency. Since January 2008, the Korean won has appreciated roughly 20% versus the US dollar making the cost of services and goods in Korea that much more costly. The effects in China have been more muted as to‐ date as the yuan is still linked to the US dollar.

JPY KRW EUR CNY While the idea of holding cash under today's economic conditions may seem less than appealing, sinking money into newbuilding assets may be just as risky as ever. Shipowners are cautioned to look at the long‐term repercussions of adding further padding to the existing orderbook. While there is still of course reason to believe in tanker demand recovery, each incremental order placed will chip away at the long‐term earnings potential over time.

Some shipowners will always look towards shipyards for fleet renewal and modernization simply due to the lack of substantial fleet or en bloc transactions in the secondhand market for quality tonnage at competitive prices. However, it is the illiquidity in the secondhand market that can contribute to an oversupply of ships and potential further disconnect between asset prices and realistically achievable freight rates.
