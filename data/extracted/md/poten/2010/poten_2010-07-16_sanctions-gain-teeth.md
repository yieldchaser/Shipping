# Sanctions Gain Teeth

With the enactment of a new U.S. sanctions regime earlier this month, the tanker industry has once again found itself drawn directly into the fray surrounding Iran. On July 1st, President Obama signed the Comprehensive Iran Sanctions, Accountability, and Divestment Act of 2010, imposing further restrictions on business dealings with Iran. These unilateral sanctions were designed to reinforce a new set of UN sanctions passed in June by attempting to further limit Iran's critical need for refined product imports, highlighted in the chart below. While companies actively engaged in such trades will likely have long suspected such a tightening was forthcoming, the wide‐ranging nature and stiff penalties contained within the recently‐passed law warrant close attention.

The new law reinforces and expands on the 1996 Iran Sanctions Act in ways that bring petroleum transportation and the tanker industry into a sharper focus. Under the new law, oil and liquefied natural gas tankers have been added explicitly to the definition of "petroleum and natural gas resources", investment in and provision of which is prohibited. The new law requires the president to impose sanctions on those that "sell, lease or provide goods, services, technology, information or support that could directly and significantly enhance Iran's ability to import refined products." The new sanctions apply when the fair market value exceeds $1 million for a single transaction or $5 million annually. Critically, the law extends its prohibitions to include shipping services, including insurance, financing and brokerage.

### Targeting Tankers

An exception for underwriters and insurance providers who have practiced due diligence in establishing procedures to comply with the prohibition was included in the law. Ongoing efforts to isolate Tehran are, nonetheless, having an effect on the ability to secure insurance for refined product imports into Iran. Whether or not companies have interest in pursuing relationships with Iran, practical hurdles, such as securing insurance, are have a limiting effect.

• In March, the Joint War Committee of war risk underwriters in London declared Iran and its ports an "excluded area", requiring vessel owners to obtain permission before entering the area • Lloyd's of London are reported to have said in a recent statement that it would no longer issue insurance for refined product shipments into Iran • Intertanko and Bimco have developed charterparty clauses that allow for the termination of a charter which might force an owner into non‐compliance Companies found to be in violation of the new U.S. sanctions will face an extended "menu" of nine possible punishments from which the president is now required to impose at least three (whereas the previous law required only two). The possible penalties now include measures designed to bar any sanctioned party from doing business with any entity required to comply with U.S. law. Another possible sanction added in the new bill would prohibit any credit transfers or payment through a U.S. financial institution. By threatening to force a decision between doing business in the United States and doing business in Iran, these provisions are designed to influence the behavior of international companies unaffected by previous U.S. sanctions efforts.

The applicability of the new sanctions may rest on the knowledge standard for the law, which defines those having "knowingly" violated the sanctions as those who have "actual knowledge" of violations, including people who "should have known". The introduction of this language into the law is designed to ensure that parent companies are held liable for the actions of any subsidiaries engaged in refined product trades to Iran. What actual requirements the "should have known" standard places on ship owners to inquire about trading activities of vessels chartered out to third parties remains to be seen.

### Trade Flows Frozen?

A Government Accountability Office report earlier this year named companies actively involved in trading gasoline into Iran and subsequent pressure on these companies forced most to relent and end their supply contracts. Throughout 2010, they have been joined by a procession of companies including Eni, Total, and Reliance which have announced that they would end business dealings with Iran. This pressure is only starting to be reflected in the volume of reported fixtures to carry refined products into Iran, which is on pace to surpass the volume of clean fixtures reported last year, as shown in the chart below. There have been no reported fixtures into Iran since the new sanctions came into effect at the beginning of July, likely a reflection of the hesitancy of some ship owners in the area to take their vessels into the country.

Negotiations to reconcile different versions of the sanctions bill reportedly focused on the need for flexibility in light of the effort to gather support for United Nations sanctions against Iran. To help provide this flexibility, the law allows for a one‐year waiver for companies found in violation that are closely cooperating with multilateral efforts against Iran. This new waiver is renewable and in addition to the existing authority of the president to grant six month waivers. The new law also contains a provision making investigation into credible allegations of sanctionable refined product trades mandatory. However, that portion of the law does not come into effect until one year after its July 1st enactment. This suggests that while the sanctions do aim to leverage the shortcomings of Iran's petroleum sector in the effort to discourage its alleged pursuit of nuclear weapons, they also reflect some sensitivity to the market impact of unilateral sanctions.

There are a number of uncertainties surrounding the new Iran sanctions and the implications for tanker demand will likely evolve in the months to come. Considering that even foreign‐flagged shipping companies are likely to eventually conduct some business involving American entities, the most severe repercussions possible under the bill could be significant. In light of this reality, it is vitally important for international shipping companies to carefully monitor their operations to ensure that they do not knowingly run afoul of the new law.
