# Ho Ho Ho And A Bottle O' Rum!

As December winds down and the focus of ship owners and charterers shifts towards the coming year's various projected outlooks and forecasts, several common themes will again catch the attention of all shipping market participants. Chief amongst these for owners of vessels operating in the vicinity of the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden is the evolving threat of piracy.

A recent report released by Lloyd's Joint War Committee notes that since the upsurge in piracy‐ related activities in 2008, the threat of piracy has evolved just as materially as the practices and strategies implemented to prevent it. Notably, the committee addresses the issue of the increased effective range of Somali‐based piracy utilizing previously‐captured vessels as 'mother ships' from which the attacks originate. The course of action taken by the committee was to expand the existing areas it deems commercial vessels to be 'at risk' of attack as 2010 saw numerous attacks occur outside the previously designated risk area.

The Joint War Committee represents the interests of those writing war risks in the London markets. Its decision to expand the stretch of waterways deemed at elevated risk of piracy threat will mean higher operating costs for owners as they are faced with the threat of attack further out at sea. Specifically, ships transiting the Western Indian Ocean will be faced with premium piracy 'riders' to reflect the risk they undertake transiting the area. A quick overview of the increasing frequency of the attacks (in addition to their being staged further offshore) makes it easy to understand why insurers will be charging more to absorb the greater cost of mounting insurance claims.

Incidents Hijackings With the scope of attacks expanding to as far south as Tanzania and Madagascar and as far east as the Maldives, the industry is still searching for answers to this escalating problem. The evolutionary nature of the piracy threat (which now includes funding from an unofficial pirate stock exchange established in Harardhere) has led some ship owners and market participants to consider tactics previously regarded as draconian in nature to prevent further attacks. Recently, a spokesman for the prominent U.K.‐based seafarer's union Nautilus International went so far as to state they were no longer opposed to the use of armed guards on ships. The stance represents a significant shift in sentiment towards armed self‐defense shared by others in the industry but still discouraged by major marine insurance underwriters and industry pundits.

### Pirates Return to Earth

Existing efforts to thwart the growing piracy problem have been met with mixed results. Multinational task forces assembled in the region operating as part of the Combined Maritime Forces under the US 5th Fleet have had limited success in dealing with the growing threat. The geographic area over which the attacks are occurring seems to have grown too broad in size for international counter‐piracy forces to constantly monitor with any degree of great effectiveness. Somalia has been mulling the idea of hiring a private military security contractor to train and deploy a 1,000 man antipiracy force in the capital of Mogadishu. The force would be funded by an unidentified country according to media reports, and spearheaded by the security company Saracen International. Saracen's operations would seek to eliminate pirate threats "on land" through the establishment of a trained security force led in part by a former US Ambassador and CIA officials. The establishment of private security forces draws concerns from all corners of the international community, however, particularly when funding for such projects is being supplied by unidentified countries - even if it is for sake of avoiding terrorist retribution as claimed.

The growing piracy threat, on both a numeric and geographic basis, is testing the limits of the current preventative measures taken by the international community. Decisions such as those noted above by the Joint War Committee will result in higher costs to ship owners and ultimately consumers putting pressure on end users and ship owners alike to find solutions. Should the attacks continue to occur and expand with the impunity and scope witnessed in the past several years, it can be concluded that attitudes toward arming merchant ships may continue to evolve as well.
