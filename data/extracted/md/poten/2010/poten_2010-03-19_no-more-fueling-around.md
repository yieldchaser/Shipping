# No More Fueling Around

When the Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC) of the International Maritime Organization (IMO) discusses options for regulating marine emissions of greenhouse gases next week, it will make its case that a single standard is preferable to a patchwork of varying fuel requirements. Whatever plan the IMO eventually produces will join an already‐crowded regime of separate marine fuel regulation that has started to become a more prominent feature in the day‐to‐day tanker market. As if on cue, recent developments in the Mediterranean Aframax market have provided IMO delegates with a clear example of how unevenly enforced marine fuel regulation can have a significant impact on shipping markets.

At the beginning of March, a decision by the harbor master in Trieste, Italy to strictly enforce the European Union's new requirement to burn 0.1% sulfur marine diesel while in port prompted owners who knew their vessels were not in compliance to avoid the port. These ships chased out of the region by threat of hefty fines decreased ship supply and helped lead to the spike in Aframax freight rates seen in the Mediterranean, shown in the chart below. After falling more than 80 percent over the first two months of 2010, earnings on the benchmark Sidi Kerir - Lavera Aframax voyage have risen dramatically since the beginning of March, reaching current time charter equivalent earnings of more than $36,000 per day.

### The New Rules

Since January 1st 2010, vessels docked in European Union ports for more than two hours while not hooked into shore‐side power have been required to use marine gasoil with no more than 0.1% sulfur to power auxiliary equipment. This requirement is the result of an EU directive published back in 2005, one of several marine fuel regulations to come into force in recent years, summarized in the table below.

Because there is no central authority with responsibility for enforcing these new regulations, European Union member states have discretion as to how to penalize those found to be violating them. Responding to widespread concerns about possible technical and operational safety issues stemming from the new regulation, the European Commission issued a recommendation late last year on how to implement it. The EC told member states that they could consider ship owners' future plans to make necessary adjustments in deciding how severely to punish owners violating the new rule and suggested that such retrofits should be completed by August 2010.

The harbor master of the Italian port of Trieste announced on March 3rd that it would no longer issue at berth exemptions and those previously granted would no longer be honored at the port. Fines of between €15,000 for the first offense and €150,000 for the third prompted Intertanko, the independent tanker owners' organization, to blast the move as a money‐making scheme that put ship and port safety at risk. As the site of the beginning of the Trans Alpine Pipeline which transports crude oil into Germany, Trieste is an important port for Aframaxes and the removal of non‐compliant vessels from the region helped support a rally in freight rates (weather‐related delays in the area were also a factor).

Ship owners able to meet the EU's fuel restrictions have commanded a premium on voyages to the port and despite initial worries that others would follow suit and begin enforcing the stricter rules, none have come forward to do so thus far. Some owners in the region have started restricting their vessels from traveling to Trieste and at least one stateside owner has already been forced to decline a trading opportunity when they were unable to find available 0.1% sulfur fuel.

### ...More to Come?

The EU's new restrictions on marine fuel burned in port and their effect on spot market rates demonstrate that inconsistent enforcement of regulations can have a significant effect on the bottom line. The rules now being more strictly enforced on a selective basis have been known since 2005, allowing plenty of time for ship owners to meet the new regulations. However, the lack of an effective policing and enforcement methodology led many to believe these regulations would not materialize on a practical basis.

As the debate regarding regulation of greenhouse gas emissions from ships reaches the next stage with the meeting of the MEPC next week, lessons learned from existing regulation are important to keep in mind. Considering the repercussions of the sulfur rules for which the market has had five years' warning, the IMO must carefully consider realistic timelines for its sweeping proposals to limit a wider range of harmful greenhouse gases. It must also clearly mandate uniform enforcement and suitable penalties for non‐compliance.

Without a realistic framework, the efforts of these agencies will likely be in vain. Until such a framework is agreed to and put into force, expect the volatility to continue as pockets of availability for specific fuels may create more complications in an already complex market.
