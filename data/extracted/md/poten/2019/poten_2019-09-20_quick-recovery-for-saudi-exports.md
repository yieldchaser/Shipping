# Quick Recovery For Saudi Exports

### Initial impact on tanker rates is mixed

### Quick Recovery For Saudi Oil Exports?

On September 14, 2019, the Abqaiq crude oil processing plant, as well as facilities related to the Khurais oilfield were attacked with drones and missiles. These facilities processed about 5.7 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) of crude oil to make it suitable for transportation and refining. The total interruption in production represents about 60% of the Saudi crude oil exports and more than 5% of global oil demand. Yemeni Houthi rebels have claimed responsibility, however, Saudi Arabia and the U.S. have stated that Iran was probably involved. The outage primarily affected the production of Arab Light and Arab Extra Light, both light to medium heavy crudes with relatively high sulfur content.

Initial reports indicated that the outage could take weeks or months to resolve, but more recently Saudi officials indicated that production would be back to normal within two to three weeks. It is uncertain whether this statement is intended to calm the markets or if this is a realistic assessment. Oil prices initially jumped about 20% but gradually declined again and are now trading about $4 (7%) above Friday's closing price. The market realizes that geopolitical risk has increased, and the forward curve for WTI remains about $1.50 per barrel above pre-attack levels.

Saudi Arabia has resumed the loading of tankers, with crude coming mainly out of inventories. Some of the cargoes that were scheduled for the coming weeks have been delayed by one to two weeks, and some of the buyers of Arab Light or Extra Light crude grades will receive heavier grades instead, if the refinery configuration gives them the ability to utilize such grades. Saudi Arabia has reportedly cut crude supplies to its own refineries, and the country has also started buying petroleum products to compensate for lost production. It is generally believed that the country can supply customers out of inventories initially, but it will be interesting to see what the situation is in a couple of weeks, if the facilities are still running at significantly reduced capacity.

More than 70% of total seaborne exports from Saudi Arabia have been shipped to Asia in 2019, as this is where demand is growing. Furthermore, the Atlantic already has a surplus of crude. However, the Saudi's are also focusing on Asia to drive down inventories in the Western hemisphere (where inventory levels are widely reported and monitored by traders).

Tanker rates (in particular for large crude oil tankers) reacted quickly to the events in Saudi Arabia, although much of the freight rate increase was caused by higher bunker prices. VLCC rates from the Arabian Gulf to the Far East increased by 9 WS-points on Monday (TCE from $27,000/day to $35,000/day) and continued to increase gradually to WS70 (TCE: $40,700/day) today.

On the other hand, large product tanker rates have weakened on the news, especially for the naphtha trade out of the Arabian Gulf. TCE's for LR2's on the AG to the Far East route declined from $16,000 before the attack to $10,000/day mid-week, before recovering slightly to $12,900 today.

Prior to the attack, tanker rates were already anticipated to increase due to normal seasonality as refineries prepare for the winter market. This year, the upcoming implementation of IMO 2020 will create extra pressure on rates as some tankers will temporarily be out of service to install scrubbers. Many ships will also need to switch to more expensive low sulphur fuels ahead of the January 1st deadline and freight rates will likely start to reflect these higher bunker costs before the end of the year.

Saudi Arabia was the only country with significant spare production capacity in the market to compensate for unexpected outages or disruptions. Even if Saudi production quickly returns to pre-attack levels, it will take longer for the spare capacity cushion to be re-established. If any unexpected interruptions happen elsewhere in the world (i.e. the U.S. hurricane season is still active, as the Houston area was reminded this week), it will be much harder to replace lost barrels. As a result, the oil and tanker markets are facing significantly higher upside risk than one week ago.
