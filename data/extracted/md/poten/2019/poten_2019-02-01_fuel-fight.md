# Fuel Fight

### Transatlantic product flows keep changing

For many years the product flows in the Atlantic Basin were fairly predictable. The United States produced surplus diesel and exported this to Europe, which was short diesel. European refiners on the other hand generated excess gasoline which could be sold to buyers on the U.S. East Coast. This provided triangulation opportunities for product tanker owners that operated vessels in the Atlantic Basin. For example, they would pick up a diesel cargo in the U.S. Gulf and discharge it in Northwest Europe. Ideally, the vessel would then pick up a gasoline cargo at a nearby terminal for delivery on the U.S. Atlantic Coast (USAC). So far in 2019, an MR voyage from Houston to Amsterdam (38,000 MT) would generate a Time Charter Equivalent (TCE) return of $7,200/day. Over the same period, the TCE for a voyage across the Atlantic in the other direction, from Rotterdam to New York has a TCE of $11,500/day. However, if an owner could combine these trips for a triangulation voyage (Houston - Amsterdam - Rotterdam - New York) the TCE for the owner increases to $17,000/day because of the significant improvement in the utilization rate of the vessel. On a typical ballast back voyage, the vessel is only utilized 50% of the time, while it is empty the other 50%. On a triangulation voyage as described above, the vessel is only empty 35% of the time, while sailing with a cargo 65% of the voyage. Increasing a vessel's utilization is one of the key levers that an owner has to increase profitability (or reduce losses). However, to make this triangulation work, it is critical that there is a certain balance in the trades going back and forth over the Atlantic. That is where problems are starting to develop.

In the early 2000's, there was a wide discrepancy between the two trades. There was a significant volume of gasoline moving from Europe to the U.S. while there was only a trickle of diesel moving in the other direction. From 2005 through 2013, this gap closed substantially as gasoline volumes fell while diesel movements increased. During the period from 2013-2016, the trades across the Atlantic were fairly balanced. However, the gap opened up again in 2017 and 2018. While the volumes of European gasoline arriving on the U.S. East Coast remained fairly stable, U.S. refiners shipped significantly less diesel to Europe. The primary reason for this was slowing demand for diesel cars in Europe. After "Dieselgate", Europeans started to buy less diesel-powered cars and more gasoline and electric vehicles. Dieselgate was the 2015 scandal that found that automakers were manipulating test data to hide the fact that certain diesel engines produced more emissions than was allowed. Partly because of the scandal, some European cities (Paris, for example) have announced intentions to ban diesel use altogether. However, the impact of these developments should not be overestimated. It takes time for new vehicle sales to have an impact on the total car fleet. New vehicles only represent a small proportion of the operating fleet and also tend to be more fuel efficient, further reducing their impact. It should also be noted that about 50% of total diesel demand in Europe is represented by the commercial sector and gasoline/electric engines are not (yet) a realistic alternative for large trucks and vans.

What is in store for the future? The expectation is that diesel demand in Europe will remain strong despite the headwinds facing passenger cars. One of the key demand drivers for middle distillates over the next few years will be IMO 2020. The Marpol regulations to reduce sulphur content in bunker fuels will be introduced in 2020. This will switch demand for bunker fuels from high sulphur fuel oil to low sulphur marine gasoil either as a direct product or blended to make 0.5% sulphur fuel oil.

It is difficult to directly translate the developments in the transatlantic product movements into changes in the tanker rates. Theoretically, a more balanced trade allows for more triangulation and higher earnings, but in practice these benefits are only temporary. The overall state of the tanker market and the global supply-demand balance of product tankers is ultimately more important. Tanker markets are fairly efficient and higher earnings in a certain geographical region will disappear quickly as more tonnage moves in.
