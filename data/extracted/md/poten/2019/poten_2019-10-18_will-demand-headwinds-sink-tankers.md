# Will Demand Headwinds Sink Tankers

### Oil demand for next year does not look promising

While tanker rates went through a record-breaking rollercoaster ride during the last two weeks, reports from the main agencies that forecast oil demand growth were notably less bullish. Last week, the International Energy Agency (IEA) cut its estimate for global oil demand growth in 2019 to 1.0 million barrels per day (mb/d), a reduction of 65,000 b/d. For 2020, they now expect 1.2 mb/d growth, about 100,000 b/d less than their previous forecast. The IEA cited a weakening outlook for the major global economies as the main reason for its outlook revision. The short-term outlook of the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) is also more bearish. The October 2019 issue shows lower growth for 2019 (-50,000 b/d) and 2020 (-100,000 b/d). OPEC has also slightly reduced its 2019 estimate, although it did not change the 2020 growth outlook in its latest report. To top it off, the IMF has also reduced its outlook for the world economy. It expects that growth this year will be the weakest since the 2008 global financial crisis. After 3.6% growth in 2018, this year's global expansion falls to 3.0%, with a slight rebound to 3.4% in 2020. These numbers are 0.2% (2019) and 0.1% (2020) lower than the previous World Economic Outlook Projections from July 2019. The subtitle of the IMF report says it all: "Global Manufacturing Downturn, Rising Trade Barriers". So, where does this leave the tanker market in 2020? Was the recent rate spike an aberration or do we expect increased volatility and higher rates to last into the next year?

While the latest oil demand projections are nothing to write home about, it only paints part of the picture. The oil supply situation looks more promising from a tanker owner's perspective. Rapid growth from non-OPEC producers in the Atlantic basin will drive tanker ton-miles. U.S. crude production and exports are expected to continue their rapid growth in 2020. While the pundits have different views on the exact volumes, they agree that growth will be significant. OPEC expects 1.5 mb/d more from North America, while the EIA sees expansion of 1.6 m/d. While some U.S. crude will end up in Europe, most of it will be exported long-haul to Asia.

Brazil is another growth story. Production in 2020 is expected to be 330,000 b/d higher than in 2019. ExxonMobil has discovered vast reserves in neighboring Guyana and production is expected to start in March 2020. Initially, production will be around 120,000 b/d, ramping up to 750,000 b/d in 2025. With an API gravity of 32.1 and 0.5% sulphur, the crude has similarities to Canada's Hibernia and Argentina's Mediante crude. Since it is a new stream, it is not yet clear, where this crude will be marketed. Last, but not least, Norway's has just started its giant Sverdrup field.

Traditionally, the bulk of North Sea crude is sweet and consumed locally, but since oil demand in Europe is stagnant, North Sea crude is increasingly finding homes in Asia.

Overall, non-OPEC output is forecasted to expand by 2.26 mb/d (IEA) in 2020, which is almost double global demand growth. OPEC probably wants to prevent oil stocks to increase dramatically because of the negative implications this has on oil prices. This means that OPEC producers will need to cut back further. The net impact of OPEC production cuts combined with non-OPEC expansion is growth in ton-mile demand for tankers.

On the tanker supply side, the risk of fleet growth is limited in the short term. The orderbook of all the major vessel classes has dropped below 10% of the existing fleet (for delivery between now and 2022). At this time last year, the orderbook for VLCCs stood at 15%. Obviously, if the market remains strong, owners will order more tankers, but these vessels will not deliver until 2021. Other factors that will impact fleet supply include scrapping, vessel speed and scrubber installations. Scrapping has been subdued this year and, as long as earnings remain strong, owners will try to keep older vessels employed. Scrubber installations will continue to play a role in reducing fleet supply throughout 2020. In the first half of next year, the new IMO 2020 bunker fuel regulations will also create uncertainty, dislocations and market inefficiencies. In summary, we expect a relatively strong tanker market in 2020 despite global oil demand headwinds.
