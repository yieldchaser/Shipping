# Just Go With The Flow

### How changes in product trades will impact product carriers

In last week's Tanker Opinion, we discussed how projected changes in crude oil balances are expected to impact the outlook for crude oil tankers. This week we focus on refined products and how changes on the downstream side of the business will impact the prospects for product tankers. The refined product side of the equation tends to be more complicated than the crude oil side and the outlook for product carriers over the next five years reflects this. While there are several positive trends, other factors create uncertainty.

One of the key drivers of crude oil demand in the past has been the relentless growth in refining capacity in Asia. The IEA expects this to continue for the next five years. In their report "Oil 2019" they forecast that two thirds of new refining capacity in the world, as well as two thirds of global refined products demand growth, will be in Asia. This, in itself, seems to indicate limited upside for global product trades, as the demand growth takes place in the same region where refining capacity is expanding. However, the situation is slightly more complicated.

The dominant country in the region is China. And it continues to grow rapidly. By 2024, China is expected to become the world leader in terms of installed refinery capacity, surpassing the United States (although the latter will remain the largest refiner because its facilities have higher utilization rates). China will add some 3.6 million barrels per day (mb/d) of capacity over the forecast period (40% of global expansion). Compared to the past, however, there are major changes. Some 42% of the growth comes from three large independent players that are building large petrochemical-oriented coastal refineries.

The move towards more coastal facilities has been going on for many years. Many of China's older refineries are located in the Northeast of the country, close to the major domestic oil fields. Newer facilities that are more dependent on crude oil imports are increasingly built on the coast. In their report, the IEA points out that all of China's new capacity will be located in the coastal provinces. As a result, China's Bohai Bay starts to resemble the U.S. Gulf Coast. A stretch of 1,300 kilometers (about 800 miles) houses 43% of China's refining capacity, but only 17% of its population. Similar to the U.S. Gulf, the infrastructure is therefore in place to facilitate both crude oil imports as well as product exports. China's refining additions are expected to far outpace its domestic demand growth and, as a result, product exports are projected to grow rapidly. Most of these exports are destined for other Asian countries and will likely benefit the Handysize/MR trades.

A lot of the gasoline imports into West Africa are sourced from the U.S. and Europe and typically arrive in larger LR1 or LR2 product tankers before being distributed along the coast in smaller product tankers. Since the Middle East/India is long diesel, they are the most likely provider of middle distillates to Africa.

U.S. refiners are expected to benefit from the global efforts to increase middle distillate yields to meet the new IMO bunker specs. In Europe, for example, refiners will see increased competition from exporters in the U.S., and the IEA expects refining utilization to fall from the current 87% to 80% in 2024.

The Middle East will continue to grow, adding 2.3 mb/d of capacity. Although the region will close some existing capacity and demand will also grow, a significant portion of the additional product will hit the export markets. Depending on the destination, these exports could benefit all product tanker segments, from small Handies to large LR2 's.
