# Yukon Trail‐Off

Since their discovery in 1968, Alaska's North Slope fields have produced 16.2 billion barrels of crude oil, and have been a vital source of the "black gold" fuelling the American economy. The Trans Alaskan Pipeline (TAPS) was built to carry these volumes from 250 miles north of the Arctic Circle, moving barrels 800 miles to ship out of the (relatively) warm water of Port of Valdez on Alaska's southern coast. Today, however, one of America's greatest feats of energy infrastructure may be facing obsolescence as a result of waning production from the American Arctic. As volumes through TAPS decrease, as does the pressure inside the pipeline, slowing the surge of oil, increasing transit time, and chilling the crude oil within to temperatures as low as 40 degrees. Some see TAPS tapping out, unless America warms up to the idea of domestic offshore drilling in Alaska's arctic waters.

Alaska ranks second only to Texas in state crude oil reserves, excluding Federal offshore fields. In 2010, total US production reached 5.51 mbd, the highest levels seen since 2003. Alaska's North Slope is still home to the single largest producing field in the United States, Prudhoe Bay, which is currently pumping out over 250 kbd of crude. However, the amount of oil moving through TAPS has been dropping approximately 6% per year, even though an estimated 10 billion barrels remain in Alaska's North Slope fields.

### What Would We Do for a Klondike Bar?

Adding more oil through the pipeline would solve TAPS's pressure problems, but pressure of a different sort has complicated this seemingly simple solution. Much of the remaining North Slope reserves lie in offshore fields in the Beaufort and Chukchi Seas, and the increased offshore drilling regulations following the Deepwater Horizon blowout have held up, and in some cases effectively blocked, plans for drilling in Alaska's northern arctic waters. Recovery of these barrels in the Alaskan Arctic is technically demanding, and some argue environmentally hazardous, given the sensitive nature of Alaska's arctic ecosystems. Regulators have had to balance Americans' desire for energy security, the government's need to produce revenue (85% of Alaska's state revenues are generated from oil taxes and royalties), and the concerns of environmentalists and federal officials. Ultimately, the decision of whether or not to drill, baby, drill, and keep the TAPS pipeline in commission will depend on which of these issues American voters hold most dear.

### I Can See Russia From Here!

In the meantime, decreased domestic production from Alaskan fields will likely require refiners along the Pacific coast to go to the global market for incremental crude oil supplies. As the world's crude oil sources diversify, West Coast refiners have been able to source foreign imports from fields closer to home, as seen in the chart below.

Last year, Arabian Gulf volumes accounted for only 36% of reported spot fixture volumes, reflecting a 9% decrease from 2007 levels. On the other hand, crude oil import volumes from the Caribbean Basin and East Coast of South and Central America grew 10% over the same time period, and volumes from the Asia Pacific region (including Southeast Asia and Australia) increased 5%.

So far this year, liftings from ports in the Far East have accounted for a remarkable 23% of reported spot fixture volumes to the US West Coast, the vast majority of which have been Aframax parcels of ESPO crude from Russia's Kozmino terminal. As Russia ramps up production and pumps more oil to her own Pacific coast for export, we are likely to see US West Coast refiners draw more barrels from our neighbors in the Far East.
