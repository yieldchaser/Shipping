# Crude Oil Bore‐a‐torium

In the year since the Deepwater Horizon blowout, conflicting and confusing messages have come from the White House regarding the direction of America's domestic energy industry. Amid political unrest abroad and the skyrocketing price of imported crude oil, the Administration has with one hand beckoned the domestic industry to narrow the gap between our production and consumption; with the other, it has signed into effect legislation making it prohibitive to do so. The US government reflexively put the kibosh on new deepwater drilling permits for the six months following the events of last April. While dozens of shallow water permits have been issued since, this week saw the Obama Administration issue its ninth Gulf deepwater drilling permit since the moratorium was lifted in October 2010. However Washington has failed to project one loud and clear message, much to the dismay of oil companies attempting to economically develop resources at home.

Despite the moratorium, 2010 was a record year for domestic crude oil production. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) domestic crude oil production increased over the two years from 2008 to 2010 to reach the highest level since 2003. By EIA's estimates, total domestic crude oil production increased by 150 kbd in 2010 to total 5.51 mbd. Oil production from Federal waters on the Outer Continental Shelf increased by more than a third since 2008. This offshore production rose from 446 million barrels in 2008 to more than 600 million barrels last year and accounted for approximately 30% of all US crude oil production in 2010.

Despite these gains, annual production declines of over 100 kbd are predicted for 2011 and 2012, driven primarily by maturation of oil fields in Alaska and a fall in production from the Federal Gulf of Mexico (GOM) over the next two years. ### Undiscovered Resources

Much of the recoverable reserves in the Gulf of Mexico lie in water depths greater than 1,000 feet, sometimes under subterranean salt layers. The tight international supply of the sophisticated, and expensive, equipment required for these projects adds one more hurdle for companies looking to develop offshore reserves. The current high crude oil price environment has made the regulatory hurdles Washington has instated somewhat tolerable, but as the abrupt collapse of prices seen in September 2008 shows, there is always the potential for this to change. If the high price environment is sustained, however, it is likely to continue spurring development of nonconventional sources of crude oil in the United States, lessening reliance on imports to meet demand.

While security of supply is a prominent consideration, the ability of the United States to actually bring proven reserves to market is likely to be pitted against the political moxy of the Obama Administration to develop reserves deep offshore. As long as American legislators continue to play lip service to domestic energy security while doing little to incentivize production, the companies with the know‐how and resources to develop these reserves will seek to do so elsewhere.
