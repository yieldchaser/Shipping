# OPEC Production: Staying the Course

One of the more closely watched meetings for tanker owners and charterers is the quarterly OPEC meeting. On Wednesday March 8th the OPEC producers met and have agreed to keep their official output ceiling on a steady course. The following chart shows OPEC actual production since 2000.

### OPEC Production

Venezuela UAE Saudi Arabia Qatar Nigeria Libya Kuwait Iraq Iran Indonesia Algeria The table on the next page compares the 2002 "low point" to average 2005 production. Since 2002, OPEC production has climbed 18% from 26.4 million barrels per day to 31.2 million bpd in 2005. This 4.8 million barrel a day increase has been a large part of the increase in overall tanker demand. Yet despite this increase in production, crude prices have risen from around $20 per barrel in 2002 to about $60 today. There are some good reasons for the increased price in addition to the obvious one of more oil demand.

### Not Every Country Is Up!

The first point is that not all OPEC members have higher production. Since 2002, Indonesia has suffered a significant decline. Iraqi production is down and Venezuela is just hanging at level production. The remaining OPEC nations have considerably expanded their output between 20 and 40% over the three-year period.

The following chart compares annual OPEC production and VLCC WS rates. The relationship between VLCC rates and OPEC production is evident, although not perfectly correlated.

### k BPD

The following table compares OPEC production with their quotas and capacities. The key point is that most OPEC nations today are producing close to or at their maximum capacity, which in some cases is either below or above their OPEC quotas. Total spare capacity is only about 1.5 million bpd, most of which is concentrated in one nation: Saudi Arabia.

### On the Razor's Edge

North Sea production is in a long-term decline with no fields coming on stream to restore production. Consumption in China, India, and the United States is still growing at a heated pace. New sources of oil, particularly out of the Caspian and Africa, will still require a few months before they are felt in the market. The world is still tense over oil production disruptions in Nigeria if civil unrest in the Niger delta is not brought under control. A nuclear confrontation with Iran has not been played out. Venezuela is leading the call for a production cutback with an expectation that seasonal demand will fall and crude prices with it. OPEC, in summary, has opted to "stay the course."

Under current circumstances, it is unlikely that oil prices and OPEC production will decline. There is limited OPEC spare capacity to increase production to make up for supply disruptions. Depending how newbuilding deliveries match up with incremental demand, there should be general support for VLCC rates over the coming months, at least until the next quarterly OPEC meeting in June.
