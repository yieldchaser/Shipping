# Venezuela: Heavy on Crude

Lately in the political arena, a great deal of discussion has surrounded the oil exporting country of Venezuela. Its President, Hugo Chavez, seems to be one of those figures whose words powerfully influence oil price volatility. In the past few weeks, industry and political experts have indicated that Venezuela may significantly reduce oil exports to the U.S. While much of this is still speculation, it is interesting to examine this hypothetical scenario with regards to the tanker market.

Total Reported Dirty Spot Liftings (Past 12 Months) Poten & Partners | new york • london • athens • perth • hong kong • guangzhou 1 The map on the previous page indicates the total number of reported dirty spot liftings out of Venezuela in the past twelve months. As it would be expected, the bulk of these cargoes stay in the Caribbean basin with the majority moving to the United States. The most noteworthy part of the story is that in the past twelve months an increased amount of Venezuelan exports moved East of Suez as compared to previous years.

### More Time On The Water

Currently, according to OPEC figures, Venezuela accounts for 12%, or 1.2 million barrels per day, of the United States' total imports. This equates to roughly 170,000 tons per day. The primary U.S. suppliers are Canada and Mexico contributing 17% each, Saudi Arabia adding 13% and Nigeria adding 11%. The remaining 30% come from other suppliers, such as Kuwait, Iraq and Angola.

Increasing trade from Venezuela to East of Suez countries will have a major impact on the tanker market. If Venezuela reduces the amount of crude exported to the United States, replacement imports will be necessary. The likely candidates to be called on will be Saudi Arabia, another AG exporter, or a producer in West Africa. Concurrently, Venezuela would be exporting to Asian replacement markets.

These two scenarios will drastically impact the tanker market by ton- mile increases, creating demand for more vessels. We assume all cargoes will be moving on VLCCs. For each scenario, the demand is calculated in terms of number of vessels per year required to cover increased ton-mile demand. These numbers are shown in the top of the tables. The lower figures denote the "savings" resulting from the eliminated activity. Changes on this order of magnitude would clearly tighten the market for VLCCs.

### Dream vs. Reality

According to the U.S. Department of Energy, U.S. imports from Venezuela are lower so far this year as compared to 2005 average levels. If this trend continues, it is certain that we will be on the path increasing demand for tonnage. The long-haul voyages will experience increased activity, while the short-haul voyages on mainly Aframaxes will undoubtedly feel a fall-out. While VLCC owners would dream of market conditions like this, charterers could face the reality of higher transportation costs and Aframax owners a potential oversupply of tonnage.
