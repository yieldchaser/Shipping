# Bottom of the Barrel

As the public continues to complain about high gasoline prices, there is an analogous sentiment heard among some ship owners and charterers regarding the cost of both light and heavy fuel for ships. It is logical that the price of products would increase with increases in the price of crude, but not as logical that one type of fuel would increase more than another. As gasoline demand continues to climb, and refiners continue to favor lighter distillates in order to capture the high margins, the supply of heavier products is likely to decline

For the majority of 2001 and 2002, the price premium for buying MDO stayed within a range of $40 - $80 per ton. By October of 2005 the spread reached its peak of $269 and the spread has been in the $220 to $240/ton range since, with the differential today close to the peak of around $260/ton.

### Can't or Won't

Looking at the price spread for MDO versus IFO, it would seem that refiners would have an incentive to produce more MDO. However, the sustained nature of the price premium indicates that refiners may not be able to produce as much MDO as the market demands.

### Price Strength - IFO:Crude

The graph above shows the NYMEX cash price of crude oil in $/metric ton versus the price ratios of both IFO and MDO to crude oil. The dotted trend lines indicate that while the price of MDO has strengthened with respect to crude oil, the relative price of IFO has decreased. With refineries, especially in the United States, geared to produce gasoline and diesel, production of proper specification MDO may be difficult, even at these relative prices.

### What Gives?

Newer vessels appear to be consuming less MDO, as a general rule, than older vessels and in some cases they do not burn any, thanks to engineering designs that have attempted to reduce this high cost fuel. In the very long run, the overall consumption of MDO will decline further as the fleet becomes more modern. But in the near-term, the amount of older vessel consumption will keep MDO demand relatively firm, despite the high price differentials.

As demand for MDO stays firm and the supplies get increasingly scarce, the relative price strength to crude will continue its upward creep. This will in turn contribute to higher daily costs for keeping ships moving and increased costs for all.
