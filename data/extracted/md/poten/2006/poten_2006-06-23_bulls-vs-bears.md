# Bulls vs. Bears

Baseball player Yogi Berra once succinctly observed, "Prediction is a risky business, especially when it's about the future." It used to be much easier to predict the tanker market direction when rates hovered in the $10,000/day range as they did for most of the 1980s and early 1990s. With rates over the past 18 months ranging from $15,000/day to over $120,000/day, the task is much more difficult. ### Outlooks Hit the Big Time

This week in New York City, the 19th annual Marine Money Week was the forum that exposed some disparities in thinking between tanker owners and Wall Street analysts on the future direction of the tanker market. From Tuesday to Thursday the conference addressed many topics pertaining to the current shipping markets in all sectors- LNG, dry bulk, chemical and crude.

It was Wednesday, however, that proved to be one of the most interesting days with the discussion "Oil Demand Growth - Can It Keep Pace With Supply?" This was moderated by CNBC's financial heavyweight Larry Kudlow, host of "Kudlow & Company." One of the points discussed amongst shipowners and Wall Street analysts was the impact of global events on current rates and hence the direction for future rates.

This conversation revealed a striking disagreement between Wall Street and public tanker owners. It has been a grievance of some owners that shares of their companies are selling at a discount to net asset value, especially in comparison to other industries. On the other side of the panel, some analysts maintained that the market will experience downward rate pressure in the future and by their estimations equity prices should be lower today.

The reality is that the benefits received from the cyclical and spot nature of the business are difficult to clearly convey to the equity markets. From the analysts' perspective, the anticipation for lower spot rates in 2006 led to a series of downgrades in the past several weeks.

The difference for 2006 from previous years, is that there is much more overall demand today for tonnage. More crude is in the market today than in prior years and vessels are employed for longer distances resulting in increased ton-mile demand.

### Future Appears Firm

Strong market fundamentals of supply and demand indicate that we can anticipate higher rates in the balance of 2006. As always the market commentary and outlooks that arise from panel discussions have proven to be very interesting. The strong projections for the market made by top executives, including one owner's outlook that VLCC rates will return to over $200,000 per day in the fourth quarter, may prove to be a bit too optimistic. While many are certainly bullish on the tanker market, projections like these are certain to raise some eyebrows. Changes in crude supply have enabled the market to absorb more tonnage overall and perhaps rates have universally shifted upward as the market experiences a higher rate floor. Tanker market history has taught us that rates will come down eventually. But using history as a guide has not been much help today.
