# Worldscale Flat Up!

The development of the "Worldscale" rate system is an extremely useful convention for chartering vessels on the spot market. Before World War II, freight rates were expressed in dollars or shillings and pence per long ton. (See website www.worldscale.co.uk/Worldscale/company/company.htm for a more thorough history of Worldscale). During World War Two, the U.S. and British governments requisitioned all tankers and paid owners on the basis of a prescribed daily earnings rate. The question arose as to what should be the freight rate charged to oil companies for out-chartering vessels to meet their needs. A system was devised based on preserving the daily earnings rate net of voyage costs (port, canal and bunker costs). The system survived the war in the form of various schedules, which, in the 1960s became Intascale published in London and ATRS published in New York. In 1969, Worldwide Tanker Nominal Freight Scale, more commonly known as Worldscale, replaced Intascale and ATRS. From its reception to the end of 1988, the nominal vessel used in calculating the flat rate was 19,500 tons with a daily nominal hire rate of $1,800. Bunkers prior to the oil crisis in 1973 was of the order of $10-15 per ton when crude was selling for $2-3 per barrel. Since 1989, the nominal vessel has been 75,000 tons with a daily nominal earnings rate of $12,000 per day.

Worldscale allows for owners, charterers, and brokers to easily determine market levels based on a $/metric ton reimbursement in a relatively straightforward manner for an estimated 70,000 port-to-port combinations (without counting the Quoin Island combinations). Flat rates, or Worldscale 100 rates, are revised when there is a significant change in voyage costs. Bunkers prices are affected by changes in crude oil prices and these changes have necessitated annual revisions to Worldscale flat rates. ### What Does the Market Say?

It is no small task to adjust the flat rates for changes in port charges, bunker prices, and exchange rates. But is it necessary? At the end of the year when the new flat rates apply to voyages with a load date on or after January 1, the spot market fixtures are based on a Worldscale percent for the new or old rates. Guess what happens? When fixing on the 2005 rates, the Worldscale percent was higher to reflect the lower flat rate and vice versa. This adjustment therefore provides the owner with the same revenues for a given voyage no matter which flat rate is used.

No doubt the discipline imposed by the annual process and constant updates keeps the system from developing inconsistencies over time. But not to worry - if and when inconsistencies develop, the market will respond accordingly.
