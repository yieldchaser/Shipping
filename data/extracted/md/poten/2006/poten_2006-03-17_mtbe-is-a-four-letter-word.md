# MTBE is a Four Letter Word

### Background

The Clean Air Act of 1990 started the ball rolling with regard to reformulated and oxygenated gasolines and their impact on air quality. These requirements were area- and seasonal-specific. Reformulated and oxygenated gasolines were intended to reduce hydrocarbon emissions that contributed to smog. Oxygenated gasoline was also intended to reduce carbon monoxide emissions during the winter. Reformulated and oxygenated gasolines were generally confined to urban areas while conventional gasoline was sold in the rest of the nation. On top of these Federal requirements, states could impose their own limits, creating a gasoline boutique in the United States where gasoline specifications varied by location and season.

Although the Clean Air Act did not specify the type of oxygenate, the two leading candidates were ethanol and MTBE (methyl tertiary butyl ether). The corn lobby was successful in defining eligible ethanol as that produced domestically from corn (maize). The oil industry picked MTBE because it was a refinery product already in use since the 1980s to enhance octane ratings after the phase-out of tetra ethyl lead. Moreover, MTBE was less expensive than ethanol. Over the years MTBE found its way into the water table affecting water supplies. Environmentalist objections to MTBE began to grow, particularly in California. In early 2003 California banned MTBE as an oxygenate and asked for a waiver on Federal oxygen content regulations. Subsequently other states, New York and Connecticut, banned MTBE in gasoline and switched to the alternative, ethanol.

### New Player

The Energy Policy Act of 2005 issued last August stepped into the fray over MTBE by: 2 Allowing California's reformulated gasoline to be made without ethanol. 2 Eliminating the requirement of oxygenates for reformulated gasoline for the rest of the nation within 270 days of the passage of the Act. 2 Not providing a limit on liability faced by refineries for use of MTBE after the RFG oxygenate requirement is abolished. 2 Requiring the use of 4 billion gallons of ethanol in 2006 and increasing the requirement to 7.5 billion gallons (equivalent to nearly 500,000 bpd) by 2012.

Ethanol is "splashed" into the gasoline stream at distribution terminals before shipment to service stations. Before the Energy Policy Act, ethanol was added in accordance with the requirements set forth in the Clean Air Act; now it is left to the discretion of oil companies on when and where to add ethanol. Ethanol is highly absorbent of water and shipping by pipeline is not feasible. Ethanol is normally shipped by railroad tankcar, which is relatively expensive. To reduce this expense, ethanol consumption might become more localized in the long term, but in the short term ethanol has a vital role to play in compensating for the removal of MTBE from the gasoline stream to maintain octane ratings. Thus, ethanol will remain a component in gasoline. As more ethanol is needed to replace MTBE, imports may be necessary that would require the building or expansion of ethanol import terminals. Generally speaking, ethanol imports are discouraged by high import duties1. However, a limited volume of ethanol can come from selected Caribbean nations under the Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI) that can enter the United States duty free. This includes Brazilian ethanol reprocessed in CBI nations.

### Impact of Phasing Out MTBE

According to an article entitled "Eliminating MTBE in Gasoline in 2006" published by the Energy Information Administration2 the following consequences are anticipated: 2 Net loss of domestic gasoline production capacity 2 A tight ethanol market making room for imports 2 Gasoline suppliers hampered by terminal facilities to store and blend ethanol 2 Loss of import supply sources that cannot deliver MTBE-free product or high quality blendstock.
