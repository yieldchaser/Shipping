# Out Producing OPEC

OPEC recently announced intentions to cut their crude oil production by 1.2 million barrels per day with a stated goal of ensuring crude oil "market stability." Additionally, swing producer Saudi Arabia verbalized support to reduce production. The chart below shows the current production, proposed cuts and quota levels of the OPEC-10 (excluding Iraq.) Interestingly, the two original proponents of the initiative to lower production were Venezuela and Nigeria, two of the countries that are actually underperforming their official production quotas.

### The Price is Right

Oil prices have continued their downward trend, despite OPEC wishes to reduce production in an effort to support prices. The recent high crude oil price levels have enabled non-OPEC supply to be developed, effectively mitigating the widespread effects of a decline in OPEC production.

The map below shows typical export trade routes (dashed lines) for Venezuela, Nigeria and Saudi Arabia and likely trades that could develop (solid lines) to compensate for a production cut.

With crude oil prices hovering around $60 per barrel, it is very difficult for producers to shut-in production- especially those that are not bound by OPEC quotas. Expensive capital investments create an incentive for non-OPEC production to increase. New developments have taken place in West Africa, the Caspian and Russia will compensate for any lost production by OPEC countries. Estimates indicate that non-OPEC supply will reach at least 53 million barrels per day on average in 2007, over 60% of total worldwide supply estimates.

Initially, it may appear that a 1.2 million barrel per day production cut from OPEC would hurt tanker demand, but there is evidence to the contrary. Increased ton-mile demand can be expected as crude supplies flow from non-traditional sources to make up for needed supplies. Despite $60 per barrel crude oil prices, consumers have not shown any signs of slowing down their demand for petroleum.

Price levels drive OPEC producers as well, and margins for less- than-$60 per barrel oil are still quite high. It is clear that not all countries will stick to their production quotas and that some will continue to 'overproduce.' In the longer term, decisions by OPEC or other producers to reduce crude oil output at a time of high demand creates more of an incentive for further exploration.

### Tankers to the Rescue

The tanker fleet is the linchpin in affording the crude oil market highly valued import flexibility. When supplies are curtailed from one location, the crude oil ends up coming from another location. Initial fears by some of a 1.2 million barrel per day hit to tanker demand appear to be overstated. In the short-run expect the market to rebound in the face of crude flow changes. In the long run, expect more crude to flow in unanticipated directions and the trend of ships passing in the night will continue.
