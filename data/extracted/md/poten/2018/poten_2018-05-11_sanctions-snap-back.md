# Sanctions Snap Back

### The oil and tanker market will feel the impact

### Iran Sanctions Snap Back

To nobody's surprise, President Trump decided to withdraw the United States from the 2015 international nuclear deal with Iran. The other signatories (China, France, Russia, the U.K., Germany, and Iran) have decided to stay in, at least for now. However, the secondary sanctions that will be reimposed by the U.S. after grace periods of between 90 and 180 days will have a global impact. Most of the sanctions affecting the shipping industry will be on the 6-month wind-down period. The oil markets are expected to lose at least some Iranian crude oil and the tanker market will feel the impact of changes in tradeflows and the uncertainty around vessel insurance and the impact of secondary sanctions on the global financial system.

We wrote about the possibility of the U.S. pulling out of the Joint Comprehensive Plan Of Action (JCPOA) in our April 6, 2018 Tanker Opinion "Deal Or No Deal?" and most of our assumptions and conclusions remain valid. Below we summarize some of our key points: 1. In December 2015, before sanctions relief went into effect, Iran produced 2.91 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) of crude oil. Iran managed to increase production to 3.77 MB/d by December 2016. Production growth has stabilized since then and the IEA estimates current production at around 3.8 Mb/d. 2. Iran has exported an average of 2.3 Mb/d of crude oil over the last 6 months. China is the single largest importer with about 0.63 Mb/d and as the 2nd graph indicates, imports have remained largely constant, even after the sanctions were lifted. Other large Asian importers are India, Japan and Korea. Combined with China, these countries account for about 65% of the total seaborne exports. Since the lifting of sanctions, countries in the Mediterranean, especially Turkey, Italy and Greece have re-emerged as large importers of Iranian crude. 3. VLCCs represent the main vessel class for Iranian exports, about 55% of the total. Suezmaxes are used for about 35% of the total exports, and logically dominate the Mediterranean trades and about half the volumes on the (short-haul) trade to India also move on Suezmax tankers. 4. The impact of the U.S. decision to re-instate the sanctions is far from clear. The earlier sanctions regime, which lead to a significant reduction of Iranian crude oil exports, was successful because of the cooperation of the international community, in particular the support from the EU. With the U.S. unilaterally reinstating sanctions, the international response is less coordinated and it is not clear what measures the U.S. might use to encourage cooperation.

5. Just as under the previous sanctions regime, the U.S. is explicitly targeting the Iranian oil industry. The U.S. will again pursue efforts to reduce Iran's sales of crude oil. It is uncertain to what extent some of the largest buyers, such as China, India and Turkey (~1.2 Mb/d combined) will participate in import reductions. Japan and Korea, with combined imports of about 0.4 Mb/d, are more likely to cooperate, given their security arrangement with the U.S. and the situation in North Korea. European buyers (~0.55 Mb/d) might take a divergent approach, depending on their individual situation and the willingness of the Trump administration to make exceptions. 6. U.S. sanctions could eventually reduce Iranian exports by 500-600 Kb/d. Replacement barrels for Japan and Korea would mostly be sourced from other Middle Eastern countries, reducing the immediate impact on the VLCC market. Lost European barrels might be replaced by a combination of Middle Eastern crude and short haul Russian barrels from the Baltic and the Black Sea, resulting in a minor negative impact.

The situation will remain fluid until we know exactly which new sanctions (if any) will be imposed and how the U.S. government will interpret and enforce them. Stay tuned!
