# Looks Familiar

### The beginning is similar - will the ending be the same?

The expression "history repeats itself" is very appropriate in the shipping markets. Seasonality and market cycles usually repeat themselves with a certain predictability. Earlier this week, one of my colleagues pointed out that so far in 2018 VLCC rates are trending very similar to 2013. Rates have been very disappointing this year with (seemingly) little improvement on the horizon. We thought it would be interesting to look back five years and see where 2018 may be similar to 2013 and where it could be different. After all, 2013 ended on a high note, with VLCC rates in November/December of that year averaging well over $50,000 per day, a far cry from the $13,000/day average for the other 10 months of the year. Could this year have a similar happy ending?

Let's first compare the fleet and the orderbook. In January 2013, there were 82 VLCC's on order for delivery through 2015. Based on a fleet of 612 vessels at the time, this represented 13.4% of the fleet. The VLCC fleet has grown significantly over the last five years. As per January 1st of 2018, the VLCC fleet stood at 735 vessels (a net growth of 123 units or 20%). However, the relative statistics are similar: the current orderbook is 92 vessels, or 12.5% of the fleet (a slightly lower proportion than in 2013). We know rates have been similar so far this year, but how do newbuilding and secondhand prices compare to the prices half a decade ago? Newbuilding prices for VLCCs are about 10% lower now than they were in 2013 ($83 versus $92 million), but secondhand prices are slightly higher. Overall, comparing Q1 2013 with Q1 2018, one could say that the VLCC fleet, orderbook and prices are fairly similar. And scrapping? Total VLCC demolition ended 2013 at 5.0 mdwt, almost double the volume of 2012 (2.7 mdwt), no doubt contributing to the market recovery. Estimated VLCC scrapping for 2017 was 3.9 mdwt and based on 2.9 mdwt VLCCs sold for scrap so far this year, it is likely that 2018 demolition will well exceed last year's number, which would help balance the tanker market.

The biggest discrepancy between 2013 and 2018 seems to be on the oil side. In early 2013, the effects of the global financial crisis were still felt. China, which accounted for nearly two- thirds of global oil demand growth during the period between 2007 and 2011, did not grow much at all for a large part of 2012 and oil demand in North America and Europe declined by a combined 900,000 b/d during 2012. These factors made the IEA rather conservative in their outlook. In March 2013, they forecast a modest 820,000 b/d (0.9%) growth year-over-year. High oil prices also tempered growth expectations. Brent was $110 per barrel in mid-March, while WTI was priced in the low to mid $90s. In the end, 2013 turned out much better than

expected as oil demand grew by 1.23 mb/d (1.4%), some 50%(!) higher than forecast earlier in the year. The current IEA outlook for 2018 is more positive with demand growth of 1.39 mb/d (1.4%). Lower oil prices are now helping the world economy fire on all cylinders. Continued oil demand growth should help the tanker markets this year, just like it did toward the end of 2013.

Some things never change. Geopolitics for example. A sentence in the IEA Oil Market Report of 18 January 2013 reads: "Today's market has a lot to do with political risk writ large, and not just in Syria, Iran, Iraq, Libya or Venezuela". This could have been written yesterday! Another parallel between 2013 and 2018 is lower OPEC output and rising U.S. production: in 2013 OPEC production declined by some 900,000 b/d versus 2012, mainly due to problems in Libya and Nigeria and sanctions against Iran. The call on OPEC was also reduced due to a 1.2 million b/d increase in crude oil production from the U.S. Sounds familiar? The same is likely to happen this year, with one significant difference: unlike in 2013, U.S. crude is now being exported (increasingly on VLCCs), which does add to tanker demand.

So, the circumstances are different, but the seeds for a recovery are there. 2018 may well end on the same positive note as 2013 (and let's hope that 2019 will see continued improvement and not look anything like 2014).
