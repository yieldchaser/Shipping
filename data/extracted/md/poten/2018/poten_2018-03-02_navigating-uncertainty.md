# Navigating Uncertainty

### The potential impact of geopolitics has increased

Back in 2014, when oil prices were well above $100 per barrel, analysts regularly talked about a geopolitical "risk premium" to explain the high oil prices. The theory is that geopolitical tensions, in particular those in oil producing countries or regions, can raise fears of supply shortages and push oil prices higher. However, since the oil price collapse in 2014, the market has been awash in oil and the impact of geopolitical events on the oil markets has been muted. This may be about to change. More than a year after OPEC and select non-OPEC countries decided to cut back production and exports, global oil markets are rebalancing (despite runaway growth in U.S. production) and the synchronized growth in the major world economies has pushed up demand for oil and oil products. At the same time, geopolitical tensions in the world have increased. In today's weekly opinion, we will review some of the key supply risks facing the oil markets and the impact potential disruptions may have on the tanker market.

A review of the key OPEC and non-OPEC producers that agreed to curtail production shows that some participants are better (or more committed) to trimming output than others. Some individual production cutbacks are also more the result of domestic problems rather than commitment to OPEC policy. The countries that currently pose the most significant risk to global oil supplies are Venezuela, Iran and Iraq. Less risky, but still worth monitoring are Libya and Nigeria.

Venezuela has an OPEC production quota of 1.97 million barrels per day (mb/d). In January 2017, Venezuela produced 1.61 mb/d, 360,000 b/d below its allocation and almost 500,000 b/d less than its output in December 2016, the last month before the OPEC cuts became effective. The reduction in Venezuelan output has nothing to do with the OPEC agreement, but is entirely due to domestic production problems. Analysts expect that Venezuelan production will decline by another 400- 600,000 b/d this year. Venezuela's troubled economy and tense political climate, which has only been exacerbated by U.S. sanctions, make a recovery in the near term very unlikely.

Iranian production has been fairly stable in recent months. After the sanctions were lifted in 2016, Iranian production quickly recovered from 2.9 mb/d (Dec. 2015) to 3.8 mb/d (the average of 2017). The main risk to Iran's production is the snap back of U.S. sanctions. President Trump chose not to recertify the Iran Nuclear Deal on January 12, which means that the U.S. Congress has until May (120 days) to propose new legislation or U.S. sanctions may be re-imposed. The impact of renewed U.S. sanctions on Iranian exports could be significant, even if the other parties to the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), such as the Europeans, China and Russia decide not to join the U.S. sanctions. Iranian crude oil exports could be reduced by 400 - 500,000 b/d.

Iraq is another wildcard. Within OPEC, it has been the country that has consistently produced above its quota. The uncertainty around future Iraqi output is mostly related to the upcoming elections, scheduled for May 2018 and the potential impact on crude oil production and exports from Iraq's Kurdish region. After the Iraqi Kurds decided to hold a referendum for independence in September 2017, crude oil exports from northern Iraq dropped from 700,000 b/d to 300,000 b/d. This 400,000 b/d remains at risk until after the general elections in Iraq.

Production in Libya and Nigeria seems to have stabilized in the second half of 2017, but both countries remain politically unstable and exports could take an unexpected dip at any time. Several hundred thousand barrels of production remain at risk.

Gradual declines such as in Venezuela are easier to deal with than sudden shocks (Iran and/or Iraq), but any oil supply disruption will likely lead to temporary dislocations and possibly increased tanker demand.
