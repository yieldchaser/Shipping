# Taking Stock of Inventories

### Movements in inventories can impact tanker markets

Few oil publications are followed as closely as the Weekly Petroleum Status Report, published every Wednesday morning by the U.S. Energy Information Administration. It gives a detailed account of weekly U.S. petroleum balances, including domestic oil production, imports, exports and movements in crude oil and petroleum product inventories. Prices move as a result of this report, especially when the movements are unexpected (which happens on a regular basis). This week was one of those weeks: counter to what most analysts expected, U.S. crude inventories fell and oil prices jumped. There are a lot of moving parts that go into these balances and some are influencing (or influenced by) the tanker market.

As can be seen from the first chart on the right, the biggest factors influencing the Weekly Crude Oil Balance are Oil Production and Refinery Inputs. Oil production was unchanged from last week (although it was up 1.3 million barrels per day (mb/d) from last year). Refinery inputs were down 329,000 b/d from the previous week as refinery maintenance starts to ramp up. Everything else being equal, a reduction in refinery runs would lead to an increase in inventories, but everything else was not equal. Two other factors conspired to bring inventories down: crude oil imports fell by 867,000 b/d to 7.0 mb/d and crude oil exports jumped by 722,000 b/d to reach more than 2.0 mb/d. The jump in exports may have been helped by the inclusion in last week's statistics of the first VLCC export from the Louisiana Offshore Oil Port (LOOP). A Bahri owned VLCC, chartered by Shell, loaded 2 million barrels of Mars crude oil destined for China. Assuming the test was successful, more direct VLCC exports can be expected going forward. LOOP is a welcome addition to the U.S. Gulf export infrastructure which will need to accommodate more exports going forward based on the expected growth in U.S. shale production.

Another potential source of future oil supplies from the U.S. is the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR). Deep underground storage caverns along the Texas and Louisiana Gulf Coast have the capacity to hold 727 million barrels of crude oil. Owned by the U.S. Government, it is the largest emergency supply of crude oil in the world. The SPR currently holds around 665 million barrels of crude oil. One of the SPR's core missions is to hold enough oil stocks to meet the requirement of the International Energy Agency (IEA) to maintain at least 90 days of net import protection. The rapid increase of domestic production in the U.S. has significantly reduced net imports of crude oil and petroleum products into the United States. Since 2005, the volume of oil stored in the SPR had been mostly stable at around 700 million barrels, but that has started to change. Since March of last year, the volume of oil stored in the SPR has been gradually reduced by 30 million barrels (from 695 million barrels in March 2017 to 665 million barrels currently). Further declines are expected over the next 10 years. Several bills enacted under the Obama presidency in 2015 and 2016 collectively call for the sale of 149 million barrels in U.S. government fiscal years (FY) 2017 through 2025. Recent legislation by the Trump administration has directed the sale of an additional 100 million barrels of oil from the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve in FY 2022 through 2027. Based on the combination of these legislated sales established in multiple acts of Congress, the SPR could decline by about 40% in the coming decade while still meeting requirements for petroleum import coverage. The SPR could decline from 695 million barrels at the start of 2017 to about 410 million barrels at the start of 2028.

The combination of these changes in the U.S. oil markets, i.e. higher production, more exports, lower imports and sales from the SPR make it more difficult to predict the movements in commercial crude oil stocks. The reduction in imports have made the U.S. a less important discharge area for crude oil tankers, but the rapid increase in crude exports (following the lifting of the export ban) has transformed the U.S. Gulf into a key export area.
