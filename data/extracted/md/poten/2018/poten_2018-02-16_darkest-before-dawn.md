# Darkest Before Dawn

### (When) will the tanker market improve?

The tanker market of the end of 2017 and the beginning of 2018 will probably be remembered as one of the worst in recent history. The fact that the tanker market of the 1980s was much worse (and probably lasted longer) is of little consolation to shipowners experiencing earnings that regularly do not cover vessel operating expenses, let alone corporate overhead and financing costs. Within the tanker markets, the product carriers appear to be doing slightly better than crude carriers, but even in those markets there is little to write home about.

The reason for the poor rates across the board is straightforward: there are too many ships. The combined age profile of all the main tanker segments shows that new tanker deliveries slowed considerably in the period 2013-2015, setting the stage for a healthy tanker market in 2015. However, the subsequent pickup in deliveries in 2016 and 2017 put tanker rates under pressure again. The current tanker orderbook shows a continued high level of deliveries scheduled for 2018, with a distinct drop-off in 2019 (although vessels can still be ordered for late 2019 delivery). In line with general market consensus, this suggests that 2018 will continue to be a challenging year. Shipowners can help the market and maybe accelerate a recovery by scrapping older tankers as special surveys come due and by not immediately ordering replacement vessels, despite attractive shipyard prices.

The other side of the equation is oil and tanker demand. The OPEC/Non-OPEC production cutbacks have hurt the tanker market, especially since Middle East producers continue to target market share in the growing Asian markets. OPEC is primarily cutting back on the long-haul trades to Europe and the U.S. as demand growth in the OECD countries in the Atlantic Basin is limited and domestic oil production (in particular shale oil from the U.S.) is increasing rapidly. OPEC also hopes that their cutbacks will reduce the highly visible inventory levels in these markets and provide support to oil prices.

Summarizing the state of the crude tanker markets, one could say that we are at a critical juncture in the market: if scrapping picks up and new orders remain subdued, we set the stage for a tanker market recovery, the timing of which will primarily be driven by OPEC's production decisions (at least for the VLCC and Suezmax markets). 2019 will be a transitional year, leading up to the highly anticipated date of January 1, 2020, when a reduced IMO sulphur cap for bunker fuels goes into effect. Unless they install scrubbers, older tankers that are still around come 2020 may be at a disadvantage because of their higher fuel consumption and the requirement to use more expensive low sulphur fuel. This could stimulate more scrappng.

The dynamics for the product tanker market are slightly different, in particular for the MR's. The orderbook for these vessels has been gradually declining and deliveries in 2017 (56) were down significantly from 2015 (105) and 2016 (75). The delivery schedule for 2018 and 2019 is similar to that of last year. Product tanker demand is not highly correlated to OPEC production levels. It is however driven by overall economic activity and the synchronized economic growth that the world is currently experiencing should be good news for this segment. Increases in U.S. product exports and more intra-Asian trade in 2018 are also supportive. The 2020 IMO rules could provide an additional boost to the product tanker market if sufficient volumes of low sulphur fuels are not in the right place at the right time.

All-in-all, the immediate outlook for the tanker market is not great, but we see enough positive developments on the horizon to remain optimistic about the future. Often, shipowners are their own worst enemy, but the current low market and dismal short-term outlook may provide the right ingredients for a turnaround. Supply side discipline, combined with healthy demand growth and some unexpected geopolitical event could turn this market around quicker than people think.
