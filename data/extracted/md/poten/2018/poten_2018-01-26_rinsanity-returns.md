# RINsanity Returns

### The impact of the Renewable Fuel Standard on U.S. refiners

Last weekend, Philadelphia Energy Solutions (PES), the largest oil refinery on the U.S. East Coast, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. PES operates two domestic refineries in Philadelphia with a combined processing capacity of 335,000 barrels per day (b/d). The refinery complex can trace its roots back to 1866, one year after the end of the Civil War, when Atlantic Petroleum Company established its operation at Point Breeze. Atlantic installed its first petroleum refining units in 1870 and changed its name to Atlantic Refining Company. In 2012, PES emerged from a joint venture between private equity firm The Carlyle Group LP and Sunoco Inc., which was bought that year by Energy Transfer Partners LP. What caused the problems at the refiner? PES has pointed to the cost associated with the Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) as a key driver for its financial troubles. According to PES, the independent refiner spent more than $800 million on Renewable Identification Numbers (RINs) since 2012 to comply with the RFS. What is the background of this government program and why is it so controversial?

The Renewable Fuel Standard was created with the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and was expanded and extended by the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007. The RFS requires renewable fuel to be blended into transportation fuel in increasing amounts each year, escalating to 36 billion gallons in 2022 (the volumes for 2017 were 25 billion gallons). The original objective of the program was to stimulate the production of renewable fuels to reduce U.S. dependence on foreign oil. In 2006, the U.S. imported more than 60% of its domestic oil requirements. Due to the shale oil boom and the corresponding growth in U.S. production, our dependence on foreign oil has been reduced to some 20% in 2017. Although the changes in U.S. production have increased the discussion about the future of the RFS, the program is still there and it has important implications for refiners, due to the high cost of RINs.

How does it work? The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) administers the RFS program and establishes the volume requirements for each category. The EPA tracks compliance through the Renewable Identification Number (RIN) system. Every time a gallon of ethanol is shipped from a production facility, a RIN is produced. This RIN has value and is theoretically included in the price of ethanol when it is sold by the ethanol producer. When the ethanol is purchased by a marketer for example, and blended into gasoline, the RIN is separated and can be sold. Entities regulated by RFS include oil refiners and gasoline and diesel importers. The volumes required of each obligated party are based on a percentage of its petroleum product sales. Obligated parties can meet their renewable volume obligations (RVOs) by either selling required biofuel volumes or purchasing RINs from parties that exceed their requirements. However, the volatility and unpredictability of RIN prices can create a substantial financial burden for merchant-model refiners that don't blend their own fuels.

Not surprisingly, there has been significant political back and forth on this subject, with farm-states (where most of the corn-based ethanol and soy- based bio-diesel is produced) pitted against the oil-states (where most of the refiners are based). So far, no changes to the RFS have been implemented, a significant win for the politicians representing the Midwest. The RFS program is very important for corn farmers: the U.S. Department of Agriculture estimates that some 38% of the U.S. corn crop is destined for ethanol plants.

It is unclear whether the bankruptcy filing of PES will have an impact on the debate. Senators Pat Toomey of Pennsylvania and Ted Cruz of Texas have been working on a number of legislative "fixes" that may offer a compromise: 1) Allow exported biofuels to count towards the refiners RIN quotas and/or 2) Require the EPA to sell "fixed price waiver credits" to refiners. Proposals like this, which reduce compliance cost for independent refiners and keep farmers in business may now have a better chance of being adopted.
