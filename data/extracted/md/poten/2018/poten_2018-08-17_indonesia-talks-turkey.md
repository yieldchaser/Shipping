# Indonesia Talks Turkey

### Key Asian producer wants to keep its oil in the country

The financial crisis in Turkey has created sharp fluctuations in the value of the Turkish Lira and a general weakening of emerging market currencies over the last two weeks. One of the countries that is affected is Indonesia, a large oil producer in Asia. Indonesia, which faces a significant (and growing) current account deficit, has seen its currency weaken significantly this year. A little more than 20 years ago, Indonesia was one of the largest victims of the Asian Financial Crisis. President Joko Widodo, who faces re-election next year, does not want a repeat of that currency crisis, which ultimately led to the resignation of then-president Suharto (after 31 years in office). To support its currency, Indonesia, a former OPEC member, wants to curb its dollar denominated imports. Earlier this week, the minister of Energy and Mineral Resources directed state oil company Pertamina to buy all domestically produced crude oil, including the production from overseas companies like ExxonMobil and Chevron. It is still uncertain if and how this plan will be executed. However, Indonesia is a significant crude oil importer as well as exporter in Asia and in this Tanker Opinion we will discuss the potential implications for tradeflows in the region.

Indonesia is one of the world's oldest oil producers. In 1885, a Dutch tobacco planter discovered the first commercial quantities of oil in North Sumatra and founded a company that ultimately became the Royal Dutch/Shell Group. Indonesian oil production grew rapidly and peaked in 1977 at 1.7 million barrels per day. Starting in the early 1990s, production gradually declined and Indonesia's output dipped below 1 million b/d in 2007. Indonesia is the fourth most populous country in the world and in 2004 it became a net importer of oil as growing domestic demand overtook stagnant production. In recent years exports have struggled, while imports continued to increase.

Indonesia's export markets have traditionally been in Asia. Thailand, China and Singapore are the main destinations for its crude. The Par (ex-Chevron) refinery on Hawaii is also a regular recipient of Indonesian crude. Indonesia exports almost all its crude oil on Aframaxes, driven by the country's port restrictions and production infrastructure, a significant portion of which is offshore. Since Indonesia is a country consisting of more than 17,000 islands, most of the domestic distribution of crude oil also takes place with tankers. Aframaxes dominate the domestic trades as well.

Crude oil imports into Indonesia have grown over time and in recent years have exceeded exports. Most of Indonesia's crude oil imports originate from the Arabian Gulf and West Africa. The AG trade involves mostly VLCCs, although many of these are only part- cargoes. The vessels only discharge one third or one half of their cargo in Indonesia before they continue to their final destination. West African imports mostly arrive on Suezmaxes, while intra-Asian movements from countries like Malaysia and Australia are typically moved on Aframaxes.

What will happen if Pertamina buys all the crude that is currently exported? Current exports are around 250,000 b/d, out of a total Indonesian crude oil production of 800,000 b/d. The exports that are subject to long-term supply agreements will continue (although the contracts cannot be renewed), but all the spot cargoes will now be absorbed by the Indonesian state oil company. Pertamina has stated that this will cut its imports by 60%.

A 250,000 b/d reduction in availability of Indonesian crude means that regional buyers will have to source this crude elsewhere. Depending on the grade and quality of the crude, the most likely alternatives can be found in the Middle East or the Atlantic Basin (West Africa and the U.S.). For the tanker market this means more ton-miles and a move away from Aframaxes to VLCCs. At the same time, Indonesia will also need to import less. Historically, this would mean less long-haul crude from the Middle East on VLCCs although recently, Indonesia increased imports from short-haul producers in the Pacific Basin. Overall, Indonesia's new policy will likely have a limited impact on the tanker market although it could be slightly positive for VLCCs.
