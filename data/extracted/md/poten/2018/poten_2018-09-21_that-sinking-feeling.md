# That Sinking Feeling

### Iranian crude oil exports under pressure from sanctions

On May 8, President Trump announced the U.S. withdrawal from the Iran nuclear deal. After a grace period, most of the oil export-related sanctions will be reinstated by the U.S. on November 4. While the start date of the sanctions is still one and a half month away, Iranian oil sales are already being impacted.

President Trump is taking a very different approach than the Obama administration did in 2012. Back then, a coalition of countries decided on a significant reduction in imports of Iranian oil. A reduction of crude oil imports by 20% was generally considered sufficient. The U.S. did not import Iranian crude and Europe also stopped importing Iranian crude while countries such as Japan and Korea reduced imports. Countries that did not adhere to the requested cuts faced U.S. sanctions. The current administration has taken a unilateral and more aggressive approach: Countries need to completely halt imports of Iranian crude to avoid U.S. sanctions. Several countries (i.e. India, Japan and South Korea) have asked for waivers to maintain the ability to import (some) Iranian crude, but the administration has not yet granted any waivers.

So far, the threat of sanctions has been very effective. As the first graph illustrates, Iranian exports have started to decline several months before the deadline. According to shipping data from Lloyd's List Intelligence, Iran exported an average of 2.35 million barrels per day (Mb/d) by tanker in the first 5 months of 2018. In August, seaborne exports were reduced to an estimated 1.77 Mb/d. Crude trade flows between individual countries are typically volatile, so it is hard to distinguish trends by looking at short periods of time. However, it appears that sanctions are starting to bite. Korea seems to have stopped Iranian imports completely (they account for about half of the decline in Iranian exports). Japanese imports have been reduced as well and so have movements to India. India imported about 180Kb/d less crude from Iran in August, compared to the average imports in the prior three months, which were unusually high. Indian imports in August were in line with the average imports of 2017, so in the previous months they may have taken advantage of price discounts offered by Iran. Iranian exports to China, which had been averaging about 672 Kb/d over the first 7 months of the year, amounted to only 574 Kb/d in August, although this could be just a part of the normal monthly volatility.

The question for the coming months is how much further exports will decline and which countries will replace the lost output. Many European companies have decided to cut imports as the threat of U.S. sanctions against their U.S. operations or their payment streams outweighs the benefit of buying Iranian crude, even though some European governments would prefer to continue trading with Iran to maintain the original agreement. Chinese oil purchases from Iran could get wrapped up in the ongoing trade dispute between the U.S. and China. According to press reports, Japanese refiners have decided to temporarily halt Iranian crude oil imports. Japan has imported about 115 Kb/d to date in 2018. Indian imports from Iran are expected to decline substantially as most Indian refiners have already announced cuts or stopped importing altogether. Saudi Arabia, Iran's neighbor across the AG, is seen as the most likely replacement of Iranian oil as it has spare capacity that could be brought to market relatively quickly.

During the previous period of sanctions, Iran significantly increased the use of their own crude oil tanker fleet for floating storage. This may happen again, although the uptick in floating storage so far has been limited.

The Iranian sanctions have not had a material impact on the tanker market so far. A switch from Iran to Saudi Arabia does little for ton-mile demand. More U.S. crude oil exports could be helpful, but this export growth may be delayed due to logistical bottlenecks in the U.S. If geopolitical tensions rise further, resulting oil price increases could start to have an impact on global oil demand and GDP growth. That is the last thing tanker owners want to hear.
