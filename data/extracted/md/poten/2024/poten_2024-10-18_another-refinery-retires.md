# Another Refinery Retires

### U.S. West Coast crude and product flows will be impacted

On October 16, Phillips 66, the 4th largest refiner in the U.S., announced plans to cease crude oil processing operations at its 138,700 b/d dual-sited refinery in Los Angeles, California, by the end of 2025. Based on market conditions, P66 is concerned about the long-term viability and competitiveness of this refinery, which has been in operation for more than 100 years. P66 committed to replacing the output of the LA refinery with sources inside and outside its refining network and with renewable diesel and sustainable aviation fuels from its recently commissioned Rodeo Renewable Energy Complex (RREC) in the San Francisco Bay area. The closure of this refinery, which produced 8% of California's gasoline, will have implications for both crude oil and refined product flows in to and out of the U.S. West Coast. It could impact both international flag and Jones Act movements. We'll delve deeper into these topics in this week's Tanker Opinion.

California has a reputation for being one of United States' greenest states, being a first mover on climate policy, renewable energy and electric vehicles. However, the "Golden State" is still a fossil fuel powerhouse. It has the third largest refining capacity in the United States, after Texas and Louisiana. California is the country's most populous state, so it no surprise that it is the largest consumer of jet fuel and the second-largest user of gasoline in the U.S., despite having by far the largest penetration of electric vehicles in the country (three times the national average in 2022). In-state gasoline consumption peaked in June 2007 at 8.96 million gallons per day. This number declined by more than 50% to 4.3 million gallons per day in February 2020, just prior to the Covid-19 outbreak spreading to the United States (no data is available from June 2021 onward). The number of refineries in California has declined from around 50 a few decades ago to just 11 currently (this excludes three small facilities that focus on Asphalt). California's remaining refineries are concentrated in three regions: Contra Costa County (East of San Francisco), Kern County (San Joaquin Valley) and Los Angeles County.

Crude oil production in California peaked in February 1986 at 1.1 million barrels per day (Mb/d) and has declined gradually ever since. Production currently stands at 283,000 b/d. California still has 1.5 - 1.7 Mb/d of refining capacity, so this means that most crude oil is coming from outside of the state. Alaska has been a reliable long-term supplier, providing 200,000 b/d of ANS crude. However, Alaskan production has been declining and will likely continue to do so, until ConocoPhillips' giant Willow oil project comes on stream. Willow is expected to supply up to 180,000 b/d at peak operation, but first oil is not expected until 2029. Middle Eastern suppliers, Iraq and Saudi Arabia are also large exporters of crude oil to California, as are producers in South America, such as Guyana (via Panama), Ecuador and Brazil. Canada has become a growing supplier to California. Since the opening of the Trans Mountain Expansion pipeline in May 2024, exports from Vancouver to California have grown from an average of 25,000 b/d in prior years to 130,000 b/d since June of this year, with further growth possible. The closure of the P66 refinery will probably reduce crude oil imports in the future and under normal circumstances shorter-haul suppliers (Alaska, Canada) will likely maintain market share, at the detriment of oil producers in the Middle East.

We don't expect refined product demand in California to drop rapidly and shutting down 139,000 b/d of refining capacity will likely boost the need for more imports into the state. California currently imports about 150,000 b/d. Some of the additional flows may come from domestic sources, either from refiners on the West Coast or from plants on the U.S. Gulf Coast. However, because of the Jones Act, these barrels will need to be moved on U.S. Flag tonnage. The Jones Act fleet is relatively small and capacity constrained. The fleet of U.S. tanker and barges is fully utilized and there is no orderbook. Given these restrictions on domestic flows, we will likely see more imports from long- haul suppliers in Asia to satisfy product demand from California. South Korea is by far California's largest supplier, but Singapore and India are also regular exporters to the U.S. West Coast. China is another potential supplier of refined products to California. The Chinese have excess refining capacity and could easily ramp up exports. By the end of 2025 and into 2026, these developments could boost product tanker employment in the Pacific Basin.
