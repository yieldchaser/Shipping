# End Of The Road For European Diesel

### However, tanker ton-miles remain robust for the time being

For many years, the transatlantic arbitrage trade for product tankers consisted of diesel movements from the United States to Europe and gasoline from Europe back to the U.S. Europe was traditionally short diesel and long gasoline, while the reverse was true in the U.S. This has been the basis for a lucrative triangulation trade that product tankers (mostly MRs) are taking advantage of. However, circumstances are changing, especially in Europe. European diesel demand is on the decline. In this Tanker Opinion we will try to determine what the reasons are, whether the declines are irreversible and - important for the tanker industry - how falling diesel demand will impact ton- mile demand for product carriers.

Demand for diesel in Europe is driven by the economy, environmental regulations and the weather. On top of the that, we have to consider the geopolitical situation. Traditionally, diesel has been an important road fuel for both cars and trucks in Europe. In the mid-1990s, European governments implemented policies favouring diesel cars. Diesel was taxed less than gasoline and consumers were also encouraged to buy diesel cars because they had lower CO2 emissions. By the mid- 1990s, diesel cars accounted for less than 10% of the European car fleet, but by 2012, this figure had surged to over 50% in some countries, like the UK. This led to a significant increase in diesel demand. However, the advantages of diesel cars may have been overstated, including the environmental benefits, highlighted by the 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal, also known as "Dieselgate". The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency discovered that Volkswagen had equipped its diesel vehicles with software designed to cheat emissions tests.

European diesel demand peaked in 2017 at 6.5 million barrels per day (Mb/d), and 2019 demand was close to that number as well. However, the pandemic reduced demand in 2020 to 5.94 Mb/d. Demand recovered after the pandemic to 6.24 Mb/d, but dipped again in 2023 (6.0 Mb/d) and for this year the IEA expects European diesel demand to drop to well below the pandemic level of 2020. Diesel car registrations are down as more people opt for Electric Vehicles or Hybrids. Economic activity in Europe is also subdued, so diesel demand for heating and agriculture is similarly down.

The European diesel market has also been impacted by the war in Ukraine. Russia was a major source of diesel for Europe (see [Chart 1](file:///C:/Users/Dell/Github/Shipping/data/extracted/charts/poten/2024/poten_2024-08-30_end-of-the-road-for-european-diesel_chart1.png)), supplying almost 50% of all imports. The sanctions against the Kremlin eliminated this short-haul supply of competitively priced diesel, leading to major price increases after the invasion. Since Europe remained structurally short diesel, other suppliers, such as the United States and Saudi Arabia have had to step up. However, this meant significantly higher delivered cost due to materially higher freight cost. More recently, the continued demand for diesel in Europe, combined with an oversupply in Asia, in particular China, has even motivated some owners of VLCCs to "clean up" their very large crude oil tankers to enable them to move large quantities of diesel to Europe. The turmoil caused by the Houthi's in the Red Sea has made traversing the Suez Canal problematic, hence the use of VLCCs going around the Cape of Good Hope. According to Vortexa, up to three VLCCs are destined to Europe or have already arrived.

In conclusion, while diesel demand in Europe seems to be under pressure, it has not yet negatively impacted ton-mile demand for diesel imports. To the contrary, increasing imports from the U.S. after Russia's invasion of Ukraine has reignited the transatlantic triangulation trade (see [Chart 2](file:///C:/Users/Dell/Github/Shipping/data/extracted/charts/poten/2024/poten_2024-08-30_end-of-the-road-for-european-diesel_chart2.png)) and the growing imports from the Middle East and (most recently) Asia has boosted demand for larger product carriers and even clean trading crude oil tankers. Over the longer term, slowing European diesel demand will eventually lead to lower import requirements. The drive towards cleaner transportation fuels in Europe (some European cities are already banning diesel cars or are planning to do so in the near future) seems irreversible. However, the current geopolitical tensions around Russia and in the Middle East have given this trade a new lease on life.
