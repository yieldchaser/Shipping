# A Goldilocks Outlook For Tankers

### The tanker market is in a good place for the next several years

The latest IEA Monthly Oil Market Report (OMR) which was published today was an interesting read. Tanker owners should be happy with the current market dynamics. Some of the key developments on the oil supply and demand side seems to be quite beneficial for tanker demand and hence supportive for tanker rates at least for the next two-three years. In this week's Tanker Opinion, we will discuss some of these developments.

First of all, oil demand keeps growing for the foreseeable future, albeit at a slower pace than in recent past. The April IEA OMR, which now includes an outlook for 2025, shows that global oil demand in 2023 was 2.3 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) higher than in 2022. The IEA considers 2023 the last year of the post- pandemic rebound and growth in 2024 (+1.2 Mb/d) and 2025 (+1.1 Mb/d) is expected to be considerably slower. The main reason for the slowdown in growth is China. According to the IEA, China made up 79% of the global demand increase in 2023. In 2024, China's share in oil demand growth is expected to decline to 45% and in 2025 it slumps to 27%. Despite China's growth slowdown, Asia/Pacific will continue to be the driver of global demand growth with 1.0 Mb/d in 2024 and 0.8 Mb/d in 2025. The Middle East and Africa will also consume more oil in the next few years. Oil demand in the OECD, by contrast, is declining and will continue to do so, especially in Europe. The IEA sees global oil demand growth returning to historical trend after the disruptions caused by Covid and the Russian invasion.

While the demand growth rates are not very exciting for the tanker market, we have to look at the combination of oil demand and oil supply to see the implications for shipping. Now the picture brightens. While oil demand is growing primarily in the Asia/Pacific region, the additional supply of crude oil is coming from the Atlantic Basin. This is due to a combination of supply growth in the United States, Brazil, Guyana and Canada. According to the IEA, these four countries alone could come close to meeting global oil demand growth in 2024 and 2025 as they expand production by 1.2 Mb/d and 1.0 Mb/d respectively. Data from Vortexa ([Chart 1](file:///C:/Users/Dell/Github/Shipping/data/extracted/charts/poten/2024/poten_2024-04-12_a-goldilocks-outlook-for-tankers_chart1.png)) shows that this trend from the Atlantic to the Pacific already started in 2022. This was due to the sanctions that were imposed on Russia after it invaded Ukraine. As countries in Europe and North America stopped buying Russian crude and products, the Kremlin expanded its sales to Asia (primarily India and China). This provided a massive boost to tanker ton-mile demand. The OPEC+ countries stimulated tanker demand further with their voluntary output cuts that began in late 2022. Less oil from the Middle East and more from non-OPEC in the Atlantic Basin.

Overall, the Russian invasion and the voluntary production cuts by OPEC+ worked out in favor of tanker owners as they increased ton-mile demand. The conflict in the Middle East and the attacks on shipping in the Red Sea that followed created a further disruption to trade flows as commercial ships, including tankers, increasingly take the longer route around the Cape of Good Hope to avoid Houthi missiles. This further adds to tonnage demand.

So, despite the fact that oil demand growth has not been spectacular in recent years, the combination of factors described above has created a healthy tanker market with significant upside volatility. A major contributing factor has been the modest fleet growth in recent years. Deliveries across all tanker segments were low in 2023, which was not surprising given that the uncertainty during the Covid-19 pandemic in 2020/2021 limited contracting. Deliveries in 2024 and 2025 will also be muted.

In summary, the outlook for the tanker market looks strong. Geopolitical tailwinds in combination with ton-mile demand expansion despite limited oil demand growth will favor tanker owners. As always, there are factors that could derail the good times, like a global recession or a reversal of Russian trade flows to its pre-invasion patterns. However, neither of these seems very likely at the moment. The market has probably more upside than downside risk. More sanctions against Russia could lower the effective utilization of the dark fleet which will support the mainstream owners. As the tension in the Middle East mounts, a further escalation is possible. If that happens all bets are off
