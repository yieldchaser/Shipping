# Lets Try That Again

### Canada revives plans for an additional crude export pipeline

Last week, on May 15, 2026, the provincial government of Alberta and the federal government of Canada announced that they have agreed on "a pathway to the construction of a new oil pipeline to Asian markets commencing as early as Sept. 1, 2027." This announcement follows the signing of an "Alberta- Canada Energy Agreement" in November 2025. This represents the start of a long process of planning, consultations among all constituents, securing permits, raising funds and maybe, ultimately, construction. If successful, this new pipeline would add 1 Million barrels per day of export capacity to the West Coast of Canada by the mid-2030s. This pipeline, dubbed "Northern Gateway 2.0", could fundamentally change crude oil trade flows in and around North America and have profound implications for the tanker market.

Let's start with the basics. This is not a new idea. Some 20 years ago, there were plans for a Northern Gateway 1.0. The original Northern Gateway project, initially pitched by Canadian pipeline company Enbridge in 2006, was designed to run twin pipelines from Bruderheim, Alberta, to Kitimat, B.C. It was officially cancelled by Canada's federal government in 2016 following a Federal Court of Appeal ruling that cited inadequate Indigenous consultation, coupled with a subsequent federal oil tanker moratorium. The updated "2.0" vision is different in several fundamental ways: (1) The current proposal shifts the destination from Kitimat to the port of Prince Rupert, BC; (2) The participating First Nations will hold substantial ownership stakes and direct seats on the board of directors, and (3) Rather than leaving a private corporation to navigate the political headwinds alone, the Alberta government has committed to spearhead the early regulatory applications to de-risk the project before handing it off to the private sector.

While the above steps are helpful, the project still faces significant hurdles. First, there is the West Coast Tanker Ban. Canada's Oil Tanker Moratorium Act legally bans tankers carrying more than 12,500 metric tonnes of crude oil from stopping or loading at ports along B.C.'s northern coast. This law will need to be repealed or amended. Second, the pipeline has to cross the province of British Columbia, and their leadership remains unconvinced of its merits and is also concerned about the environmental risks. While some First Nations backing has been secured via the National Coalition of Chiefs, an alliance of Coastal First Nations in northwest B.C. remains staunchly opposed to pipelines reaching their shores.

This is a very ambitious project, that has a long way to go (if it is approved at all), but for the purpose of this Tanker Opinion, we assume that the project will be successful and the pipeline will be built. What will the impact be of having another 1 Mb/d of crude oil, or 15 VLCC cargoes per month available to load on Canada's west coast?

The commercial target would be Asian complex refiners that can process heavy sour or diluted bitumen-type crude: China first, then South Korea, India, and potentially other Pacific refiners. Asian refiners will be eager to snap up these Canadian barrels. It will help them to diversify away from the Persian Gulf.

A new export outlet on Canada's west coast will also have an impact on the flows from the Transmountain (TMX) pipeline. In the first 4 months of 2026, about 65% of the exports from Vancouver went to Asia, mostly on Aframaxes. The Northern Gateway may switch the TMX emphasis back to the USWC.

The vast majority of Canada's crude oil exports are traditionally directed towards its southern neighbour because there is ample existing pipeline capacity linking the two countries, and the U.S. is a large consumer. The U.S. Midwest (PADD2) in particular is very dependent on pipeline crude from Canada. If the Canadian producers have an alternative outlet (Asia), refiners in PADD2 may have to pay more to retain access to these barrels. PADD3 (U.S. Gulf) refiners have alternatives, so they can switch to alternative (seaborne) imports from Latin America (Venezuela, Colombia, Brazil) or the Middle East.

It is impossible to predict all the changes in trade flows resulting from a potential new pipeline, but it seems very likely that Northern Gateway 2.0 will boost seaborne trade and tanker demand, mainly in the Pacific (for VLCCs), but potentially also in the Atlantic Basin (for Aframaxes and Suezmaxes).
