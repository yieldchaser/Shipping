# Strait Back To Normal

### What happens after the Strait of Hormuz reopens?

It has been 69 days since the U.S. and Israeli military campaign against Iran started. This led immediately to the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, blocking some 20 million barrels per day (Mb/d) of crude, condensate, and petroleum products from reaching world markets. This is equal to roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption and more than one- quarter of global seaborne oil trade. After a 40-day combat campaign, a ceasefire was concluded on April 8. However, one month after the fighting (mostly) stopped, negotiations between the U.S. and Iran have not yet led to a breakthrough and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed. It is impossible to predict when a deal will be concluded and under what terms. However, at some point, the Strait of Hormuz will reopen and in this Tanker Opinion we will brainstorm about some possible scenarios for the oil and tanker market when that happens.

The best-case scenario is a "clean reopening". The war ends with credible monitoring, naval deconfliction, assurances that all mines are cleared and no major post-deal attacks. Under this scenario, oil prices initially sell off, then stabilize. Gulf exports ramp up, initially from inventory and floating storage, while Middle East producers start to repair facilities and bring back production. Freight rates remain strong for several months because of backlog and market inefficiencies. Tankers will reposition back to the normal trade patterns. By late 2026, crude supply is mostly restored. Oil prices will be lower, and tanker rates start to cool. Under this "relief rally" scenario, consumers get lower fuel prices, Asian refiners regain supply, and tanker owners still earn well during the recovery phase.

An alternative, but maybe more realistic, scenario is the "nervous reopening". Under this scenario, diplomats have declared peace, but the market keeps acting afraid. The Strait is technically open, but insurers, crews, and owners do not fully trust it. Transits resume under escorts or special permissions. War-risk premiums fall but insurance remains expensive. Oil prices do not collapse; they trade with a stubborn risk premium. Tanker rates stay choppy because the market keeps paying for reliability, crew risk, and flexibility.

Another possible scenario is the "false dawn". The Strait reopens, but shortly thereafter a tanker is attacked, seized, or hits a mine. Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz slows immediately, oil spikes again and governments start thinking about releasing emergency reserves. Tanker rates skyrocket and recession fears return. Even a small incident could trigger this because the market no longer treats a Hormuz closure as unthinkable. Under this scenario, the market would be back to square one.

The last scenario we like to consider is what could be called "supply flood". This scenario pictures a future where peace comes with broader sanctions relief and political arrangements that allow Iranian and more Gulf barrels into the mainstream market. Under this scenario we envision that the UAE, no longer a member of OPEC, will open the taps and boost production and exports. At the same time, demand has already been damaged by high prices and will not return to pre-war levels right away. Inventories rebuild quickly, OPEC+ struggles to manage returning supply, and oil prices slide faster than expected. This scenario projects mixed fortunes for tanker owners: cargo volumes will rise, but tonmile demand may disappoint as Asian refiners return to shorter Middle East routes instead of long-haul Atlantic replacements.

A possible path is the second scenario fading into the first: a nervous reopening, followed by gradual normalization. The Strait of Hormuz will reopen, but traffic will only return slowly because it will take some time to regain the trust from owners and charterers. Oil prices probably fall from wartime levels but will not go back to the old pre-war curve.

Tanker markets will probably experience a two-stage move. First, spot rates and war-risk surcharges fall from extreme levels as the "closed Strait" premium comes out. But then there is a backlog effect: cargoes waiting inside the Gulf, ships waiting outside, port inspections, naval escort protocols, and charterers competing for owners willing to send crews back through the Strait. After that we will enter the restocking phase. Over the longer-term (1-3 years), however, tanker rates could come under pressure because fleet supply growth is heavy.
