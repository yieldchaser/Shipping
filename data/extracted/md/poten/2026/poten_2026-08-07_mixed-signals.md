# Mixed Signals

### U.S. crude oil exports drop, but refined products keep flowing

As a result of the war in the Middle East and the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, global oil and refined product supplies tightened significantly. Countries around the world responded by tapping emergency stockpiles, cutting imports and restricting exports. The International Energy Agency (IEA) led its 32 member countries in executing a release of 426 million barrels of oil from their emergency reserves: 301 million barrels of crude and 125 million barrels of refined products. The United States was by far the largest contributor, committing 172 million barrels of crude oil from its Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR). The SPR releases contributed to a large increase in U.S. crude oil exports. Refined product exports jumped as well. After three months of high outflows, crude oil exports declined in July, but product flows out of the U.S. remained strong. In this Tanker Opinion, we will explain why crude and product exports are diverging and what impact it has on the tanker market.

The Trump administration authorized the SPR release on March 11, 2026, and the first physical shipments started on March 20th. The Department of Energy structured the discharge to distribute the oil over roughly 120 days. The increases in U.S. crude oil exports were therefore concentrated in April, May and June 2026 ([Chart 1](file:///C:/Users/Dell/Github/Shipping/data/extracted/charts/poten/2026/poten_2026-08-07_mixed-signals_chart1.png)). U.S. crude oil exports averaged 5.9 Million barrels per day (Mb/d) during this period (Q2 2026). The average exports in Q1 2026 were around 4.6 Mb/d, so the SPR releases were main driver for the bump in exports, and it is logical that the exports dropped back to pre-war levels when the SPR releases stopped on July 9, 2026. The emergency releases have reduced the U.S. SPR to about 308 million barrels, the lowest level since March 1983. However, there are plans to replenish the reserves. The Trump administration structured the SPR releases as an "oil exchange" program rather than outright sales. Participants in the program "borrow" the oil and are contractually obligated to repay the oil "in kind" from November 2026 through 2029 with a 18-24% premium. This means companies must return roughly 1.2 barrels for every 1 barrel they borrowed, adding millions of bonus barrels to the reserve at no cost to taxpayers.

The SPR releases did alleviate some of the shortages in the oil market, albeit temporarily. Most of the additional barrels were shipped to Asia (on VLCCs) and Europe (on Aframaxes), boosting ton-mile demand and tanker earnings. When the releases were scheduled, most people did not expect that the conflict would still be going on and that transit through the Strait of Hormuz would still be restricted some six months after the conflict started. Now, the Houthi's also entered the conflict, limiting transit through another chokepoint (Bab el-Mandeb Strait) as well. World oil supply remains highly restricted and as global SPR releases reduced supply cushions, price volatility in the oil markets will remain high. In the meantime, tankers continue to deal with logistical challenges and dislocations that increase ton-miles and reduce their efficiency.

On the refined product side, the global supply-demand situation was and remains equally (if not more) challenging than for crude oil. Export refineries in the Middle East cannot get their product to market due to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the global shortage of crude oil restricts refining output in most other parts of the world. The U.S. is the big exception here. Ample domestic crude oil supplies and high product prices have incentivized the massive U.S. refining complex to maximize output and boost exports. Lost Middle Eastern product exports, tight European refining capacity and restrictions on Russian-linked fuel flows made U.S. diesel and jet fuel especially valuable in export markets. The decision by the Trump administration to temporarily waive the Jones Act, allowing foreign flag tankers to trade between U.S. ports, has boosted refined product movements within the United States.

As [Chart 2](file:///C:/Users/Dell/Github/Shipping/data/extracted/charts/poten/2026/poten_2026-08-07_mixed-signals_chart2.png) shows, U.S. refined product exports have remained at record highs through July and record crack spreads will likely keep U.S. refiners operating at near maximum practical capacity (>95%) for the foreseeable future. While countries in Central and South America remain the biggest customers, exports to Asia (+21%), Europe (+17%) and Africa (+51%) increased significantly from Q1 to Q2, boosting ton-mile demand for product tankers.
