# OPEC At A Crossroads

### Will OPEC's influence fade or will the cartel adapt again?

The crisis in the Middle East and, in particular, the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints, has reminded oil markets of the importance of Middle East OPEC. It also highlighted that the key drivers to OPEC's influence is not only the cartel's massive oil reserves, production levels and spare capacity, but also the ability of its members to reliably and cheaply ship these barrels to global markets. While the former drivers remain undisputed, the latter has been cast in doubt. This is not the first crisis in the storied history of OPEC, and it probably won't be the last. In this week's Tanker Opinion, we'll discuss some of the cartel's history, how they dealt with challenges before and how this crisis could shape the future of OPEC and the oil market for years to come.

OPEC was founded in 1960 by five countries: Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela. At the time, global oil markets were dominated by Western companies (the "Seven Sisters") and producing countries had little control over pricing. The founding members decided that it was important for these countries to regain sovereignty over oil revenues. In the early years, OPEC grew rapidly and by the early 1970s, it had grown to 12 members, turning OPEC into a broad coalition of producers across the Middle East, Africa, Latin America and Southeast Asia.

The 1973 oil embargo during the Yom Kippur War (carried out by the Arab members of OPEC) gave rise to oil as a geopolitical weapon. Prices surged dramatically and OPEC demonstrated that it could move global economies. By the late 1970s, OPEC was at peak power. Things changed in the 1980s. High prices caused countries to switch to alternative fuels (coal, nuclear and natural gas) and develop domestic (non-OPEC) supplies. Discoveries in Alaska, the North Sea and Siberia started to erode OPEC's control of the oil markets and as a result, OPEC's market share fell from ~50% (1979) to under 30% by 1985. Oil prices collapsed in 1986, when Saudi Arabia gave up its swing producer role and increased production ton regain market share. In the years that followed, OPEC focused on managing volatility. The cartel begins cooperating with non-OPEC producers (eventually forming OPEC+ in December 2016).

The last 10-15 years, OPEC has been stuck in a strategic trap. Non-OPEC production has increased dramatically, driven in large part by the U.S. shale revolution, which has made the United States the world's largest oil producer. Canada, Brazil and Guyana have also significantly expanded output. As OPEC's market position has eroded, it can try to maintain prices or protect its market share, but it cannot do both. Even before the Iran war, OPEC faced multiple challenges: competition from flexible producers (U.S. shale); members with vastly different fiscal needs and political agendas; a complex set of targets/quotas that are hard to enforce consistently and, slower oil demand growth due to efficiency gains and the impact of the energy transition (renewables, EV's and climate policies).

The Iran war has added a new set of challenges for OPEC. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the Iranian attacks on energy infrastructure in the Persian Gulf have forced the Middle East members of the cartel (except Iran) to shut in production. OPEC Gulf crude production, excluding Qatar, declined by a sharp 8.9 mb/d according to the IEA. They estimate that even after the war ends and the Strait of Hormuz fully reopens, it will take several months to re-establish steady exports, and initial volumes will remain well below pre-conflict levels. Infrastructure damage has to be repaired, workers have to come back, tankers need to be repositioned, etc. And even after everything is back to "normal", the uncomfortable truth is that Iran has shown that they have the ability to choke off 20% of global oil supply. That is a new reality.

The current situation does pose significant challenges for OPEC. Countries will try to diversify their sources of oil supply and replenish (or build) petroleum reserves, while at the same time reducing their independence on fossil fuels and accelerating the transition to renewables. However, OPEC has proven in the past that they can adapt to new realities. If they can maintain internal cohesion and accept a lower market share (two big "ifs"), they can maintain influence and act as a price stabilizer. Ultimately, as non-OPEC investment in oil declines due to lower prices and the ongoing energy transition, OPEC's low-cost reserves will become valuable again.
