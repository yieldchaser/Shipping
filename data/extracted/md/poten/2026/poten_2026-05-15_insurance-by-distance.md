# Insurance By Distance

### Asia's oil diversification drive can redraw global tanker routes

For many decades, mapping the oil flows in Asia was simple. The largest consumers in the region, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and later, China and India had limited or no domestic crude oil production. At the same time, the Middle East, which was geographically fairly close, had abundant resources. Despite the occasional regional conflicts in the Middle East, the flow of oil from the Persian Gulf was deemed both reliable and competitively priced and Asian refiners had little incentive to diversify their sources of crude. That is, until March 2026, when the Strait of Hormuz closed as a result of the war on Iran. Now, the same countries in Asia are looking to diversify their sources of crude oil supply. In this Tanker Opinion, we will discuss to what extent this can be achieved and what the potential implications are for the global tanker market.

As mentioned above, there are a number of countries in Asia that have been highly dependent of the Middle East for their crude oil supply. Japan imports around 95% of its crude oil from the Middle East, South Korea 72% and Taiwan 70%. Smaller importers, like Pakistan, the Philippines and Sri Lanka source almost 100% of their crude from the Middle East. China and India are more diversified, but due to their size remain the largest importers by volume. Both countries are importing more discounted crude from Russia in recent years since sanctions prevent most Western countries from buying their oil. Until the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the Middle East had a clear structural advantage: giant fields, large export terminals, established term contracts, compatible crude qualities and proximity to Asian refining systems built around sour crude.

Now, after the lengthy supply disruption, many countries are looking at their crude import dependence on the Middle East as a liability. Some countries are taking advantage of temporary waivers and are buying more crude from sanctioned countries such as Russia. This is a short-term, opportunistic approach. Other importers, such as Japan are taking a more strategic view. They are looking to buy more crude from the Atlantic Basin (the U.S. Gulf, Brazil, Guyana and West Africa) as well as Canada. However, diversification is difficult. For example, U.S. crude involves longer lead times and higher freight costs, Panama Canal constraints, and a quality mismatch because Japanese refineries are often optimized for heavier, sour Middle Eastern grades rather than light sweet shale crude.

While the current crisis has shown that the Strait of Hormuz represents a real risk to global crude oil flows, the 15 Million barrels per day of crude oil from the Persian Gulf are hard to replace. The world needs those volumes. So, while the Middle East might lose some market share at the margin, the most realistic outcome is that high-income, security-sensitive importers such as Japan and Korea reduce exposure where they can, especially for spot and incremental barrels. Fast-growing, price-sensitive buyers such as India and parts of Southeast Asia will keep buying Middle Eastern crude when it is competitive but arbitrage more aggressively among Russia, the Americas and Africa. China may remain diversified by supplier, but its sheer scale keeps it central to Gulf exporters.

Middle Eastern exporters will respond. They are likely to defend market share through pricing, storage deals, and refinery and petrochemical investments in Asia. They will also consider using and building more bypass infrastructure. Saudi Arabia and the UAE already have pipeline routes that can avoid Hormuz, and both countries have announced investments to boost their capacity. Saudi Aramco wants to expand the loading capacity at Yanbu, the export terminal in the Red Sea, while the UAE is accelerating the construction of a new "West- East" pipeline to bypass the Strait of Hormuz, aiming for completion by 2027 to double export capacity via Fujairah.

In conclusion: diversification will not end the Middle East-Asia crude axis, but it will make the marginal barrel travel farther and raise demand for VLCCs and Suezmaxes. Freight markets should see a higher "security premium". Trade routes get less concentrated but not necessarily shorter or cheaper. If oil demand plateaus, ton-mile demand may still be supported by longer voyages. If demand keeps growing, the Middle East-Asia core remains huge while Atlantic-to-Asia flows grow around it.
