# The Art Of The Iran Nuclear Deal

### How would the tanker market respond to a new deal?

In recent days there has been increased chatter in the market about a possible new Iran nuclear deal. The most recent round of negotiations between the U.S. and Iran started in April after President Donald Trump sent a letter to Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, in which he expressed a desire to start new nuclear talks. Several meetings took place during April and May and during the most recent discussion, which concluded on Sunday, May 11, the U.S. presented the Iranian delegation with a written proposal. While it appears that the countries are getting closer to a deal, it could take a lot more time to negotiate the details. However, there is momentum towards an agreement currently. There are reports that senior diplomats from Iran and France, the U.K. and Germany are meeting in Istanbul today (Friday, May 16). This is important because, according to European diplomats, the three European powers have decided to trigger "snapback" sanctions under the 2015 nuclear deal if no new deal is reached by August. So, while these negotiations are ongoing, we want to take a look at what will happen to the tanker market if a deal is reached and sanctions on Iran are lifted. To get a sense of how the oil and tanker markets may respond, it is useful to take a look at what happened in 2015, when the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), the first "Iran Nuclear Deal" was concluded.

In order the compare and contrast the impact of the original Iran Nuclear Deal with the potential new one, let's go back to 2015 and highlight a few important factors to keep in mind. First of all, negotiating these agreements is complicated and takes time. Back in 2015, a framework agreement was reached on April 2nd, while a final deal was concluded on July 14th. The Iran Nuclear Deal was only implemented in January 2016, after the International Atomic Energy Agency verified that Iran had dismantled key nuclear infrastructure. Similarly, if an agreement on a new deal is reached, if could take six months or more before sanctions are lifted. Until that time, one would expect that Iran will continue to ship most of its crude to China using its vast network of dark fleet vessels. It is possible that in the period leading up to the lifting of sanctions, enforcement will be relaxed.

In the period leading up to the Iran Nuclear Deal in 2015, Iran accumulated a significant amount of oil in storage, both onshore and offshore. Back in early 2016, analysts estimated that Iran had 30-50 million barrels in floating storage, mostly concentrated on tankers floating in the Arabian Gulf. The current situation is different. The volume of oil in floating storage appears to be lower this time (20-30 million barrels), and it is not all concentrated in the region. In an effort to circumvent sanctions, Iran has oil in floating storage offshore Malaysia and Singapore. As of the start of 2025, Iran still had an estimated 25 million barrels of crude oil in bonded storage in China, which became stranded in 2018, after President Trump reimposed sanctions when he pulled out of the JCPOA. Both offshore and onshore barrels will be available for sale as soon as sanctions are lifted. Since much of this oil is located close to potential customers, the positive impact on the tanker market will be more muted.

One of the key drivers for the tanker market in the event of a new Iran Nuclear Deal is what will happen to the Iranian controlled tanker fleet as well as the broader dark fleet that has supported the sanctioned trades in the last 7 years. Back in 2016, a significant portion of the fleet owned by the National Iranian Tanker Company (NITC) was used for floating storage. After sanctions were lifted, these vessels gradually returned to the international market. Vetting and P&I issues took time to be resolved, and vessels required maintenance to be brought back up to international standards. We expect the situation to be similar this time around. The NITC fleet is old, not well maintained and lacks vetting approvals, limiting their trading flexibility. Modernizing their fleet will take time and money and Iran will likely have many competing priorities for its oil revenues if/when the precious dollars start flowing again.

One thing seems certain. Once international owners are comfortable again to load in Iran, the dark fleet will quickly be marginalized, in particular older VLCCs, which don't have many alternative employment opportunities. Even if it takes time for Iran to ramp up production and exports after the sanctions are lifted, the relegation of the dark fleet will provide an immediate boost to the tanker market, in particular the VLCCs.
