# Another One Bites The Dust

### Product carriers gain from another California refinery closure

Last year, we wrote a Tanker Opinion titled "Another Refinery Retires", triggered by the announcement of Phillips 66, the 4th largest refiner in the U.S., to cease crude oil processing operations at its 138,700 b/d dual-sited refinery in Los Angeles, California. Last month, another refinery closure was announced. Valero Energy said that it would cease operations at its 170,000 barrel per day (b/d) Benicia refinery in the San Francisco area. Just like Phillips 66 last year, Valero points to a challenging regulatory environment, high operating costs and new state legislation as reasons for their decision. This comes on top of limited prospects for oil demand growth on the U.S. West Coast (USWC). It appears that refining capacity on the USWC is closing faster than demand is declining, which can potentially boost refined product imports and improves the prospects for product tankers in the Pacific.

Despite its reputation for being one of United States' greenest states, California is still a fossil fuel powerhouse. It has the third largest refining capacity in the United States, after Texas and Louisiana. It is also the country's most populous state, making it the largest consumer of jet fuel and the second-largest user of gasoline in the U.S., despite having by far the largest penetration of electric vehicles in the country. The number of refineries in California has declined from around 50 a few decades ago to just 11 currently and (as mentioned above), several of these are under threat of closure.

There are a number of regulatory challenges facing refineries on the USWC, in particular in California. Historically, California has enacted some of the nation's strictest environmental regulations, which have significantly increased the cost and complexity of refinery operations in the state. In addition to the unique fuel blend requirements, the compliance standards in the "Golden State" make it very challenging for refineries to remain profitable. Things got worse in 2024, when Governor Newsom signed additional legislative measures targeting refiners. These laws will, if enacted: (1) Require refiners to maintain minimum stocks of gasoline and blending components; (2) Authorize the California Energy Commission (CEC) to set an "acceptable refining margin" and penalize companies that exceed it (i.e. price controls) and; (3) Mandate refiners to submit "resupply plans" 120 days before planned maintenance, subject to state approval, with non-compliance penalties ranging from $100,000 to $1 million per day. It is no surprise that some marginal refineries facing these new laws decide to close up shop.

It is important to remember that refining capacity on the USWC does not only service California, Oregon and Washington State.

California also delivers products to Arizona and Nevada, Western states that lack refining capacity. Refineries in the San Francisco area supply gasoline to Reno, NV via pipeline, while refineries in Los Angeles supply gasoline to Las Vegas, NV and Phoenix, AZ, also via pipeline. California supplies about 33% of the Arizona demand (the remainder comes from Texas) and 88% of the Nevada market. If refineries in California can no longer cover the demand in these states, that will add to USWC import demand.

Any shortfall of product on the USWC will lead to increased imports. The USWC is difficult to supply from other parts of the U.S. due to its unique fuel specifications, strict regulatory environment and the fact that there are no pipelines into California. Seaborne movements from the USG are time- consuming and expensive. Vessels need to pass through the Panama Canal and be Jones Act compliant, adding to the cost.

Imports have been growing for several years already. In recent weeks, imports have regularly exceeded 400,000 b/d. As can be seen from the charts above, most of the product is sourced from South Korea (42% in 2025 YTD), with India a distant second. In terms of tonnage employed, the vast majority of imports are done on Handy and MR tankers, with some employment of LR1s as well (in particular to import jet fuel from South Korea). Growing imports into USWC will therefore mostly benefit MRs, especially during the summer driving season, when gasoline is the main import.
