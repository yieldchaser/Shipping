# The Next African Giant

### A bright future for oil production and exports from Namibia

Africa has always been a prolific source of new oil discoveries. In West Africa, countries like Nigeria and Angola have been a mainstay of the international oil markets for decades. Others, like The Republic of the Congo, Ghana, Gabon, and Equatorial Guinea, are more recent additions to the energy landscape. Another country that has come up in conversations recently is Namibia. At the moment, Namibia, located on the west coast of Southern Africa, between Angola and South Africa, does not produce any oil, but a number of major offshore oil discoveries could change that over the next decade. In today's Weekly Tanker Opinion, we will take a closer look at this potential source of tanker demand in the future.

Millions of years ago, the continents of South America and Africa were joined as part of the supercontinent Gondwana. This explains the geological connection between South America and Africa and is a significant reason for the presence of oil deposits in both regions. The pre-salt geological formations found offshore Brazil (South America) and off the coast of West Africa (including Angola, Namibia, Gabon, and Congo) show strong similarities in their tectono-sedimentary sequences, reservoir rocks, source rocks, and trapping mechanisms. These hydrocarbon systems are essentially "mirror images" resulting from the two continents once being joined and then splitting apart during the breakup of Gondwana millions of years ago. The salt layer acts as an effective seal, preserving large oil and gas deposits beneath it in both regions. It is therefore no surprise that oil companies have discovered deposits in Namibia as well.

The most interesting fields are in the Orange Basin, where consortia led by TotalEnergies and Galp have made promising offshore discoveries. Galp is evaluating bids for a stake in its Mopane discovery, which generated considerable interest. TotalEnergies hopes to make a final investment decision on its Venus oilfield by early 2026. Both projects, if they are completed, will utilize FPSOs to bring the oil to market. While the companies have not published any detailed plans, independent analysts estimate that the Galp field could produce about 240,000 b/d using two FPSOs, while the Venus field will use a single FPSO pumping about 150,000 b/d. The Namibian government is eager for the companies to commit to the projects and hopes that they can see first oil by 2029. Success from these two fields will also stimulate other companies to keep drilling. If more projects are developed, production from offshore Namibia could exceed 700,000 b/d by the mid 2030s.

Just like most other countries in West Africa (with the exception of Nigeria with its giant Dangote refinery complex), it is expected that Namibia will export virtually all its crude oil production. This could lead to a recovery of West African crude oil exports and stimulate ton- mile demand for tankers. Depending on the size and ultimate capacity of FPSOs, this could benefit Suezmaxes (most likely) and possibly VLCCs, especially since most of the oil will likely end up in Asian refineries.
