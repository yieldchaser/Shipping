# Slow Boat To China

### Chinese imports from the U.S. picked up in recent months

Movements of U.S. crude oil to China have been volatile over the last 3-4 years. Chinese crude oil imports from the U.S. were slow to pick up after the U.S. lifted the ban on crude oil exports in December of 2015. Liftings of U.S. crude heading to China gradually picked up in 2017 and continued at a healthy level until mid- 2018, when the trade war heated up and crude oil exports got impacted by the eb and flow of trade negotiations. In January 2020, the U.S. and China agreed a trade deal that called for an increase of Chinese imports of energy products from the U.S. The global Covid-19 pandemic has affected global oil flows dramatically, including Chinese imports from the U.S. Crude oil exports from the U.S. to China declined dramatically in the first quarter but recovered in May and have remained high throughout the summer months. This week we will take a closer look at the dynamics of the crude oil trade between the U.S. Gulf and China.

Over the last couple of years, the U.S was the fastest growing oil producer and exporter, while China was the fastest growing consumer of oil, so it would seem logical that China was going to be a significant buyer of U.S. oil. There are two main factors that have limited the oil trade between the U.S. and China: Geopolitics and distance.

China, along with other Asian buyers started importing U.S. crude in 2017, when companies started 'experimenting' with reverse lightering of VLCCs. Previously, almost all U.S. crude export cargoes were shipped on smaller tankers and headed to Canada and Europe. From October 2017 to July 2018, an average of 5 VLCCs per month loaded in the U.S. Gulf for China. However, in July 2018, the U.S. imposed tariffs on a range of Chinese product imports valued at about $200 Bn, resulting in a halt of crude imports by China. Imports restarted in 2019 as the U.S. and China agreed on a 'cease fire' for 90 days and the implementation of tariffs was delayed allowing for trade negotiations to proceed. When progress on the negotiations stalled in August 2019, a range of tariff increases were announced by both sides, including 5% tariff on crude oil imports from the U.S., starting in September 2019. In January 2020, in a 'Phase 1' trade deal, China agreed to increase its purchases of U.S. products by $200 bn over 2 years, including $18.5bn of energy products in 2020 and $33.9 bn in 2021. Some of the higher volumes in recent months could have been related to a planned trade review meeting between the U.S. and China that was scheduled for mid-August but has since been postponed.

We believe a second reason for the volatility, especially relevant in 2020, is the distance and the freight rates. Phase 1 trade deal was signed, exports did not resume initially, probably partially due the Covid-19 crisis, which was raging in China at the time. For the first six months of 2020 almost no VLCCs loaded for China, even though China increased its purchases of crude oil from the Arabian Gulf and other areas to take advantage of the low crude prices due to the demand destruction in March and April. However, we believe that the high freight rates during this period made U.S. crude exports to China uncompetitive. The second graph, which compares the number of reported spot fixtures with the freight rates, illustrates this. When the freight rates collapsed in May 2020, the number of fixtures increased quickly as the freight of the long-haul voyage decreased from $8.00 per barrel in April to about $2.20 currently.

U.S. Gulf exports to China are expected to remain elevated for at least the coming weeks as another 10 VLCCs have already been fixed for the remainder of September and early October. After this, the volumes could be caught up in rhetoric related to the U.S. presidential elections in early November while next year's volumes could be affected by the outcome of these elections.
