# Supply Side Savior

### Can low ordering and increased scrapping revive the tanker market?

The tanker market appears to be in dire straits at the moment. Covid-19 continues unabated and is curtailing economic activity and global energy demand in the short-term. All the major forecasting agencies (IEA, EIA and OPEC) have reduced their oil demand outlook in their latest reports and new consensus is that petroleum demand will not return to 2019 levels until 2022 at the earliest. Some pundits suggest, demand will never return to these levels, i.e. 2019 was the peak. Over the long term, the need to combat climate change will limit future use of fossil fuels, with coal and oil the early victims, while there may be a role for natural gas as a transitional fuel. So, what is a shipowner to do? They are faced with an uncertain future and a dismal current market where tanker earnings are at or below operating expenses. Against this backdrop, it is understandable that most shipowners are not interested in ordering newbuildings, even though they are being offered at increasingly discounted prices. However, there is a potential silver lining. Can this lack of ordering in itself bring about a market turnaround, especially if it is combined with accelerated scrapping? Maybe, but a lot of pieces have to fall in place and it is by no means a sure thing. In this Weekly Opinion, we will explore the possibilities.

Let's start with the current situation (October 1, 2020). The orderbook as a percentage of the current fleet for each of the main tanker segments ranges from a low of 3.7% for the Panamax/LR1's to a high of 11% for the Aframax/LR2 and Suezmax segments. VLCC's (8.1%) and MR's (7.4%) are somewhere in the middle. Overall, these are relatively modest numbers. The low orderbook for Panamax/LR1 tankers is a reflection of the fact that this segment has fallen out of favor in recent years, in particular after the Panama Canal was expanded to accommodate Aframax and light- loaded Suezmax tankers.

Since the demand outlook for tankers is muted in the coming years, it is important to look at the age profile of the fleet to see how the orderbook compares with the pool of "scrapping candidates", i.e. the vessels that are 20 years old or older by the end of 2022 (by the time the current orderbook is delivered). This paints a reasonably optimistic picture. Take VLCCs for example. There are 67 VLCCs on order for delivery through 2022. By the end of 2022, some 120 VLCCs will be 20 years old or older. Vessels that are older than 20 years are more difficult to trade and could face significant idle time (especially if there are ample modern units available). Unless there are plenty of floating storage opportunities on offer, many of these vessels will likely be candidates for scrapping.

As can be seen in the table, the ratio of "20yr old / Orderbook" exceed 100% for every vessel segment, except for the coated Aframaxes (LR2's), which are a relatively modern tanker segment.

Obviously, scrapping is not automatic at a certain age. Elevated scrapping usually coincides with extended periods of low freight rates and a poor outlook. However, although rates are very low at the moment, they were very high not that long ago. Many shipowners made a lot of money at the end of 2019 and in the 1st half of this year. This means that their financial position remains comfortable and they are not forced into demolition sales. Therefore, owners have the financial ability to wait and see if there may be a winter market. If this typical seasonal uptick does not materialize due to Covid-19, we expect scrapping to pick up in 2021. Previous periods of low rates saw a significant increase in demolition. As recently as 2018, 33 VLCCs, 24 Suezmaxes, 39 Aframaxes and 35 product carriers of various sizes were scrapped. Similar numbers in 2021 would absorb most, if not all, of the scheduled deliveries for next year. If this happens against the backdrop of improving demand, this could balance the market toward the second half of the year. There is a lot of uncertainty and nobody knows how the Covid-19 pandemic will play out, but as long as ordering remains at current low levels, a supply driven recovery is a real possibility.
