# Katrina-Rita The One-Two Punch

Rita is expected to make landfall tomorrow morning in Texas and Louisiana delivering a one-two punch to the nation's most important oil producing and refining region. The impact of Katrina on oil production and refining is well- known; here we speculate on Rita's potential impact on U.S. Gulf production, refining, and tanker demand.

### U.S. Gulf Production Shut-in

Hurricane damage and precautionary platform and rig evacuations have reduced U.S. Gulf production to 7/8th of 1% of its pre-Katrina levels: 99.125% of Gulf production is shut-in. The highest operating level for U.S. Gulf platforms and rigs since Katrina hit on August 29th was only 44.2% of total pre-hurricane capacity.

### MMS/Poten

In short, Rita and Katrina's cumulative effect on Gulf production has already been severe. Time will tell the overall severity, but indications are that Gulf production will be significantly reduced for at least several weeks.

### Refinery Problems

Will refineries miss this crude supply shortfall? Current conservative estimates show pre-cautionary refinery shutdowns in Texas and Louisiana at 3.1 million bpd of refining capacity. These refineries, however, are better positioned geographically to return to full production quickly. So it appears that these refineries will need to find alternative crude supplies via the tanker market.

With LOOP and the port of Houston closed, further drawdowns may be necessary from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. LOOP was able to get back on the line within days of Katrina and perhaps this will happen again. If the worst of Rita misses Houston, the port also may be back in operation not long after Rita passes. Problems could arise, however, due to growing bottleneck of large tankers waiting to discharge not only in LOOP and Houston, but in other U.S. Gulf ports as well. ### Effects on VLCC Supply

Focusing on VLCCs, approximately 2 million bpd of crude cannot be discharged in the U.S. Gulf because of hurricane related delays. This means one VLCC per day must, in essence, make the transition from a means of transport to floating storage. A three week period will probably be necessary to fully restore U.S. Gulf refineries, ports, pipelines and infrastructure. The three week assumption includes the time some VLCCs have already spent waiting in the U.S. Gulf. This means that about 20 VLCCs will be acting as floating storage and each may have to wait up to 20 days before returning to load areas. If this is true, these vessels will miss their loading windows for their next cargoes and would need to be replaced by charterers given that Arabian Gulf production has not ceased. This rescheduling of liftings with nearly 5% of the fleet essentially out of commission waiting to discharge will, at least on a temporary basis, will force charterers to scramble for replacement tonnage. There is only one direction for rates when uncertainty, confusion, and reduced vessel availability reign- this is the result of the one-two punch delivered by Katrina and Rita.
