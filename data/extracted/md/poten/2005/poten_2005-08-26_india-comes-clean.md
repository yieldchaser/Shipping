# India Comes Clean

The opening of new refineries, and capacity additions at older refineries, has made India a net exporter of clean products; they were net importers as recently as 2003. Product tanker markets have benefited from this buildup as increased exports have helped to improve ton-mile demand for product tankers both discharging and loading in South Asia.

Below is an historical analysis of India's refining capacity. A combination of new refineries opening and several older refineries growing and modernizing, especially since 1998, has increased Indian refining capacity to more than 2 million barrels per day.

### Exports

Export volumes of clean products via the spot market have nearly tripled over the last five years, from a very minor 43,000 barrels per day in 2000 to about 110,000 thus far in 2005. Refinery expansions at Koyali, Mangalore, Mumbai, and Visakh, and most importantly the opening of the Jamnagar refinery, added more than 850,000 barrels per day to national refining capacity since 1999. These expansions have enabled India to meet its own domestic need for products and export product world-wide.

In addition to cargoes headed for Far Eastern countries and Singapore, India has been exporting a great deal of jet fuel to the UK/Continent region thus far in 2005. In 2000 India exported just 2,400 barrels per day of refined product to the North Sea; thus far in 2005 nearly 27,000 barrels per day have been loaded for this long-haul voyage. ### Imports

From 2000 to 2004 spot clean import volumes fell by nearly 60%, as refinery capacity additions meant that India was able to meet its own internal needs. The major source for these declining imports has been the Arabian Gulf, having accounted for 85% of Indian clean spot imports in 2004.

A lag between new higher spec requirements for domestic mogas and refineries' abilities to produce this new fuel type has reduced net exports thus far in 2005, however imports should fall off sharply from their yearto-date levels of nearly 99,000 barrels per day as Indian refineries finish the work necessary to produce this fuel type.

In addition to the clean exports that have been transported to the UK/Continent via spot tonnage, India is also sending volumes for discharge in the United States and the East coast of South America. These different and far-afield destinations for clean spot cargoes from India equate to greater ton-mile demand for the product tankers that move the cargo, a trend shown in the graph below.

Since 2000 ton-mile demand for clean cargoes moving both into and out of India on spot tonnage has exceeded 40 billion ton-miles every year, and shows no signs of slowing down any time soon: ton-mile demand for tankers moving clean cargoes into and out of India should remain high for at least the next several years.

### Conclusion

India's refinery situation has made the country a net exporter of clean products when price levels for these products are at or approaching all-time highs. In addition, product tanker demand has also increased, as more and more distant places demand India's clean product exports. Product tanker owners therefore benefit because of higher demand in the clean spot market for Indian exports, at the same time that those forward-thinking companies who built or upgraded refineries are being rewarded with more than adequate margins on these exported products. Given anticipation of continued long-term worldwide demand for clean products, there is every reason to believe that this model will be repeated in other places.
