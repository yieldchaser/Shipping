# The Bullwhip Effect

The bullwhip effect is a phenomenon noticed in supply chains where the failure of a buyer to communicate nothing but a spastic order now and then creates an increasing degree of disorder as orders cascade from the first tier supplier to second and third-tier suppliers, and beyond, increasing inventory throughout the chain. If the buyer communicates the actual production schedule to the suppliers, then the suppliers have the information necessary to adjust their production to actual demand, significantly reducing inventory. Thus information can make a market operate more smoothly. The bullwhip effect, or the absence of information, recently occurred in the product carrier market.

### Demand More Balanced

4/8 4/15 4/22 4/29 5/6 5/13 5/20 5/27 6/3 6/10 6/17 6/24 7/1 0 The premium for transatlantic trading persisted through May, attracting vessels out of the Caribbean to ballast over to Europe. This of course is the free market at work as owners changed employment patterns of their vessels to maximize returns. The premium for transatlantic trading persisted into June even though fixture activity was more in balance. The further weakening in Caribs rates in mid-June was partially caused by the prevalence of good weather that improved vessel productivity. Finally, at the end of June, the market reacted to an oversupply of tonnage dedicated to transatlantic trading with a surge both in fixture activity and rates in favor of Caribs upcoast, restoring freight rates to parity.

### The Market Really Works

The bullwhip effect has a way of working itself out even if it introduces a temporary imbalance in vessel deployment. Now that freight rates are back to parity, owners with vessels in the transatlantic trade may be tempted to enter the Caribbean trades after they discharge their cargoes if they feel the market may climb in the short term. Alternatively if they feel that the market may decline, they may prefer ballasting to Europe for a longer haul voyage.
