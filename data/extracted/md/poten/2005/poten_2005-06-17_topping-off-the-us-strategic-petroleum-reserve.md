# Topping Off the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) was established in December 1975 as a consequence of the Arab oil embargo of 1973. Its purpose was to have a 700 million barrel reserve of crude oil in the event the United States faced another embargo. The SPR received its first shipment in October 1977 after the salt dome storage facilities in the U.S. Gulf had been set up. As the following chart shows, it has taken 28 years for the SPR to reach its current near-full capacity.

It took 15 years for the SPR to reach 600 million barrels (1990) and remained near that level until President George W. Bush's election in 2000 when the fill resumed. The current fill rate of between 75,000 and 100,000 barrels per day will shortly cease when the SPR reaches 700 million, the limit set by Congress. However the fill could continue with Congressional approval as the total physical capacity of the SPR is 727 million barrels. Moreover it is possible that Congress could authorize the expansion of SPR capacity to 1 billion barrels, but not in the immediate future.

The SPR is theoretically for emergencies in the form of disruptions of crude imports in case of an embargo against the U.S., civil disturbances (Nigeria, Venezuela), and war (Kuwait, Iraq), although it can and has been used to dampen high crude oil prices.

U.S. commercial crude inventories held by the oil companies are theoretically another source of crude that could be used to handle potential disruptions in oil imports.

### U.S. Commercial Crude Oil Inventories

U.S. commercial crude inventories declined sharply during the Iranian Revolution in 1979 when Iranian supply to the world market was severely curtailed. This was unsettling to the oil companies and commercial inventories rose dramatically peaking in 1981. Since then crude inventories have been in a decline partly in response to a shift in attitudes towards inventory. Inventory was once considered a current asset on a balance sheet and it really didn't matter how much was held. Now it is considered a carrying cost and is minimized consistent with having enough for smooth operations. The recent run up in inventory is in response to fears of supply availability more than interruption.

With oil prices currently above $58 per barrel, any actual disruption would push oil prices into the stratosphere, which could be disastrous not only for the U.S. economy, but for the global community.

The current fill rate of around 75,000 bpd requires the dedicated service of about three Aframaxes for crude originating in Latin America. Even if the continuing rapid growth in U.S. dependence on non-domestic sources may prompt Congress to further expand the SPR, the impact of a stopping or continuing the fill of the SPR on tanker demand is minor.
