# KATRINA!

Hurricane Katrina's effect on New Orleans and surrounding areas has been likened to the 1906 San Francisco earthquake that essentially wiped out an entire city. Like Katrina's impact on New Orleans, much of the resulting damage to San Francisco was collateral in nature. The earthquake shattered buildings, but it also shattered manufactured gas and water pipelines. Fires started by the broken gas mains could not be put out because of the broken water mains. As a result, much of San Francisco burned to the ground. In a similar fashion, Katrina did not do a great deal of damage initially, but after it passed New Orleans, the water continued to rise through breached levees. Katrina's full fury was felt along the Mississippi coastline around Gulfport and Biloxi where a tsunami-like surge 20 or more feet high washed away everything up to one mile inland. As bad as the situation is in New Orleans, it might be worth remembering that a flattened and burned out San Francisco was rebuilt.

The economic repercussions are yet to be played out. The economy of this part of the United States is wrecked with hundreds of thousands losing all their possessions (homes, furnishings, automobiles) plus their jobs. But rebuilding can also be a spur to economic activity as was learned in the wake of Hurricane Andrew that left southern Florida in ruin. Economic recovery from World War Two made Europe and Japan greater economic powers than they were before the war.

But Katrina has had substantial impacts on the energy market that in turn may have long-lasting effects on the tanker market. Crude oil inventories were down to 321 million barrels as of August 26, off somewhat from the 2005 peak level of 334 million barrels in May of this year, but well above the year-earlier level of 287 million barrels. Gasoline inventories stood at 194 million barrels as of August 26, down from the peak level of 224 million barrels in February of this year and down from 207 million barrels a year earlier. However, with the summer gasoline seasonal peak coming to an end, the nation now faces the winter heating oil seasonal peak with a gasoil inventory of 133 million barrels, the peak level for this year, but up from 125 million barrels same time last year.

While the inventory situation doesn't look that bad on the surface, the overall problem extends far beyond inventory levels. The Plantation and Colonial pipelines that can move a maximum aggregate of 3 million bpd primarily from Texas to the middle Atlantic and Northeastern states were down due to power outages at the pumping stations, but are partly restored and may be back to 75% operation by sometime next week. After initially shutting down, the Louisiana Offshore Oil Platform (LOOP), which allows for about 1 million bpd of imports from ocean going tankers, is now reported operating at half its capacity. The Mississippi River was closed to commercial traffic until navigational aids can be replaced and its depth checked. Upstream terminals were damaged by Katrina's surge flowing up the Mississippi River. Initial surveys indicate little damage to the river channel and it is possible that limited daylight one-way traffic may resume shortly. Another ray of hope is that Capline, the major pipeline that feeds crude from the Gulf coast to Midwestern refineries, has restarted operations at a reduced flow rate.

More alarming for the long term is the reported 95% of offshore oil production and 88% of natural gas production that is off-line from pre-storm evacuations, with an estimated 1.4 million bpd of oil production shut in. Fifty-eight oil platforms and drilling rigs are reported damaged (the estimate keeps going up) with 30 rigs and platforms "missing"- either adrift or sunk with one found stranded on a beach. For the remaining rigs, how fast their production can be restored depends on their condition of the rigs, some may have been moved by Katrina, and the condition of the underwater pipelines connecting them to shore.

President Bush temporarily waived the Jones Act to allow foreign vessels to move cargoes from the U.S. Gulf to the Northeast at least for as long as the Colonial pipeline is down. At this point it appears that there is limited opportunities for foreign flag owners to take advantage of the waivers. The government has also waived certain environmental regulations on gasoline and diesel specifications to expand the potential supply of imports. President Bush has also opened up the Strategic Petroleum Reserve and at least two oil companies have requested crude oil.

### Tanker Market Implications

The rated capacity of U.S. refineries is approximately 15.6 million bpd. Katrina has demobilized an estimated 2 million bpd of refinery output, or about 13% of the nation's capacity. The crude oil tanker market in the short term is unlikely to be impacted as reduced refinery capacity cuts the demand for crude oil. The real impact is being felt in the product carrier market. One possible possible scenario is that the loss of refining capacity is 2 million bpd for two weeks, 1.5 million bpd for another two weeks and 1 million bpd for still another two weeks. With a 78% combined yield of gasoline and gasoil, this would imply the necessity to import an incremental 50 million barrels of gasoline and gasoil over this period. If we assume an average cargo of 250,000 barrels, then about 200 additional shipments, or about five additional cargoes per day will have to arrive in U.S. ports. The fact that Katrina has created a product not a crude problem has already been reflected in tanker rates where clean rates have jumped from about $17,000 per day last week to near $40,000 today.

### What's Next?

The government's relaxing of regulations on clean product specifications will have a marginal impact. The reopening of the Colonial pipeline removes the rationale for granting Jones Act waivers.

Ultimately it is up to the oil companies and tanker owners, not the government, to fix the gasoline and gasoil supply problems created by Katrina. Spot clean cargoes into and within U.S. waters will help alleviate supply shortfalls due to refinery closures. The sudden increase in demand for product imports should keep clean tanker rates and fixture activity high in the short-term. If history is any guide, expect the recovery to go faster than anticipated and for the tanker market to be back to "normal."

Our hearts and deepest sympathy goes out to those who have suffered personal losses during this tragedy.
