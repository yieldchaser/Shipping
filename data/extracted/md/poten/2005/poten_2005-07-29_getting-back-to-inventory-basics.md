# Getting Back to (Inventory) Basics?

In the good ol' days, the purpose of oil inventories was to cover contingencies and uncertainties. Greater amounts of inventory on hand meant greater coverage to mitigate the risk of supply interruptions. Since inventory was viewed as basically an insurance policy, "the more the better" drove inventory volumes. Both the volume of inventory and the days of inventory on hand have been in a general decline, reflecting a changing attitude towards inventory from that of an asset to a cost. If inventory is a current asset on a balance sheet, then it doesn't matter how much there is. If inventory is a carrying cost that absorbs cash that can be better deployed retiring debt or funding capital expenditures, then inventory should be minimized. "Just in Time" operations treat inventory as a cost that should be reduced to an absolute minimum. Also, historically oil has been in backwardation: the further- forward future prices of crude oil being less than the nearer-forward price. If it is cheaper to acquire oil through the exercise of a futures contract, what then is the purpose of holding inventory that generates a cost?

As the previous chart shows, there has been a recent run-up in crude oil inventories. If oil prices fall from present levels near $60 per barrel, oil companies could suffer a loss. However, with the crude oil market presently in contango (further-forward future prices are higher than nearer-forward), oil companies can hedge their exposure to higher priced inventory and possibly even profit by holding oil in storage. The question is whether industry attitudes towards inventory are reverting to the traditional hedge against a supply interruption or treating inventory as a potential source of profit.

Gasoline inventories have a much greater seasonal swing than crude oil as they are generally accumulated in the spring in anticipation of the peak summer driving season. Thus gasoline inventories in the United States are a buffer against seasonal demand. Gasoline inventories, averaging 210.3 million barrels since 1990, have not displayed the same decline as crude oil inventories. However, in terms of days of consumption in inventory, which has averaged 26.2 days, there has been a significant decline from around 31 days in the early 1990s to 23 days in recent years.

### Gasoil

Gasoil (diesel fuel and heating oil) also has substantial inventory swings in anticipation of the peak winter heating season. Similar to gasoline, gasoil inventories in the U.S. are primarily concerned with meeting seasonal needs.

Also similar to gasoline, overall inventory has remained about the same, averaging 121.7 million barrels since 1990. However, there has also been a significant decline in days of consumption in inventory from about 41 days in the early 1990s to 27 days in recent years for an overall average of 35.8 days.

### Jet Fuel

Jet fuel (kerosene) has shown the least change both in the volume of inventory and in the degree of decline in days of consumption in inventory. Average inventories have been 41 million barrels whereas days of consumption has declined from about 32 days in the early 1990s to a current level of about 25 days since the mid 1990s.

### So What's the Story?

There appears to be some reversion back to inventory as a means to mitigate the risk of supply interruptions and away from acting as a sink for corporate capital. Indeed a contango market permits oil companies to hedge against accumulating high priced crude oil if indeed the present run up in crude prices should falter. Moreover a contango market permits inventory accumulation to be used for making a profit by buying inventory today and locking in a profitable sale tomorrow.

Regardless of the reason, a buildup in inventory translates to more cargoes for tanker owners and to more vessel demand and that can only be good for business as long as the buildup lasts.
