# Breaking Into Brent

### What will the inclusion of WTI Midland into Brent Pricing mean for tankers?

Starting with the June 2023 contract, the price of Dated Brent will include WTI Midland, a US Gulf crude grade, next to the existing North Sea crudes. This means that for the first time in history the price of Dated Brent crude oil could be determined directly by crudes that are shipped into the area from across the Atlantic. Dated Brent is a physical marker, distinct from ICE Brent, which is a crude contract traded on the Intercontinental Exchange. This week we will take a look at the implications of this change for the tanker market.

The price of Brent is critically important in the global oil market as it is the most widely used benchmark crude oil and many other global crudes - as well as some refined products and a great deal of LNG - are priced as a spread to Brent. There are also a wide range of financial instruments linked to Dated and ICE Brent that are used to hedge oil and other commodity price exposure. Originally, light, sweet crude oil from the Brent oil field off the coast of Scotland was used to set the price of Brent. In the 2000's, as production and transactions of Brent crude oil gradually declined, new crude oil grades were added to maintain liquidity in physical transactions: Forties and Oseberg (added in 2002), Ekofisk (added in 2007) and Troll (added in 2018). As production and sales of these crudes continued to decline, Platts, which establishes and publishes the most widely used assessment of Dated Brent, started looking for alternative crude grades to add to the mix. The additional volumes need to be traded in sufficient volumes and the quality of the crude needs to be similar to the other crudes in the Brent basket. Dated Brent is priced on an FOB (Free on Board) basis in the North Sea and to be able to include crudes from different locations, Platts had to develop ways to blend the prices of the various grades.

Growing exports of crude oil from the US Gulf combined with the fact that WTI Midland is similar in quality to North Sea crudes, made this grade an attractive candidate to add to the dwindling Brent volumes. However, Platts needed to solve the location issue. While Dated Brent is priced FOB, WTI cargoes are priced CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) Rotterdam. To address the different basis, Platts developed a WTI Midland Freight Adjustment Factor. This means that the price of WTI Midland that will be used in the Brent basket, will be calculated by deducting the freight cost for a North Sea to Rotterdam voyage from the WTI Midland price in Rotterdam, so that the freight is equivalent to a cargo shipped from the North Sea. For this calculation, Platts is using the freight cost of a 700,000-barrel Aframax cargo.

Platts establishes the Brent price from transaction prices combined with bid and offer prices for physical crude oil transactions on its trading platform during a defined period at the end of the trading day (the so-called Window). Platts only includes Aframax cargoes into its pricing discovery process. That is why, a number of traders initially used Aframax tankers to ship US Gulf crude to the North Sea so their cargoes could be included in the Platts pricing window. As a result, Aframax tanker rates in the US Gulf jumped at the beginning of May (see Fig. 1), when the June contract, which included WTI Midland for the first time, started trading. Aframax rates had declined from previous highs, making them more competitive against larger vessels, and, when more Aframaxes left the Caribbean, TD9 rates increased dramatically. However, higher rates attracted more ships into the region and rates weakened again.

Oil companies and traders will quickly adjust to the new Brent pricing methodology, and we don't believe there will be a long-term impact on the tanker market. Flows will stabilize and trading patterns will adapt. VLCCs and Suezmaxes will continue to be active in these trades due to their superior economies of scale. However, for these cargoes to be considered in the Brent pricing window, they need to be transshipped from larger vessels to Aframaxes.

The WTI-Brent spread is an important indicator for U.S. East Coast refiners in their choice of crude, but it is too early to say how this spread will change and if it will impact Jones Act moves of crude oil from the U.S. Gulf.
