# Fuel For Thought

### Oil demand growth and refining expansion in 2018

As we are approaching the end of 2017, a year most tanker owners would probably rather forget as soon as possible, we want to look forward to 2018, in particular focusing on some of the key elements that will drive tanker demand: oil demand growth and refining expansion. How much more oil will the world need, where will they need it, who will supply the crude and where will it be refined? We already discussed in last week's opinion ("No Surprise From OPEC") that, as long as OPEC maintains its productions cuts, most of the incremental crude oil in 2018 will likely be light tight oil coming from the U.S.

In terms of refining capacity growth, there is always a mix of new greenfield refining projects on the one hand and upgrading capacity and efficiency improvements on the other hand. As a result of heightened environmental regulations and a sustained lower crude oil price environment (which led to a "lower forever" outlook of many people in the oil industry), most expansions involve upgrades and debottlenecking of existing facilities, rather than new refineries.

However, there are still a number of greenfield refineries that are expected to start operating in 2018. To name a few examples: In Vietnam, the 200,000 b/d Nghi Son refinery is getting ready to start up. Kuwait's national oil company KPC is a partner in the project and Kuwait will provide a significant portion of the crude for the refinery. Test cargoes started to arrive earlier this year. In Canada, the 50,000 b/d Sturgeon refinery in Redwater, Alberta will enter its first full year of operation. This is the first such refinery built in Canada in more than 30 years. The Sturgeon refinery is small and will use domestic crude and sell its products locally, so it has a minimal impact on trade-flows. Another new refinery which will have only a limited impact on crude oil and product tradeflows is the Star refinery that Azerbaijan's state oil company SOCAR is building in Turkey. Not surprisingly, this 200,000+ b/d refinery will mainly run Azeri crude, although it will also source crude from Russia and Kirkuk crude from Iraq. Most of the refined product output is expected to stay in Turkey. In Poland, Grupa LOTUS is upgrading/expanding their refinery in Gdansk. This will add 100-150,000 b/d of capacity in 2018. This will have an impact on tradeflows. It is a sophisticated coastal refinery and although Russian Urals remain a key feedstock, LOTUS also sources oil from the North Sea and the Middle East. Recently, it bought two cargoes of light sweet crude from the U.S. and before that, a cargo of Hibernia crude from Canada.

One would expect that the pace and the location of refinery investments will follow oil demand growth and this is generally the case. The majority of refinery investments in recent years and plans for the future are expected to take place in developing countries, but the level and timing of these investments is influenced by oil prices. According to data from OPEC's World Oil Outlook 2017, refining additions were relatively stable from 2013-2015 at 1.2-1.3 Mb/d per annum. In 2016, growth dropped sharply to 0.85 Mb/d as companies responded to the oil price drop and reduced cashflow with a cut in investments. There was a recovery in 2017 to 1.1 Mb/d. For 2018, OPEC expects that the world will add 0.8 Mb/d, followed by an expected bump of 1.7 Mb/d in 2019 from delayed projects. Unlike earlier years when expansion was concentrated in the Middle East and China, refining growth in 2018 appears to be more balanced throughout the world. Growth of only 0.1 Mb/d in the Middle East and China, with 0.2 Mb/d of expansion in the U.S. and Canada.

The implications for the tanker market are uncertain. Refiners seem to take a breather in 2018, before growth accelerates again in 2019. Some are also recalibrating their output to make sure they focus on the right products going forward, i.e. less fuel oil and more low sulphur products, like ULSD.
