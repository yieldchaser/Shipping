### The Biggest Losers

### Which tanker trades are not pulling their weight?

It is usually more pleasant to write about growing business prospects and new trade routes, but sometimes it makes sense to reflect on the other side of the coin. Highlighting some of the trades that have declined year-over-year can help explain the overall changes in tons and ton-miles as well as the (regional) shifts from one segment to the other. In this weekly opinion we will review some of these developments, look at the factors driving them and try to determine whether they are likely to be permanent or temporary.

In the first three months of 2017, crude oil movements from the Middle East to most Asian destinations declined relative to the same period last year. According to the APEX statistics of Lloyds' List Intelligence, less oil was transported to countries such as China, Japan and Singapore. The most obvious and likely explanation for this reduction in trade would be the OPEC production cuts which went into effect at the beginning of this year. However exports from the Middle East did not decrease, they increased (see table), which contradicts this explanation. It appears that less Middle Eastern exports moved to Asia and more to Europe and the Americas.

So far this year, India has bucked the declining trend in Asia. Volumes from the Persian Gulf to India were higher in the first quarter of 2017 compared to 2016. The International Energy Agency (IEA) forecasts that Indian oil demand will continue to increase throughout the year, which bodes well for overall crude oil imports. So far, this seems to have only benefited the VLCC segment, where volumes were up 44% in Q1 this year. Suezmax trades from the Middle East to India were actually down 34% in Q1. It is too early to say whether this shift away from Suezmaxes towards VLCCs is permanent, but it makes sense for Indian refiners to take advantage of the economies of scale of VLCCs if they can.

Suezmax tankers were also hit by the declines in some of the West African export trades. Export volumes to Asia, the Mediterranean and North West Europe were all down in Q1 of 2017 relative to the same period in 2016, but the biggest declines were in the trades from West Africa to the Americas (primarily the U.S. and Canada), which were down by 40%. Looking at this market in more detail, the destinations that markedly reduced imports from West Africa were the U.S. Gulf and Canada's east coast. U.S. east coast volumes were up, but not enough to compensate for the declines elsewhere. A key reason for the reductions in West African exports were the production disruptions in Nigeria, but there were also other reasons for the declines on some of the specific trade routes. Other losers so far this year have been the intra-Mediterranean oil trades. This has also hurt the Suezmax and to a lesser extent the Aframax segments. The declines in the Mediterranean are partly due to lower regional crude oil production, but there has also been a shift towards more imports from the Arabian Gulf, as Iran is regaining some of its market share in Southern Europe. The intra-regional trades will likely recover when Libyan exports return to normal. Additional volumes out of Ceyhan and the Black Sea will also help this market, while a return of Syrian exports is probably much longer away. A negative for VLCCs in the Mediterranean this year relative to last year is the reduction in exports coming from the Ain Sukna - Sidi Kerir pipeline. It is unclear if and/or when this trade will recover.

It is difficult to draw any major conclusions from the declining trades discussed above. Some trades are likely to come back quickly when production in certain areas of the world recovers. Other changes may be more permanent. In any case, oil tankers are the ultimate mobile assets and they will quickly move to where the business is. As long as the overall world demand for oil continues to grow, tankers will be needed to transport it.
