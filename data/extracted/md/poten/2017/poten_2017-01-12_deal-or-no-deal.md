# Deal ... Or No Deal

### A year after sanctions are lifted, uncertainty has increased

One year ago, the United Nations lifted its sanctions against Iran after the International Atomic Energy Agency verified that Iran complied with certain key requirements. Immediately following the U.N. decision, Iran started to grow its crude oil production and exports. While not everything is back to "normal", Iran has once again become an important player in the oil and tanker markets. But what will the future hold?

Iranian crude oil production quickly increased, from 2.91 million barrels per day (mb/d) in December 2015 to 3.72 mb/d in November 2016, an increase of 800,000 b/d. The volume and speed of Iran's production increases has surprised some pundits, who expected the country to struggle bringing production back quickly due to a maintenance backlog and a general lack of spare parts after years of sanctions.

In parallel to producing more oil, the National Iranian Oil Company (NIOC) also quickly ramped up crude oil exports (Figure 1). In 2015, Iranian crude oil exports averaged about 1.44 mb/d. About 75% of this crude was transported to China, India, South Korea and Japan. After the sanctions were lifted, Iranian exports expanded to include countries that were significant customers before the penalties were imposed. Destinations in the Mediterranean and in North West Europe made up 20% of the total in 2016 compared to only 12% in the prior year. In 2016, Iran restarted exports to Belgium, The Netherlands, France, Greece, Italy and Spain, among other countries.

In 2015, about 55% of Iran's crude oil exports were done on VLCCs, with 25% on Suezmaxes, and 19% on Afra's. The remainder were Panamax cargoes. The segment distribution did not change much after the sanctions were lifted in 2016. The VLCC share rose slightly (58%), as did the Suezmaxes (26%), while the Aframax participation was reduced to 15%.

Iran started 2016 with some 47 million barrels of crude oil and condensates in floating storage (Figure 2), mostly onboard vessels owned by their own National Iranian Tanker Company (NITC). During the first few months after the lifting of the sanctions, volumes of oil in floating storage actually increased. It reached a post-sanction peak of 51.6 million barrels in March, before starting a gradual decline at the end of May. We can only speculate about the exact reasons for the initial increases in floating storage (which seems counter-intuitive) but it probably had to do with some of the following reasons: (1) Foreign shipowners were reluctant to load Iranian crude because there were question marks around vessel insurance, the availability of letters of credit and a lack of dollar clearing: (2) Initially, there was significant uncertainty about P&I insurance cover; and (3) Iran needed some time to (re-) develop its customer base in Europe, where it lost market share to other producers.

In recent months, Iran's has accelerated the sale of oil from floating storage and last week the level was down to 26.3 million barrels, the lowest level since July 2014. It is believed that the new OPEC production agreement has contributed to the quickening pace of sales. Iran is exempt from the OPEC cuts and appears to have taken advantage of higher prices by selling more crude oil from storage.

One of the challenges still facing the Iranians is the limited trading flexibility of their fleet. Iranian vessels typically lack up to date Ship Inspection Reports (SIREs) and have therefore limited trading flexibility in the Atlantic Basin. Other tankers have maintenance issues and are in need of repairs or replacement. The NITC has started a fleet renewal and expansion program, although the benefits of this will take years to show. It recently ordered six MR product/chemical tankers at Hyundai and signed a joint venture agreement with Daewoo to build a shipyard in Iran.

The main uncertainty facing Iranian oil production and exports at this point seems to be the upcoming inauguration of President-elect Trump in the U.S. During the campaign, candidate Trump was a vocal opponent of the Iran nuclear deal (as are many of the Republicans in Congress). Nobody knows for sure what President Trump will say and do about the Iran agreement once in office, but it is safe to say that it is unlikely to be business as usual.
