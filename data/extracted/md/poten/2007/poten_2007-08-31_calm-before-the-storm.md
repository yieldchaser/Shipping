# Calm Before the Storm?

As this week marks the second anniversary of Hurricane Katrina, residents of the Gulf Coast can't help but remember how destructive Mother Nature can be at times. While there have been a fewer than average number of hurricanes this season so far, the recent destruction that Hurricane Dean wreaked also served as a reminder that the energy markets face numerous potential hazards when trying to meet customer demands. Refining operations in the US Gulf have faced other obstacles as well that have left some capacity offline and subjected the industry to heightened risk in the face of any storms. In addition, gasoline stock levels are lower than they were at this time last year, as shown in the chart below. Recent draws have pushed stocks lower by 6% since July alone, and levels seem to be headed toward the lows seen during the aftermath of the 2005 hurricane season.

### Caribbean Rates Today

Over the past few weeks the market for product carriers in the Caribbean has continued a weakening trend that started in the beginning of the summer. Gasoline stock building during June may have been a factor that curbed the need for incremental imports of clean products for the balance of the summer. The chart below shows time charter equivalents (TCEs) for Caribs-up spot voyages basis 38kt parcels for 2005, 2006 and 2007 thus far. After peaking at nearly $35,000 per day in the early spring, rates are at their lowest levels in more than two years (about $6,000 per day.)

It is interesting that the correlation between the rates in 2005 and those seen so far in 2007 has been roughly positive 60%, compared to a correlation of negative 33% to 2006. From a purely statistical point of view the rate pattern so far experienced this year suggests that there could be some significant upside potential. With an expected firming in vessel demand over the next few weeks to replace low inventories, this seems a likely path.

### Weathering the Storm

While it is challenging to predict the day-to-day weather, let alone the severity of the hurricane seasons, it is even more challenging to predict rates. One thing is fairly certain: the US is in a potentially precarious situation with respect to gasoline. The potential economic damage that a storm might cause this season would likely be amplified by the historically low stock levels available to the marketplace today. These factors would no doubt lead to a big upward tanker rate push in order to meet the needs caused by the disruption but also to meet continuing strong demand.
