# No Longer Kings of the Road

In a huge upset, it was recently announced that sales of Ford's dominant F-series truck lagged smaller, more fuel efficient cars like the Honda Civic and Accord, and the Toyota Camry and Corolla. In fact, the Ford F-series's fifth place showing in May 2008 US auto sales data marks the first time these trucks have failed to hold onto the top spot since June 2005 (when another truck briefly topped it). Ford sold just under 43,000 F-series trucks in May 2008, a 30% drop from May 2007.

The graph below shows US gasoline consumption since 2000. Though the overall trend has been increasing overall consumption, it is clear that change is coming. With gasoline at $4+/gallon, consumers' driving habits appear to be changing to more car pooling, public transportation, and of course the purchasing of more fuel efficient automobiles. The question is: how big of an impact will this vehicle sales shift actually have, and how will the tanker market be affected?

That supposition may only be half-right, however, if at all.

If we take data on the top 10 best selling autos in the US in May 2008, we calculate a weighted average fuel economy of 27.9 miles per gallon. Compare that figure with the weighted average fuel economy of 24.2 miles per gallon for the top selling cars from full-year 2006, and it is clear that fuel economy is a more important factor in auto sales than in previous years.

Further, reports indicate that auto sales in May 2008 were down 7.4% versus the prior year, to 1.4 million vehicles. If we assume that each vessel drives approximately 15,000 miles per year, indicative annual gasoline consumption for May 2008 vehicle purchases would be 752 million gallons, and 936 million gallons for May 2007 vehicle purchases. This 183 million gallon decline over a year certainly appears to be substantial.

If we apply this analysis to tanker demand effects, though, we see that 183 million gallons represents 4.3 million barrels, or about 14 MR gasoline cargoes per year. In short, this volume change only represents lower MR demand of about 1 vessel per month. In addition, it is possible that the lowered gasoline consumption could equate to lower domestic refinery output, rather than reduced imports, so the net impact could be even less than 1 MR cargo per month.

In fact, new auto sales may not actually replace other less efficient vehicles: they may represent new drivers, or may reflect the need to drive even more. So the net effect of the changing preference for more fuel economic vehicles may not actually bring down gasoline consumption in the short term.

The recent shift in consumer preferences towards more fuel efficient vehicles is a major development that should significantly impact gasoline consumption over the long term. Over the short term, however, the net effect is actually quite small, and even smaller for the tanker market: demand remains high.
