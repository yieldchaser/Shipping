# Bill Me Later

The past couple of weeks have been quite tumultuous in the United States financial markets. The wild ride was capped with the fire sale of a cornerstone American investment bank. Bear Stearns was salvaged with a $2 per share offer from JP Morgan giving the bank a total value of less than the current price for two modern VLCCs. Just a month ago the company had a market capitalization of nearly $10 billion. This event has sounded an alarm for the American financial markets drawing further attention to the ongoing credit crisis. The chart below shows the Standard & Poors 500 index over the past two months compared to the share price of Bear Stearns and clearly demonstrates how quickly equity prices can collapse.

### Can It Happen To Tankers?

The question is whether or not the tanker market could experience a similar liquidity crisis. The table below shows the size of the orderbook in the large tanker sectors and the approximate values at current price assessments. Based on today's prices the forward orderbook for delivery in 2009 and beyond has a value in excess of $110 billion. In simple terms, to take delivery shipowners will need to either draw on internal funds, or reach out to the banks or the investment community to raise the necessary cash.

The above figure of $110 billion excludes the financing needed for chemical carriers, dry bulk carriers, container ships, LNG and LPG tankers and the vast orderbook for the offshore sector. With a conservative 70%/30% debt to debt to equity financing structure, the implication is that tanker owners will need to tap into the markets for nearly $80 billion.

### Cash or Credit?

The tie between shipping and the investment community, and Wall Street in particular, has been growing stronger for the past several years. The coming months and years will be a test of this bond. Fortunately for shipping companies, the public finance markets and commercial banking sources have been willing providers of liquidity to shipowners via equity and/or debt financing.

The liquidity scare in the investment banks should serve as a reminder to shipping companies that an overreliance in credit can lead to some sticky situations. But with spot and term charter rates remaining fairly firm the odds of a Bear Stearns type plunge seem long indeed.
