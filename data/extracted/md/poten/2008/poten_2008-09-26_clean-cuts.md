# Clean Cuts

One market that has stood out rate-wise recently is the clean product trade in the East. While freight rates for the Aframaxes in the East trading dirty cargo saw a sharp decline recently, rates for the clean product trade appear to have been unaffected. The chart below shows the relationship on a time charter equivalent (TCE) basis for Aframaxes trading Arabian Gulf to Southeast Asia on 80kt and Long-Range 2 (LR2) product carriers trading Arabian Gulf - Far East on 75kt carrying clean products. In the past 12 months, both markets have tracked each other fairly closely in terms of earnings. Since the beginning of August however, dirty rates have averaged $27,000 per day while clean trading Aframax rates have averaged over $60,000 per day. Far Eastern refinery utilization is likely the root cause of the trend, but limited supply of large clean product carriers has created further upward pressure on rates.

### Get Dirty?

Ships that have coated cargo tanks have improved trading flexibility and can carry dirty cargoes as well as clean petroleum products. Shipowners can transition between clean and dirty trading, but cost and time associated with cleaning the cargo tanks must be warranted by higher earning potential. Transitioning into the naphtha trade (the primary cargo trading Arabian Gulf - Far East) is even more difficult, as charterers often require that the ship has traded at least three clean prior cargoes before loading this cargo.

Most Aframaxes trading worldwide are involved in dirty trading. Of the approximate 650 Aframaxes trading in the spot market, the data suggests that just over 15% of them have traded clean products at some point in 2008 to date. Based on reported spot market fixtures, only 85 ships trading spot have been dedicated exclusively to clean products. The chart below shows the total Aframax fleet and orderbook by build year and type. Twenty percent of the trading fleet is coated suggesting the preference has been to operate Aframaxes in the dirty trades.

### Easy Come, Easy Go

The ebb and flow of the clean product trade is driven by refinery runs and the price relationships between the markets. With demand for refined products in the Far East facing downward pressure, the region's refiners have reduced their runs. Under these circumstances, domestic naphtha production in the Far East also suffered thus leaving an opening for cheaper imports. Refiners in the Arabian Gulf fit the bill as sources of naphtha and their net long positions help to push naphtha cargoes into the Far East market.

With relatively limited tanker supply, any surge in demand usually contributes to a spike in freight rates. Perceived prolonged demand for clean-trading ships could create a potential oversupply however. Shipowners looking to make the switch should take warning; the seasonal change in Middle East demand could see lower Arabian Gulf refinery runs meaning fewer cargoes for the taking.
