# In Memoriam: Morris Louis Feder (1917 - 2008)

### In Memoriam: Morris Louis Feder

### (1917 - 2008)

Today's business world seems to be getting ever more competitive, litigious and fast-paced - the maritime industry is no exception. Given the changing climate for day-to-day activities, it is not surprising that business ethics are continually tested. This week saw the passing of a legend in the shipping industry, Morris Louis Feder. Mr. Feder touched the lives of many, as a friend and a mentor, and continuously exhibited several of the qualities - honesty, integrity, and fairness - that can sometimes be lost in the quest to make the next dollar.

So that Mr. Feder's ethics and stories will live on and serve as a lesson for all of us, we have included an abridged version of the emotionally moving eulogy recapping his extraordinary life: Morris [Louis Feder] was born in Czechoslovakia on March 28, 1917. Morris entered the world while Europe was in the midst of World War I. He was one of seven children...

From an early age, Morris was bright, enterprising, resourceful and respected. He began working for a Czech shoe company called Bata. The company was expanding all over the world and they asked Morris to be their representative in Singapore. On the eve of WWII he managed to reach Shanghai, which was truly a haven from the Nazi onslaught... [it] remained one of the rare free transit ports. 'Shanghai required neither visas nor police certificates. It did not ask for affidavits of health, nor proof of financial independence. There were no quotas.'

There was a Czech club in Shanghai where Morris was able to gather with his ex-countrymen and women, and it was there that Morris met a gorgeous young woman named Lucy. The two quickly fell in love, and together they dreamed of a time when they might make a family together.

After the war in 1947, Morris and Lucy each found their way to this great country. They arrived on these shores with nothing. It took years of struggle and exceptionally hard work for them to build a foundation for their new life together, but Morris and Lucy would not be deterred.

Morris got a job as a longshoreman. One day on the docks he met a landsman from Shanghai who recognized Morris as the man who had helped so many to find their way to freedom and safety. This man told Morris that he would help him leave the grueling work on the docks to a position in the front office of the shipping company. Who could have known that from that humble start, Morris would raise himself to the very top of the maritime shipping world and make friends and colleagues all over the world as he did?

In 1950 after a couple of years of hard work, Morris and Lucy finally had saved enough to be married in Brooklyn. They were devoted to each other. With Morris working extremely long hours to make it in the shipping business, it was left to Lucy to manage affairs at home. Morris and Lucy were blessed with two children, Harry and Karen.

When Karen was a young girl, they were away on a vacation. Morris was on the phone much of the time. Karen complained that her father was always so engrossed in his work. Lucy explained to her that this was his job and that because he did his job so well they had enough money to take nice vacations. So when her father finally got off the phone and rejoined his relaxing family, Karen told him to go make another call.

During Harry's and Karen's childhood Morris traveled frequently and worked long hours. Harry remembers that whenever one of Morris' ships was in a nearby port, whether it was New York, Brooklyn, Newark or Philadelphia, his dad would take him along to see the ship. The family even went on cruises on the Freighter, which was a thrill eating at the captain's table and seeing the inner workings of the maritime world.

Once Harry and Karen grew older, Lucy accompanied Morris on many of his business trips. She was a remarkable asset, charming business associates and placing foreign customers at ease. Together they were an unstoppable team.

In 1991, after 43 years of extraordinary leadership, Morris Feder retired as the Executive Vice President and Director for the Maritime Overseas Corporation.

Over the course of his career, Morris played an instrumental role in MOC's growth from an operation of 5 ships to one of the largest U.S.-owned shipping companies.

His entire career has been focused on the commercial aspects of MOC's business and since 1953, Morris has been the company's senior commercial representative in bulk shipping matters.

There was hardly a place Morris visited that he could not speak the language. He spoke Czech, Slovakian, German, Hungarian, Russian, Yiddish, Chinese and English.

One of the most emblematic stories of Morris' long and distinguished business career took place a few years back. Morris was acting as the United States government's negotiator with Russia over shipping the very large wheat deal. You'll remember that in July and August of 1972 the U.S. sold Russia about 440 million bushels of wheat for a total of 700 million dollars.

At one of the key negotiations, the Russian negotiators began speaking about confidential matters in Russian, assuming that no one from the U.S. side spoke Russian. Morris told them in perfect Russian that if they wanted to speak about important matters in Russian they ought to go outside.

The U.S. delegation was initially upset at Morris's impeccable ethics, but the Russians were so impressed with Morris' integrity that they came back and closed the deal, trusting this remarkably honest man. That was the kind of man Morris Feder was...

Talk to anyone in the maritime industry and you will hear over and over again how Morris Feder conducted his business--honestly and with integrity.

This past June, Morris was honored at the Silver Bell Awards Dinner. The Silver Bell Award is presented by the Seaman's Church Institute to recognize an outstanding individual's, company's or organization's commitment to merchant mariners and the issues facing the maritime industry.

At the dinner, Morris L. Feder received a Lifetime Achievement Award in recognition of his distinguished service to the maritime industry, as a respected international negotiator and a businessman of integrity as well as for his dedication to human dignity and the promises of globalization...

There can be no doubt what a towering giant Morris was in the maritime industry, but it was more than that. He was beloved by those he worked with. They trusted and respected this remarkable man.

In recent years he worked as a consultant in the shipping business. He even enjoyed investing in a number of Broadway shows...

When Lucy passed away in 1998, Morris's heart was surely broken. After all, they had shared so much over the years: enormous sadness and ineffable joy.

Morris never stopped being a devoted "grampa" to Jean, Laura, Ali and Emi. Living around the corner from Ali and Emi, he was able to share in so much of their lives. As a father-in-law to Gilli and Ross, Morris was a person they could always count on.

In this Feder family, there will be graduations and weddings in the coming months and years, and yes, Morris will be with you at these sacred milestones.

In conclusion, surely Walt Whitman's poem can help us mourn our "Skipper."

O Captain! my Captain! our fearful trip is done; The ship has weather'd every rack, the prize we sought is won; The port is near, the bells I hear, the people all exulting, While follow eyes the steady keel, the vessel grim and daring: But O heart! heart! heart!.... O Captain! my Captain! rise up and hear the bells; Rise up--for you the flag is flung--for you the bugle trills; For you bouquets and ribbon'd wreaths--for you the shores a-crowding; For you they call, the swaying mass, their eager faces turning; Here Captain! dear father!....

The ship is anchor'd safe and sound, its voyage closed and done; From fearful trip, the victor ship, comes in with object won;...

Whitman wrote these words as he was overcome with grief at the death of Abraham Lincoln in 1865. But we, too, have lost our captain. May we find comfort knowing that Morris' "ship is anchor'd safe and sound, its voyage closed and done; From fearful trip, the victor ship, comes in with object won;...".

Morris's life will always be a beacon of hope to all who face darkness and pain in the world. To his family, to his friends and to everyone who had the privilege of knowing him-he will never ever be forgotten...

His memory will never stop blessing us.

Rabbi Richard Jacobs Riverside Memorial Chapel January 13, 2008 New York City
