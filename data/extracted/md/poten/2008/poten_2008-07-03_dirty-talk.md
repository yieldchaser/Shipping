# Dirty Talk

Producers, traders, brokers, buyers, regulators and financiers gathered this June in Houston at Platts 5 th annual Bunker & Residual Fuel Oil Conference to hear other industry players' views of what the future holds for the "bottom of the barrel" oils. All of the presentations outlined the general notion that the fuel oil industry grapples with fast- changing developments such as heavier crude slates, refinery upgrades, decline in consumption by the electricity and manufacturing industries, and fuel oil quality modifications reflecting increasing environmental concerns.

To give some perspective on the direction marine fuel regulations are headed, Stefka Ilieva, a fuel oil analyst and editor of Poten's monthly publication Fuel Oil in World Markets, was invited as a panel speaker to share her views on the impact of the International Maritime Organization's (IMO) bunker fuel standards on bunker production as well as possible solutions. The following is an outline of the presentation given in Houston on June 24 th.

### IMO's Alphabet Soup

The Marine Environmental Protection Committee (MEPC), one of five IMO committees, introduced a maritime pollution convention - MARPOL back in the 1970's. MARPOL is currently comprised of six Annexes. Annex VI: Prevention of Air Pollution from Ships, is aimed at reducing harmful sulfur oxides emissions from ships by restricting the sulfur content of bunker fuels. It entered into force on May 19, 2005 by establishing a worldwide sulfur cap of 4.5 percent. The MEPC is meeting this October to possibly adopt further proposed global and SECA restrictions.

### The US Letter of the Law

For MARPOL Annex VI to become a reality at US ports, it has to become a US law. The closest the nation has come to enacting Annex VI as a law is the bill H.R. 802. H.R. 802 was approved by the House of Representatives in March 2007 and was passed by the US Senate on June 27 th, 2008 (several days following the Platts conference). The bill currently awaits presidential signature. If the President does not act on the bill by the end of the 110 th Congress in November 2008, the bill will be cleared from the books and a new bill would have to be proposed and ratified in 2009.

### Refiner's Incentive - $$$

One common misconception about the future marine fuel regulations is that refiners will increase their low-sulfur bunker fuel output because Annex VI regulations would create an increased demand for it. In reality the refiners would satisfy this demand only if it creates a sufficient economic incentive versus other refined products.

One way to satisfy Annex VI low-sulfur bunker fuel requirements is for refiners to install resid hydrogen desulfurizers. Depending on the price differential between high sulfur fuel oil (3.5%S max) and low-sulfur fuel oil (1%S max) there may or may not be an incentive to build a resid desulfurizer. Poten estimates that a differential supporting investment in a 40 kbd resid hydrogen desulfurizer in Northwest Europe is likely above $12/bbl over a 15-year project life. By comparison, Platts NW Europe FOB fuel oil 1.0%S - 3.5%S differential year-to-date is $8.23/bbl.

### No Quick Fix

The October 2008 meeting of the MEPC may show what the future holds for marine fuel quality. To speculate - by the time a global 0.5% sulfur bunker fuel restriction is imposed - unless exhaust scrubbers become prevalent, refiners will need price incentives to desulfurize. A combination of both may occur. One thing is for sure, future marine fuel availability and cost will continue to be a front-runner issue with which the industry will need to come to grips.
