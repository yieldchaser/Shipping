# Chinese Data Pressures Outlook

For the past ten years, China has been a reliable source of bullish macroeconomic indicators across many sectors, although China has not been completely insulated from worldwide economic slowdowns and recession. Even as Europe has slipped towards recession and the United States has produced progressively-weaker numbers, sustained Chinese growth had allowed global economists to maintain positive sentiment towards the region amid a two-tier global economy. Unfortunately, recent statistics out of China are looking worrisome.

Among the myriad of Chinese macro statistics, economists have favoured electricity generation as strong coincident indicator of industrial production that is open to limited manipulation. As shown in the above chart, the indicator is subject to considerable volatility in the months surrounding the Lunar New Year, and this year's earlier timing certainly distorted year-over-year (yoy) comparisons. Still, electricity generation for April was the first clean post-holiday data point and the results were not encouraging, recording a minor 1.5% yoy gain. This outcome seems more consistent with the HBSC Chinese Manufacturing PMI, which has remained below the 50 threshold that denotes manufacturing contraction. Meanwhile, other Chinese indicators are signalling a measurable slowdown. Following average 13-14% gains during 2011, Chinese industrial production in April recorded its lowest yoy gain since 2009, at 9.3%, while Chinese passenger car sales slipped to a 1.8% yoy decline in 1q12, the first such decline since 4q08.

These bearish macro indicators have extended to key oil statistics. For years, strong Chinese oil product demand growth has supported rapid increases in refining capacity and utilisation rates, leading to robust crude runs and crude import demand growth. As shown in the chart below, yoy growth in Chinese crude runs has slipped since mid-2011, falling from the 0.5 mbpd pace consistent with capacity growth, to an actual 58 kbpd yoy decline in April.

Moreover, government-controlled retail fuel prices, that limit Chinese refiners from passing on higher crude prices, also pressured runs. Chinese first quarter earnings reports revealed that Sinopec lost 9.2 billion yuan (US$1.46 billion) from its refining business, while smaller Petrochina lost 10.4 billion yuan (US$1.65 billion) from refining, given its higher dependence on light/sweet crudes. Large official price rises in mid-March offered some relief for Chinese refiners in April, allowing a small sequential rise in crude runs over March, but runs still remained muted. Meanwhile, this month's official price cuts will pressure margins, as will potential future cuts based upon the recent collapse in crude prices. Although Chinese refiners have maintained sufficient crude imports to support further stock builds, the uncertain refining outlook may be finally pressuring crude imports. After rising by 527 kbpd yoy during 1q12 , April crude imports posted a 176 kbpd yoy gain.

Although Chinese macroeconomic growth and oil demand could rebound, consistent with earlier reversals, the recent spate of weak Chinese data is troubling. Within the backdrop of a recessionary Europe and weakening US statistics, the current Chinese data flow is making a buoyant tanker demand outlook difficult to support for the moment.
