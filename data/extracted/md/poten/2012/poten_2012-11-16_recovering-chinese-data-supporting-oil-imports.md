# Recovering Chinese Data Supporting Oil Imports

### Improving Chinese economic stats during change in leadership

As China's new fifth generation leaders were announced to their public this week, a wave of encouraging assessments of the nation's coming economic health fortuitously seeped into the media. Late summer's unveiling of some $150 billion in rail and infrastructure project stimulus funding seems to be helping stabilize the economy. Recent data shows the year-over-year growth of China's economy as a meagre 7.4% during the third quarter. Whether last quarter's growth assessment embodies the view of some observers that the recent Chinese economic figures are "man-made" and they are for "reference only" remains to be seen, as does the breath of any recovery. After enduring a seven-quarter slowdown, the country's expectations for economic improvement, the new Communist Party leader Xi Jinping seems careful in managing expectations. As economic vitality returns to China, its need for oil imports also seems destined to expand.

Indications of an economy's reviving growth may also be gleaned from basic data reflecting its on-going activity. A nascent indication of a bottom being found in China's industrial activity was suggested by the HSBC Purchasing Managers' Index as October registered a modest pickup over September's assessment. Electricity power generation is another litmus gauge for the Chinese economy as about 70 % of its consumption originates in the industrial sector. Viewed from a year-on-year change perspective rather than a monthly one, some stabilization and even an improvement over 2011 generation levels are suggested since July 2012.

Increasing crude oil throughput rates lately in Chinese refineries, too, support the notion that the country's economy may have at least stabilized and that it also may even be improving. October's processing level recorded a high of 39.92 million tons. This quantity represents a 3% increase from September's crude oil run of 38.76 million tons. The imminent commissioning of expanded capacity at Petrochina's Hohhot and Sinopec's Jinling refineries will open room for 190 kbpd more crude oil processing this quarter.

China's crude oil imports have risen lately, with October's imports tallying 23.68 million tons, or an average of around 5.6 mbpd. This flow represents a rise of nearly 14% over October 2011's imports of 20.8 million tons and a jump of nearly 18% over September's level of 20.08 million tons. Prospects of China's stabilizing, and even possibly improving, economic health may hence be on the horizon. Owners of oil tankers - especially those operating VLCCs - might wonder how these hopeful signs will first trickle into the nation's thirst for imported crude oil and then translate into increasing vessel ton-mile demand.

The average of monthly VLCC reported spot market fixtures from the AG to the Far East during the first ten months of 2011 of 69 is comparable to the same period of 2012. As shown in the chart on the following page, in just the first half of this November, however, the number of reported spot fixtures has already exceeded those for the entire previous month.

If older processing capacity in other refineries remains in service, the implied increase in imports bodes favorably for an improvement in tanker ton-mile demand. Offsetting any enthusiasm on the part of China's refiners to raise crude oil processing, though, could be the recent trimming of margins brought about by a lowering of retail product prices. In light of the usual cross winds generated by the swirl of economic data, it seems prudent that those attempting to gauge the direction of China's economic growth manage their expectations very carefully.
