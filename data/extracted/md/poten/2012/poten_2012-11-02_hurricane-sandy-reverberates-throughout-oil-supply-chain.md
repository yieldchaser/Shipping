# Hurricane Sandy Reverberates throughout Oil Supply Chain

### Hurricane Sandy took a heavy toll on gasoline availability...

Hurricane Sandy captured headlines during the past week, and her effects will be felt throughout the Northeastern United States to varying degrees for the foreseeable future. Various media outlets note that drivers in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut have been forced to wait in lines of 100 cars or more to refill their automobiles as they attempt to return to their daily routines. The supply chain has been jolted elsewhere as well: prolonged delays and outages at pipelines, refineries, and terminals have lifted tanker rates both into the region and into areas that compete for tonnage with the region.

...but sagging regional demand and sufficient refining capacity will dull the impact Gasoline stocks at five year lows Power outages and flooding largely to blame for short-term issues with gas stations The hurricane's timing was particularly unfortunate because gasoline supply in the area is low. Stocks had been driven seasonally lower by the switching of grades of motor gasoline and regularly scheduled refinery maintenance. With regional demand down by 0.9% year over year, the finished product supply picture is not quite as dire, though, with approximately 15.6 days of stocks available.

These disruptions have in turn triggered delays for pipelines feeding into the region and left a significant amount of tonnage waiting to make deliveries into area ports.

Regulatory waivers have changed normal market dynamics, further complicating matters. In anticipation of constrained supplies and rising gas prices - less than a week before Election Day - the US Environmental Protection Agency has issued a temporary waiver enabling the sale of some grades of motor gasoline that would otherwise be prevented under the Clean Air Act. Furthermore, the US Department of Homeland Security has issued a blanket waiver of the Jones Act. This creates an option for tanker owners and operators to move tonnage that would have otherwise been bound for foreign ports out of USG to be directed to USAC instead, likely tightening supply for routes such as TC3 and TC14.

Between aforementioned pipeline delays and ships on charter that had to wait - or are still delayed - in order to make deliveries into ports and terminals impacted by Hurricane Sandy, the region that felt the brunt of her impact is not facing a crude supply shortage. On the contrary, bottlenecks are located at regional refineries and individual gas stations without power. These bottlenecks should be short-lived as refineries come back on line and power is restored within the region. Acutely elevated tanker rates for exports from the US Gulf and Caribbean regions in particular should likewise subside within a few weeks as regional pipelines, refineries, terminals, and ports return to normal utilization levels.
