# A Product Tanker Investment Hypothesis, Overextended

### IEA makes case for product tankers...

Today's release of the long-awaited Medium-term Oil Market Report (MTOMR) by the International Energy Agency (IEA) featured a more relaxed oil market forecast for the next five years -- driven by weaker global demand growth, stronger North American supply and rising Iraqi production capacity. The agency went to great lengths to dramatise a "deeply transformed" global oil map, as a rebalancing of regional supply and demand would drive crude trade flows lower, but boost longer-haul product movements. Of course, the market has embraced this product tanker investment hypothesis for years, and the fundamentals remain still intact, so this is hardly new news. Still, the IEA's assertion that China would become a "new powerhouse in product exports" may be a fanciful overextension of this hypothesis. .

...but China as "export powerhouse" is a stretch China and AG dominate refinery expansion plans 8.8 mbpd of refinery projects possible The expected rapid expansion of export-oriented refining capacity in the Middle East and India should drive longer-haul product flows and product tanker demand, but this premise has its limitations. Simply, the refining industry faces too much capacity.

With refiners contemplating as much as 16 mbpd of potential refinery projects, we estimate that approximately 8.8 mbpd of distillation capacity additions are likely during the 2013-17 five-year period, as shown in the above chart. As expected, China and the Middle East dominate the figures, although Russia and Latin America contribute to Atlantic Basin capacity, despite the delay of Brazilian projects. Still, against forecast demand growth of 5.8 mbpd during 2013-17, gross refinery capacity additions would overwhelm demand, suggesting additional refinery closures to balance supply and demand. Of course, this is the thrust of the product tanker investment hypothesis, that closures of non-competitive refining capacity in Europe and the Atlantic Basin would facilitate the flow of long-haul products from the AG and India.

This pattern of refinery growth would suggest additional declines in refinery utilisations, from 80-81% today, to 79% by the end of the period, which is not a sustainable equilibrium for the refining industry. The IEA acknowledges this in their report, but also emphasises a key constraint that Poten has asserted for some time -- that a rising portion of global liquid supply will bypass the refining system, limiting potential refinery inputs. The sharp rise in forecast NGL production, flowing directly into LPG supply or as a petrochemical feedstock, as well as increasing biofuel production, gas-to-liquids, direct crude burn and refinery processing gains, could meet as much as one third of incremental demand, according to the IEA. Poten estimates are lower, and imply cumulative crude inputs into refineries of 4.7 mbpd by 2017, leading to almost 2 mbpd of additional spare capacity.

Incredibly, this implies that refiners must shut down more than 4 mbpd of refining capacity during the next five years, just to keep utilisations at the lacklustre levels of today. Current Poten forecasts for 2.3 mbpd of refinery shutdowns during 2013-17 provide a 1.3 mbpd decline in European capacity, or 9%, and a 0.9 mbpd, or 12%, cut in OECD Pacific capacity during 2013-17. Powerful forces are driving OECD refining rationalisation -- declining demand, tighter environmental regulations, aging infrastructure, and newer, more complex and competitive refineries in non-OECD locations -- but these have limitations. During the 2011-12 Atlantic Basin refining purge, the market discovered that refining capacity is difficult to kill. In addition to local government interference, new investors emerged when refinery prices collapsed to more popular price points, allowing refineries once left for dead to re-emerge under new ownership.

### Chinese as pragmatists?

So, something has to give. If the argument that relentless refinery construction in non- OECD regions drives out older Atlantic Basin capacity has practical limitations, then someone has to stop building refineries. The Brazilians took pause when the costs of their 230 kbpd Abreu e Lima refinery project ballooned, but several players are committed to projects for strategic reasons. The Saudis and other producers are pursuing vertical integration into refining, while China has adhered to a long-standing commitment to building sufficient refining capacity to meet domestic product requirements.

Given the natural tension between the large Chinese refiners and the government price control and subsidy structure that they face, the Chinese seem poised to take a more pragmatic approach to refinery construction. Indeed, Chinese government regulators have been more reluctant to grant new project approvals and the refiners have scaled back their expansion plans.

So, the IEA arguing that China would become a "new powerhouse in product exports" was surprising. This assertion is based upon the agency's lower demand forecasts for China, which have sliced 2016 demand estimates by 1.14 mbpd from their June 2011 MTOMR. It also relies upon the assumption that the Chinese will continue to build refineries, regardless of the eroding global and domestic economics of additional refining capacity.

Build it and they will export Job as export powerhouse already taken, but...

...Chinese gasoline exports welcome No more MR ordering, please Conversely, the Chinese are more likely to maintain the product equilibrium shown in the chart on the previous page, in which Chinese refiners have been modest net exporters of transportation fuel for the past four years. Chinese distillation and upgrading capacity additions should continue to pace strong domestic mid-distillate demand, providing a gradual rise in gasoline surpluses. While AG and Indian refining capacity should help meet a widening European structural deficit in mid-distillates, local gasoline demand growth in those regions limit should export potential. China has a growing role in meeting an expanding gasoline deficit in non-OECD Asia --supporting intra-regional MR demand -- but calls for an export powerhouse are pre-mature.

The demand fundamentals supporting the product tanker investment hypothesis remain in place, but overextending this premise to new geographies, when the refining industry faces severe threats to profitability, is unsettling. The product tanker investment case faces its own potential threat from renewed MR ordering and "eco-design" marketing-- it does not need an additional ordering rationale from an illusory export potential.
