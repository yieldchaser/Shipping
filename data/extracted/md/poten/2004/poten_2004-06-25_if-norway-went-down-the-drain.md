# If Norway Went Down the Drain

Norway's oil workers threatened to strike this past week potentially knocking between 720,000 to 2.5 million barrels per day out of the current exports of 3.2 million bpd. Norway is the world's fourth largest crude oil exporter behind Saudi Arabia, Russia, and Iran. The Norwegian government was successful in ending the week-long strike by negotiating a settlement between employers, which include several oil majors, and workers. But what would have happened if Norway did stop exporting crude oil?

Saudi Arabia stepped up to the plate and said it would make up for Norway's waning exports, should the strike have occurred. But could that have happened and what affect would it have had on the tanker and oil markets?

Saudi Arabia exports 9.8 million bpd with a production capacity estimated to be as high as 12 million bpd. Saudi Arabia could feasibly fill the void of Norway's cessation of production. As seen in the graph on the next page, Saudi Arabia is already producing at historically high levels.

### Longer Voyage, More Ships, Higher Rates

The upshot of a cessation of exports from Norway would have to be a substitution by Saudi Arabian crude since Russia is producing at maximum capacity and Venezuela and Iran are close to maximum. Such an event would not only strain Saudi Arabia, but also would drain the VLCC fleet capacity and consequently drive rates to extreme highs. VLCCs would have to load in the Arabian Gulf and sail around the Cape of Good Hope instead of Aframaxes making the short run between the Norwegian North Sea terminals and the UKC. Assuming a 75 day round trip, taking into consideration some delays for weather and terminal availability, about 100 VLCCs would be needed per year to ship the Saudi Arabian crude in place of the 2.5 million barrels per day of Norwegian North Sea crude.

This represents almost 25% of the existing fleet of 432 vessels, which, to put it mildly, would exert some upward pressure on rates. As 2.5 million bpd would probably consume the entire spare capacity of Saudi Arabia, then one could also look to escalating oil prices to accompany increasing tanker rates. ### The Existing Situation

Not all North Sea oil is destined for Europe. For Aframax tankers fixed on the spot market, most are destined for other European ports with a small number fixed to the Mediterranean. (Note 2004 data has been annualized.)

### Aframax UKC Spot Fixtures

### Not An Oil Crisis, But a Tanker Crisis

Saudi spare capacity is about equal to that of one major oil producer such as Venezuela, Nigeria, or the Norwegian North Sea. If two major oil producers were to cease for a sustained period of time, the paramount problem would be lack of oil availability. But if, as in this case, the oil were replaceable, but trade routes were diverted from short haul UKC to long haul AG shipments, then the problem may become a shortage of spare tanker capacity to move the oil. With only one nation's oil strike, at the end of the day, this would not have been an oil crisis, but a tanker crisis.
