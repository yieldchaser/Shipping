# Reality Versus Perception: Oil Spills

Reality Versus Perception:

### Oil Spills

The general public's perception of oil spills is that tankers are a major source of pollution on the high seas, that their record, if anything, is worsening, and that little is being done to correct the situation. The irony and reality is that the greatest oil polluter is nature itself. Restricting our attention to the coastal waters surrounding North America, average annual releases of petroleum were 260,000 tons during the 1990s according to the National Research Council publication Oil in the Sea III: Inputs, Fates, and Effects. Of the 260,000 tons, 160,000 tons were natural petroleum seepages. As an aside, the initial discoveries of oil in western Pennsylvania and in the Baku region of the Caspian Sea were in areas where oil had seeped to the surface of the ground. Oil Creek in Pennsylvania received that name from its oily sheen, which predated the age of oil.

### National Research Council/Poten

In June, Dr. Henry S. Marcus, Professor of Marine Systems, Department of Ocean Engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology spoke about this phenomenon at the API Biennial Conference in a presentation entitled: Improving the Tanker Industry's Image. Oil spills from the extraction of oil (oil wells) total only 3,000 tons per year. The transportation of oil releases another 9,000 tons of oil into the environment with coastal tanker spills responsible for 5,000 tons and 2,000 tons each from pipelines and coastal facility spills. The largest source of manmade spills is from the consumption of oil. Of the 84,000 tons from this source, 54,000 tons are from land-based river runoff such as individuals dumping used lubricating oils in the local stream. Another large source is oil product fumes escaping into the atmosphere (21,000 tons), recreational marine vessel leakages (6,000 tons), jettisoned aircraft fuel (2,000 tons) and the remainder from non-tanker spills and operational discharges.

The International Tanker Owners Pollution Federation (ITOPF) provides interesting statistics on tanker spills on a global basis. The reality here is that the number of oil spills over 700 tons has been in a steady decline averaging 24.2 spills per year during the 1970s, 8.9 spills per year during the 1980s, and 7.3 spills per year during the 1990s. The decline in spills is impressive considering tankers have been carrying a greater number of cargoes in the 1990s compared to the 1980s. ### Reality

The reality is that the quantity of oil spilled has declined relative to the amount of oil transiting the world's seas.

### Perception

The public's perception is quite different. Between 1979 and 1990 the Atlantic Empress, Castillo de Bellver, and the ABT Summer each dumped more than 250,000 tons of crude into the waters yet public perception has been shaped primarily by three relatively smaller oil spills: Exxon Valdez in 1989 of 37,000 tons, Erika in 2000 of 20,000 tons, and the Prestige in 2002 of 77,000 tons.

The tanker industry has its work cut out for itself to improve its credibility with the public and make the public aware of the true situation. There has to be greater transparency of ownership and less tolerance for substandard vessels. With the new phase out of single-hull tankers about to take hold, the public should know that the problem of future spills is more related to performance standards and operating proficiency than technological improvements. Double-hull tankers cannot be relied upon to be a "Silver Bullet" that solves the problem. Mother Nature can spill as much as she'd like. She doesn't need a publicist to defend her actions.
