# Selling the Strategic Petroleum Reserve Make Any Sense or Cents?

### Selling the Strategic Petroleum Reserve

### Make Any Sense or Cents?

This week the US Senate voted to sell some of the oil from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve and use the money for homeland security programs. Members of Congress in both houses debated the issue and discussed whether oil in the ground is more important than cash in the bank. The current reserves hold 648 million barrels of oil in salt domes offshore from the US Gulf. The total capacity is 700 million barrels. What would happen if our oil imports from one source or another were cut off?

### US Top Importers

The listing on the next page contains America's top importers as of January 2004. The top two are non-OPEC producers (Mexico and Canada), which would be ranked as the most secure sources of imported oil. As OPEC promises to cut output beginning April 1, likely sending prices higher than the current $38/barrel mark, the US drawing down on its SPR inventory cuts imports and removes demand from the market.

### Growing U.S. Imports

The US has been experiencing growing oil consumption and declining domestic production resulting in expanding imports since the 1950s. The chart on the next page illustrates the growing gap between consumption and production since 1965. In recent years domestic consumption has more or less stabilized with growing production offshore US Gulf slightly more than compensating for falling Alaska and Lower 48 production. Thus the logic of the SPR to act as a strategic reserve in case of interruption of exports from the oil producers makes good sense.

000 Bpd Equivalent Days in SPR Saudi Arabia 1,432 453 Venezuela 1,362 476 Nigeria 932 695 Venezuela & Nigeria 2,294 282 Saudi Arabia & Venezuela 2,794 231 All Three Exporters 3,726 174 We estimate that the SPR fill rate of slightly over 100,000 bpd accounts for 10% of the average number of Aframax fixtures in the Caribbean. If the SPR were drawn down at 100,000 bpd, then this will cut effective imports by 200,000 bpd. Hence cessation of the fill and drawing down on the SPR will dampen demand for Aframaxes in the Caribbean and soften rates. But does it make sense to stop buying crude for the SPR and start selling it given the present world situation and the price of crude? As long as the exporters are exporting, and considering the softening impact of SPR crude on prices, it does make some sense to draw down on SPR crude, and at nearly $40 per barrel, it also makes some cents for a government in need of funds.
