# The Tourists Are Coming Can the Oil Be Far Behind?

In September 2003, the United Nations lifted its sanctions against Libya. Last month, the US lifted its 23-year-old travel ban on Libya and allowed four oil companies to negotiate business contracts in advance of possibly lifting trade sanctions. The US Congress is currently debating whether to reopen full trade between the two countries. Trade between Libya and the US has been illegal since 1986.

A US Congressional delegation recently visited Libya and received the promise of Col. Muammar el-Qaddafi - once considered a rogue leader - that Libya will keep a lid on the development of nuclear weapons as well as meet other conditions. Libya currently exports about 1.4 million bpd of crude. This is down from a peak of more than 3 million barrels in the early 1970s and it has been steady at this level for the past 15 years. The country hopes, with the help of outside investment, to reach an output of 2 million bpd by 2010. Does this mean tanker owners can look forward to increased demand for transportation as more crude oil and clean products are exported from Libya?

### Issue Before US Congress

Proposed legislation to reopen trade with Libya will go before the US Congress for a vote at the end of this month. Oil companies who had pre- sanction arrangements with Libya were granted permission last month to conduct contract negotiations. That initiative makes four companies eligible to return to Libya to negotiate business, specifically Marathon Oil, Amerada Hess, ConocoPhillips and Occidental Petroleum. Separately, some US oil companies operating in Libya signed "Standstill Agreements" with Libya's National Oil Company (NOC) in 1986, which allowed the state-run company to operate and develop the oil fields in the interim.

### Libyan Crude

For the past 15 years, Libya's crude has held a relatively strong position in the Mediterranean due to its light, sweet characteristics. Libya is rich in high-quality crude oil with API gravities between 36° and 44°. The crude comes from 12 major oil fields. Es Sider is the field credited with producing the sweetest crude with a gravity of around 37°. Last year the known fields had proven oil reserves of 36 billion barrels.

### Refineries and Port Facilities

Libya has two major export coastal refineries - Azzawiya Oil Refining and Ras Lanuf Oil and Gas Processing. These refineries have estimated total capacity of about 350,000 barrels per day. Libya receives tankers at Marsa el- Brega, Bouri, Es Sider, Marsa al Hagia, Ras Lanuf, Azzawiya and Zueitina. Ships of all sizes can be accommodated, although VLCCs are rarely fixed. The most commonly chartered ship size from Libyan ports is by far Aframaxes, though as trade grows, larger ships will be needed. ### Where is the Action?

Most Libyan crude is shipped to ports in Italy and Spain. In 2003, 36 or so different charterers fixed vessels from Libya. But a majority of the activity was accounted for by a select group, with the largest charterer being Tam Oil of Italy, which is owned by Libya-controlled Oilinvest. The chart below shows the 10 most active charterers fixing ships ex-Libya in 2003. Shell appears on the list with the majority of its cargoes going to the Mediterranean. It has more than one refinery in Germany and fixes ships to Italy to feed German refining via the Trans Alpine pipeline that begins in Trieste, Italy. Also ranking high on the chartering list, Italian refiner ERG processes Libyan crude at its Priolo Gargallo plant in Sicily.

### Where is the Future Trade?

There are signs that Libya's crude oil trade is expanding. As Libyan crude production increases, the nation will likely want to market its crude and will accept larger ships to carry it. Aframaxes have cornered this market, though Suezmaxes are increasingly being fixed out of Libya. India is a growing market for Libyan crude. In 2003, 14 Suezmaxes made voyages to India.

Libya has also reportedly signed a crude export contract with China's Sinopec. We expect this crude to be shipped on Suezmaxes. It is too early to tell how much crude will come to the US, but at a projected production capacity of 2.0 million barrels per day, expect this to occur sooner rather than later.
