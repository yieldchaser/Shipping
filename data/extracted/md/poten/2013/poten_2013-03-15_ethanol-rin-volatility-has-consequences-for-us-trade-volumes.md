# Ethanol RIN Volatility has Consequences for US Trade Volumes

Environmental credit “market” for ethanol production subject to fraud and volatility

### Credit prices have increased astronomically

The Renewable Identification Number (RIN) market administered by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has largely been a boondoggle from its inception. A RIN is a number assigned to individual batches of biofuel that are then used by regulated entities to prove that they are in compliance with the EPA’s Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) program. Companies that produce more RINs than they need can sell the credits to those that need to purchase them in order to be in compliance. Not unlike other purported market solutions for mandating increased renewable energy production, the tradable RIN market has been beset by wild volatility and frequent cases of fraud. EPA in fact estimated in 2012 that there were approximately 140 million illegitimate credits in circulation. Though falling off since, the credits reached a level earlier this week of over 1,490% above where they opened 2013 as some traders noted a potential RIN supply deficit in the next two years. These credits can be banked for a limited period of time after they are generated, which is reflected in the discount that has developed for those generated last year.

This is problematic as there is a “blend wall” that “Blend wall” is coupled dictates most gasoline can contain no more than 10% ethanol. The confluence of a rising with declining mogas renewable fuel standard, decreased domestic mogas consumption, and decreased demand domestic ethanol refining margins has already resulted in low availability of domestic ethanol.

Not only have imports of ethanol increased, but petroleum product exports may increase as well as refineries seek to avoid selling domestically where they have the added expense associated with the RFS. In any case, pricing dynamics will not be favorable to motorists in the United States. Domestic refineries, many of which are already squeezed, may look for markets elsewhere, and analysts at Credit Suisse note Artificially inflated prices that a price of $0.78 per RIN is enough to also deter gasoline imports into the United bad for mogas demand States. Unfortunately for the American consumer, the most obvious response to ensure in the long run sufficient production margins for refiners selling in the United States is to simply increase prices at the pump. Indeed, relevant gasoline futures have gained this year despite a corresponding decline in crude oil prices. This would drag further on already declining domestic mogas demand and could, more ominously, temper recent gains seen in consumer spending. South American ethanol producers may be poised in the short-term to take advantage of the self-inflicted problem in the United States. But while this is a positive development for ton-mile demand, the more likely long-term impact is simply higher mogas prices and decreased mogas demand if the policy is not remedied to avoid such ridiculous artificial price swings.
