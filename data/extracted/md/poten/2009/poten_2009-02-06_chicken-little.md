# Chicken Little

With the contraction of the global economy, the question on the minds of many is how it will affect the tanker market in the immediate term. Although tankers is still one of the most viable shipping sectors, the last few months have seen asset prices soften, newbuilding orders cease and freight rates return to long-term averages. In attempts to answer the question, we turn our attention to the physical crude tanker market to see what happened so far this year.

The graph below is a reflection of the past four months (October through January) of dirty fixture activity year-on-year for 2007-2008 and 2008-2009. Across the major dirty trading sectors, spot activity has decreased on a number of trades. Potential factors include the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) and Non-OPEC production cuts and more importantly decreased demand in the OECD countries. The VLCC sector was most noticeably affected showing a 13% decrease in volume in the comparable periods.

### All's Quiet on the Waterfront

The map below suggests generally less supply from the Arabian Gulf (AG), which saw a total decrease in spot fixture volume from October 2008 through January 2009 as compared to the previous year. VLCCs bore the weight of the decrease with a 67 fixture drop. Fewer reported VLCCs fixed to the East, however more Suezmaxes steamed for India. In the West Africa (WAF) region, VLCCs saw 12 fewer fixtures compared to last year while Suezmaxes saw a 7 fixture gain. Movements on Suezmaxes to South Asia have increased, and as we identified in another Tanker Opinion, Workhorses of West Africa, demand for VLCCs seems less as charterers opt for the simplicity of million barrel parcels.

Turning to the Aframax market, the UK Continent load zone in October 2008 - January 2009 was down 47 fixtures from the previous year. Much of the loss was sustained by the cross-UKC with 36 fewer fixtures. Fundamentally softening demand could have been compounded by refinery outages and strikes. In the United States, namely the US Gulf, there was notably less lightering activity, most likely a result of the crude oil contango and storage. Lighterings slid down a steep slope from 20 to 6 reported spot fixtures reflecting about a 70% drop in activity.

### Month-on-Month

While most signs point to a slowdown, it should be noted that December 2008 alone saw increased activity versus December 2007. VLCCs, Suezmaxes and Aframaxes saw gains of 65, 70 and 75 reported spot fixtures respectively on last year's December totals. For now, it appears that VLCCs and Aframaxes have sustained the biggest losses in activity while Suezmaxes show promise, potentially as a result of developing trades.

Perhaps it is too soon to tell if the lighter fixture activity is here to stay, as the month on month variation can swing greatly. As psychology can often override fundamentals, the tanker market can change on a dime. Shippers and charterers alike should hope for the recovery of spot tanker activity as it will be a transparent metric of the road to economic recovery.
