# Polar Express

Last month, retreating sea ice allowed two multipurpose heavy‐lift project carriers to complete a voyage through the Northeast Passage from South Korea, around the Russian Arctic, and on to Rotterdam. Beluga Shipping, the German company whose vessels completed the trip, called it the first non‐Russian commercial voyage through the route. Upon the trip's completion, some commentators heralded the opened sea lane as a global warming's "silver lining." While news of the recent transit is newsworthy, claims that the Northeast Passage will transform trade routes in the near term are overblown. Complexities associated with Arctic navigation, including hull‐type and draft restrictions, added costs and unpredictability are likely to limit use of the route for the foreseeable future. Over the long term, however, the opening of the Northeast Passage may help shape future trade flows. As far as tankers are concerned, the location of oil is the key element.

The ice‐classed 12,744 dwt Beluga Fraternity and Beluga Foresight were carrying power plant equipment when they departed from Ulsan, South Korea in late July. After entering the passage's eastern end near Vladivostok, Russia on August 21, the vessels were escorted by Russian nuclear‐powered icebreakers through the Vilkizki Strait, considered the most treacherous leg of the journey.

The traditional route takes ships through the South China Sea, past Singapore, around India and through the Suez Canal.

Earlier this summer, Norway's foreign minister told an audience in England that the "pirate‐free" route would cut tanker transit times between Europe and Asia by 40 percent within a decade, fueling speculation that the Northeast Passage could emerge as a summertime competitor to the Suez Canal. The fundamental forces that shape tanker trade flows, however, do not currently support a significant role for this newly‐traversable route. Very little oil moves from Japan to Europe, and exports of North Sea oil have been on the decline for many years. Also, the Northeast Passage offers no logistical benefit for tankers loading in Saudi Arabia and other key oil producing areas such as countries in West Africa.

Shorter transit time and reduced fuel consumption would certainly offer some savings for shippers of finished goods using the Northeast Passage alternative, but there are a number of extra costs likely to accompany the voyage which might eliminate the promised savings. Vessels do need to be ice class in order to use the Northeast Passage, adding an estimated 20 percent to the cost of any newbuilding vessel. Absent a dramatic increase in the speed of the ice's recession, even ice class vessels transiting the Passage route will need to be escorted by ice‐breaking vessels. The charges for these services are likely to be subject to vessel availability, threatening further delays that could use money saved by using the shorter route.

The need for ice breaker accompaniment also underscores the difficulty of navigating through the Northeast Passage, another factor likely to limit its attractiveness to tankers looking to travel from Asia to Europe. Even when the ice has retreated enough to make transiting the Passage possible, significant quantities of sea ice remain to pose ever‐present threats. The depth of the Northeast Passage has been estimated at 15 meters, limiting the size of ships that could potentially use the route and further complicating hopes of a summertime sea highway at the top of the world.

Ice delays, unforeseen territorial and diplomatic hurdles as well as having to wait for the formulation of a convoy for an ice breaker to escort would all complicate efforts to plan an efficient shipping schedule. Without the ability for such planning, no significant traffic volumes will likely make their way to the Northeast Passage in the near future. An additional layer of uncertainty is added when considering the shaky understanding of the scientific forces behind climate change. This uncertainty is demonstrated by the fact that scientists report that the retreat of the Arctic Sea ice surrounding the passage this summer was not as severe as it had been in the previous two years.

The future development of the Arctic region's resources and the likely creation of new ports and infrastructure, however, will certainly impact the tanker industry over the long‐term. The U.S. Geological Survey estimated in 2008 that the region holds 90 billion barrels of oil, 1.6 trillion cubic feet of natural gas and 44 billion barrels of natural gas liquids.

There may be a time when a constellation of circumstances align to make the Northeast Passage an efficient route for a tanker with a very specific cargo and positioning, but it is unlikely that many owners will react to the recent news by jumping to on the ice class bandwagon.

The tanker industry would nonetheless be well‐served to follow developments surrounding the Northeast Passage closely. The passage's transit underscores the need for the flexibility that is characteristic of the industry. It is also a reminder that as more of the word's oil is being sourced from harder to reach regions, and hence tankers are likely to be forced out into uncharted waters. Don't break out the ice scrappers just yet!
