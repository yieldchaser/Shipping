# Light At the End of the Tunnel

### Short‐Term  Energy  and Summer Fuels Outlook

Light At the End of the Tunnel EIA Short-Term Energy and Summer Fuels Outlook The US Energy Information Agency (EIA) has just released the Short‐Term Energy and Summer Fuels Outlook providing a taste of what can be expected through 2010; lower crude oil demand and lower petroleum prices in the short term until the global economic engine kicks back into gear in 2010.

A year ago, the tanker market was on the verge of experiencing one of the most volatile summers in history, crude oil prices were about to embark on a major climb and the effects of the Beijing Olympics on oil demand were being hotly debated. A year later, with the memories of robust times fading, the EIA is predicting the West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil price to average $54/bbl this summer as compared to its forecast for last summer of $97/bbl. In reality, the predictions of prices are largely irrelevant, what matters most for the tanker market is the direction and magnitude of the change in demand.

China US Other World The EIA projects crude oil consumption in the Organization for Economic Co‐Operation and Development (OECD) countries to fall by 1.6 million barrels per day (bbls/day) while Non‐ OECD growth is predicted to increase by 270,000 bbls/day - yielding a net decrease in demand of roughly 1.3 million bbls/day.

By the end of 2009 and into the first quarter of 2010, the EIA is expecting economic recovery pushing world crude oil consumption up by 1.1 million bbls/day from 2009's projected level of 84 million bbls/day.

The EIA outlook throughout 2009 logically points out the shift in crude oil production from the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) to Non‐OPEC crude oil supply. According to the EIA, OPEC production was down 1.1 million bbls/day in the fourth quarter of 2008 and another 2.1 million bbls/day in the first quarter of 2009 suppressing total OPEC output to 28.5 million bbls/day. In fact, roughly 1.5 million bbls/day have noticeably been absent from reported spot VLCC liftings in the Arabian Gulf, OPEC's main exporting region, from October 2008 through the end of March 2009.

While OPEC has been cutting production, the US, Brazil and other Non‐OPEC producers have been maintaining and in some cases increasing production from 2008. Notably, the US will actually increase production by an estimated 440,000 bbls/day in 2009 due to major contributors in the Gulf of Mexico - the Thunder Horse platform and the commencement of operation of the Tahiti platform. This will be the first increase in US crude oil production since 1991.

### Blinded by the Light

The EIA projects that global demand for crude oil will continue to be soft until the economic recovery in 2010 reinvigorates worldwide industrial growth and consumer spending. Although the outlook provides plenty of food for thought, actions at the pump will ultimately speak louder than words.
