## Market Commentary

Coaster owners push rates much higher on bunker hype, but downward correction looms amid  unreadiness of export markets

For the second week in a row, small-tonnage shipowners have been aggressively pushing for higher rates in the Black and Mediterranean Seas, trying to squeeze the most out of the hype surrounding the increase in bunker prices. “Seems like shipowners bet everything on zero, feeling that this is the only chance to earn, but regretfully for them, most experienced charterers are abreast of shipping arithmetics and completely understand what really happens,” a Turkish broker commented to ISM sarcastically. Indeed, owners are now voicing rates incredibly surpassing levels seen a couple of weeks ago (+$10-15/t on average), while the $350/t increase in MGO and a $200/t upturn in VLSFO prices are actually offset by a $5-7/t increase in long-haul rates and a $3-4/t growth in the segment for short-haul trips (and by just $2- 3/t in the case of shipments within the non-SECA zone, i.e. within the Black Sea or from the Black Sea to Marmara). Also, last week we were witnessing a clear trend towards the decline in tonnage list and could talk about an improvement in market conditions, but the regional vessel count has now increased again (by about 20% in the 2-4k dwt segment and within 10% in larger tonnage groups), while “firm” cargoes are becoming increasingly scarce. “It seems like WMed is really coming down, we see a lot of spot/ppt ships and several are willing to ballast, despite the bunker prices; EMed and BlSea are still tight, but from the cargo side with these prices not much is happening,” an EU broker commented. Nevertheless, despite the huge

bid/offer gap, owners have managed to raise rates by another $2-5/t this week, playing on the panic mood of desperate charterers. “Most grain charterers avoid fixing at such high rates and prefer to wait and see, but those deals that have finally been fixed are agreed at much higher rates,” a Ukrainian grain charterer mentioned. At the moment, most owners are seeking to get low-mid $40s/t for shipment of 5-7,000 t of corn or soybeans from POC to EgyptMed or Lebanon, while most charterers are not ready to pay more than $36-37/t. According to ISM estimates, TCEs for 5-12k dwt fleet have grown by 10-15% over two weeks, meaning that owners have managed to benefit from the rise in bunker prices (at least for now, since there is still no roof for bunkers), although only those who have succeeded to fix their rates, as most are still just voicing ideas.

In the Azov Sea, owners of sea-river tonnage have also managed to raise rates, despite widespread predictions of a downturn on the local market. In addition to bunker prices, the shortage of available vessels in this region is still having its impact due to the still significant downtime in Kerch. Freight rates have added another $3-5/t by the end of the week. For instance, a couple of deals for transportation of 3,000 t of wheat from Rostov to Marmara have been agreed at $54-55/t. Note that many sea-river owners now prefer to compete for cargo from Russian seaports (steel, coal, fertilizers), realizing the opportunity to earn more.

### and dynamics on major routes

Shipowners are also trying to push rates up in North Europe, but Baltic Sea cargo traffic is frankly weak, while spot tonnage list has even increased slightly. Players agree that the Baltic market has been significantly overheated over the past month, so in the current situation, owners will be lucky to achieve the same TCEs as before. Ice conditions are also gradually improving, and the hype surrounding ice- class vessels is slowly subsiding. The Continent market looks a little healthier, with even some decline in local vessel count. Shipowners have managed to raise rates by €0.5-2/t, balancing on the same TCEs as a couple of weeks ago.

Despite the resilience and intractability of shipowners, there is virtually no doubt that rates will fall in the Mediterranean and especially the Black Sea by the end of March. Unfortunately for shipowners, they will hardly manage to outmaneuver market conditions: their intransigence will lead to an increase in traders' logistics costs and make it impossible to maintain maritime trade, resulting in decreased cargo offer and, accordingly, tonnage demand, followed by an exacerbation of tonnage surplus, increased competition among owners, and as a result, the freight market softening. In this case, shipowners can only hope for an upturn in demand from importers of major cargoes (grain, steel products, fertilizers), but so far there are no signs of this happening.
