## Market Commentary

Tonnage outflow eases pressure on Black Sea coaster market as Azov sees a surprising upturn

The Black Sea and Mediterranean small-tonnage shipping markets are still seeing poor cargo traffic; the only notable activity is referred to fairly brisk shipments of steel scrap from EU ports to Turkey, as well as a slight upturn in fertilizer traffic from North Africa. Despite this, the number of available vessels has decreased significantly during the week. Brokers attribute this to a considerable ballaster outflow to neighboring regions (primarily to North Europe) ahead of the extended holidays in Muslim countries in late May. According to ISM, this situation is partly caused by worsening delays at certain specific terminals (for example, in Nemrut, where more than seven coastal vessels laden with scrap have been waiting for over a week to berth for discharge at metallurgical terminals). “It seems many owners are tired of this downward pressure on rates and are trying to 'hide' their vessels to break the trend,” a Turkish broker shares his opinion. In any case, the number of vessels available for May dates has decreased by more than 25% (from 236 to 171), which particularly applies to 2-4k and 8-12k dwt ships open in the Black Sea, the Marmara Sea, and the eastern Mediterranean, where the number has halved. Under these circumstances, owners have managed to stabilize rates in the Black Sea during the week, although there is still no mention of any increase. Thus, shipment of 5-6,000 t of corn or soybeans from POC to Marmara still costs $26-27/t, while rates for transportation of 5-6,000 t of wheat from CVB to the Italian Adriatic are still hovering at low-$30s/t. In the Mediterranean Sea, freights have dropped by another $0.5-1.5/t. For instance, shipment of 5-6,000 t of urea from EgyptMed to SpanMed already costs $40-41/t.

The market in North Europe continues to show negative trends. Thus, the vessel count keeps growing in the Baltic Sea, while fresh cargo requests remain scarce. We can only note an uptick in shipments of larger cargo lots of 8,000 t or more, whereas demand for vessels of 2-4k dwt remains extremely weak. In the Continent, activity still appears to be slightly higher than in the Baltic Sea. However, pressure on rates persists across all of North Europe. By the end of the week, freights have declined by €0.5-2/t.

Against a generally lackluster tone, the Azov sea- river market has been the only factor that caused the coaster shipping segment to perk up, surprising everyone with its incredible volatility. While Azov Sea rates continued to decline as usual at the start of the week (which, in fact, has been happening for several weeks in a row), the situation changed dramatically by midweek. Amid rumors that Turkey would impose import restrictions on wheat starting June 1, many traders have stepped up shipments with 2H May laycans, which led to increased tonnage demand. Therefore, many owners are feeling bullish again. For comparison: while deals for wheat transportation from Rostov/Azov to Marmara were signed at $31-32/t earlier in the week, by Thursday some charterers have reportedly agreed to pay as much as $37-38/t. The Caspian Sea freight market also continues to strengthen slowly: rates have risen by $1-3/t due to lively grain shipments from Russia to Iran.

According to ISM, a more likely scenario is that rates will stabilize, with a decline in bunker prices being the only thing that could help owners achieve higher TCEs.

In North Europe, ISM sources expect a further activity decline, so it is quite possible that rates will continue sliding down. Meanwhile, it is difficult to predict further developments in the Azov Sea, but it seems that the current spike is positional, and further rate growth is unlikely.
