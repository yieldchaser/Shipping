# Weekly Market Report

## Market Insight
*By Nikos Tagoulis, Senior Analyst*

Amid broader uncertainty in the LNG market, the outlook is being shaped by disruptions to Middle Eastern flows, an uncertain Qatari recovery, and Europe's historically low gas inventories, which are together redefining the market's approach to flexible supply this year. As Europe and Asian importers compete for the same pool of US LNG exports, routes offering reliable feedgas and shorter, more flexible delivery options are becoming increasingly valuable. Against this backdrop, a modest single-train terminal in West Mexico, outside Ensenada, is worth paying attention to.

Energía Costa Azul (ECA) LNG, Mexico's first LNG liquefaction facility on the Pacific coast, sent its first commissioning cargo to South Korea in early July. The 3.25 mtpa single-train project near Ensenada is supplied with Permian gas through a cross-border pipeline for export to Asian markets, creating a Pacific outlet for US-produced gas outside the established Gulf Coast export system. The facility had produced its first LNG shortly before that shipment, and later that month the commissioning process was extended after planned inspections identified technical issues, pushing substantial completion to the fourth quarter of 2026.

The advantage is primarily geographical. Direct Pacific access bypasses the Panama Canal and avoids Cape of Good Hope diversions and routes exposed to heightened geopolitical risk in the Middle East, reducing voyage time and transportation costs. For Asian buyers, this provides a more direct supply route for US LNG and reduces exposure to potential congestion or disruption along longer alternatives.

ECA's capacity is relatively modest, and little near-term expansion is expected. The broader Ensenada terminal operates as an LNG import and regasification facility, with ECA LNG adding liquefaction capacity to this established infrastructure. However, existing import and regasification commitments, which run through 2028, limit the extent to which the site can accommodate additional export capacity without compromising its original function. This creates a structural constraint on development, making any large-scale expansion more likely to be a longer-term prospect. ECA's immediate contribution to LNG exports will therefore remain limited in volume, even though the project retains broader strategic relevance within the Pacific Basin.

However, prospects remain for further LNG output growth in the region. Farther southeast, the AMIGO LNG project (American Mexican Integrated Gas Operations) is set to develop as a floating terminal off Guaymas, Sonora, in the Gulf of California. The proposed facility may reach 5.2mtpa and would use US feedgas to serve Asian markets. The project is reportedly close to FID, with operations estimated to commence in 2028. AMIGO would establish a separate Pacific outlet and broaden Mexico's export base. Mexico therefore provides the US gas system with a Pacific-facing export platform, allowing US-origin volumes to increase presence in Pacific and compete directly with Canadian West Coast LNG.

More broadly, Mexico's emerging LNG export corridor underscores the growing importance of Pacific LNG trade. By creating a west-coast outlet for US gas, it extends the reach of US supply in the Pacific Basin while offering Asian importers a shorter shipping route and greater diversification of export channels. Although these cargoes will generate fewer tonne-miles than Atlantic-to-Asia shipments, the expected volumes remain too limited to materially affect aggregate LNG shipping demand. If AMIGO LNG enters operation by 2028, however, Mexico's Pacific corridor could become a more significant channel for delivering US gas to Asian markets. In the near term, particularly while disruptions to Middle Eastern supplies prompt Asian importers to seek alternative sources, exports of US gas through Mexico could strengthen regional supply security. To the extent that these volumes reduce Asian demand for Atlantic Basin cargoes, they could also modestly improve the availability of US LNG for European buyers.

## Tanker Chartering

The crude tanker freight market saw divergent trends last week, mainly supported by the robust performance of Suezmaxes, while VLCCs suffered small losses. Overall, BDTI averaged 2,683, up by 4.7% on a weekly basis.

VLCC economics revealed a market last week that was absorbing a minor headline dip while its structural underpinnings firmed. TCE declined marginally by 1.6% w-o-w to \$237,696/day, however this was in contrast to a tightening vessel count across major trading areas, the Gulf, West Africa, and Americas, the real story is disappearing tonnage, not weakening demand. Visible fixing stayed thin, partly a function of Hormuz-related caution keeping some deals private. Against this backdrop, the segment looks better positioned for a rate recovery than the modest weekly decline suggests, provided charterer enquiry follows through.

Suezmax segment firmed, with TCE rates up 17% w-o-w to \$279,244/day, driven largely by geopolitical risk and tightening tonnage. Owners' growing preference to ballast via the Cape rather than risk Hormuz is quietly clearing Gulf tonnage, while West Africa mirrors that scarcity as cargoes outpace available ships.

The Black Sea tells a different story: security incidents near the terminal are pushing owners to avoid the region, making vessel availability, not cargo volume, the real constraint that drives rates upwards. The wider Atlantic is tightening in tandem, with strong Guyana and USG activity drawing on the same limited tonnage pool.

Aframax markets showed local fundamentals in the North Sea, Mediterranean, and UKC that were largely soft, with oversupplied tonnage weighing on rates, only offset as ships ballasted west toward stronger U.S. earnings. Asia stood apart, tightening on a strong level of enquiries combined with scarcity of prompt tonnage, rather than borrowed support. Overall, sentiment across regions remained tied to U.S. Gulf strength, which underpinned the segment's TCE at \$80,913/day, up 4% on a weekly basis.

## Dry Bulk Chartering

The dry bulk market moved broadly downwards last week, with BDI averaging 2,955, down 1.4% w-o-w.

The Capesize market weakened, with divergent regional conditions. The Pacific saw declines, with activity affected by the Singapore holiday and reduced major-miner presence, before an expanding tonnage list took over as the dominant driver, pushing West Australia-China rates down. The Atlantic was mixed: South Brazil and West Africa stayed relatively supported by tight tonnage availability despite limited activity, while the North Atlantic drifted lower as declining transatlantic demand met a slowly growing tonnage list.

The Kamsarmax/Panamax segment held broadly steady on average, though this masked a clear regional split. The Pacific stayed firm, supported by consistent demand and prompt tonnage tightened further by weather delays. The Atlantic moved in the opposite direction, losing momentum as vessel supply outpaced cargo availability, particularly for transatlantic business, pushing owners to lower rate expectations. Resilience in the Pacific offset weakness in the Atlantic, leaving the segment's average largely unchanged despite diverging regional dynamics.

The Ultramax/Supramax market posted a modest overall improvement, though performance varied widely by region. The Pacific led gains, supported by rising Chinese steel demand and weather-tightened tonnage, while North Pacific grain volumes underpinned Asian rates. North America stayed steady on Panamax spillover strength, and the South Atlantic nudged higher despite ballasters capping gains. Elsewhere, the Mediterranean softened on thin demand, while SE Asia and the Subcontinent saw rates decline.

Handysize rates eased marginally, with mixed performance. The USG lagged, weighed down by an excess of tonnage chasing limited cargo. Early-week firmness across the Continent and Mediterranean faded as enquiry thinned. Asia was the outlier remaining balanced, with steady cargo flow.

## Demolition Market

The major ship recycling hubs of Subcontinent saw improving demand conditions last week, however, candidate tonnage remains limited.

India's ship recycling sector, unable to match the pricing levels of its Subcontinent rivals, has leaned into more niche segments, ro/pax vessels and reefers, while continuing to absorb dark fleet units. That positioning is what's keeping activity ticking over. The local steel market lends support to sentiment, with steel prices trending higher and reinforcing a positive outlook. On the economic front, inflation is proving a bigger concern, with CPI climbing at its fastest pace in nineteen months, propelled largely by food costs, while the government has taken preventive action to support the rupee as price pressures intensify.

At Chattogram, demand remains healthy, with interest focused on larger tonnage amid stable pricing, though a gap persists between buyer and seller expectations. Bangladesh's compliant recycling capacity is set to expand, with five ship-recycling facilities securing conditional DASR environmental approval, clearing them to import vessels and demonstrate safe-recycling credentials, a development that adds buying power to the market. The steel market, meanwhile, remains sluggish, with an extended outage at the Maheshkhali FLNG having curbed gas supply to the grid, triggering power cuts that have slowed steel output, with relief expected within weeks.

At Gadani, demand remains strong but supply-constrained: recyclers are eager buyers, particularly in the dry bulk segment, though the bottleneck is scarce tonnage rather than weak appetite. The steel market is holding steady, supported by a decline in Iranian imports. On the policy front, ship recyclers are concerned over a new tax rule that rewards steel mills for importing over 70% of their scrap with a lower sales tax, a threshold that excludes locally recycled steel. Registered mills buying local scrap would face the higher rate, refundable only later, while non-registered mills would absorb it outright, potentially weighing on recyclers' cash flow through delayed payments.
