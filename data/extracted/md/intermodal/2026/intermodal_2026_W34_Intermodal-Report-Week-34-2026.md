## Weekly Market Report

## Market Insight
By <u>Nikos Tagoulis, Senior Analyst</u>

Months after taking effect, the Jones Act waiver continues to bolster the US coastal trade. The exemption has now been extended for a further 90 days, with the latest renewal since August 17, 2026, under a stricter framework than previously, reflecting political debate in Washington over this century-old protectionist law governing US cabotage, first introduced in 1920. The law has long divided US maritime industry and policy makers. Supporters cite domestic shipbuilding, seafaring employment and defence readiness, while critics point to market distortion, higher freight costs and a coastwise-eligible fleet insufficient to meet demand.

Under the revised framework, priority is preserved for coastwise-qualified vessels through a MARAD availability survey, with foreign-flagged tonnage admitted only where none is available. Applicants must provide vessel, voyage and cargo details to the relevant US authorities, which assess domestic vessel availability and eligibility. Operators must also report the completed voyage within 10 days.

The extension follows a substantial increase in waterborne US flows. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, combined shipments by tanker and barge from PADD 3 (US Gulf Coast) to PADD 5 (U.S. West Coast) reached 190k bpd in April, compared with less than 30k bpd in January and February, and remained elevated in May. The composition of volumes changed as well. A trade previously dominated by renewable diesel broadened to include gasoline components, finished gasoline, jet fuel and crude oil.

The shift is also evident on the East Coast, where flows from the US Gulf to the East Coast (PADD 1) rose as well, reaching a record high of 1.2m bpd in April, mainly comprising gasoline blending components and distillate fuels. Moreover, crude shipments to the East Coast rose in May to a record 180k bpd. This increase is also partly attributed to Florida's high dependence on US Gulf refineries for its fuel needs, making access to coastal tonnage critical for the region's fuel security.

The commercial implications are visible. By excluding foreign-flagged vessels from coastwise trade, the Jones Act prevents the wider international fleet from responding to US domestic transportation demand. When coastwise-qualified tonnage is unavailable, cargoes may face higher freight costs, delays or alternative routing.

The latest waiver therefore provides some relief, while preserving priority for eligible tonnage, also allows international vessels to meet part of the demand. This activates previously latent transportation demand, as cargoes that may have been uneconomic or difficult to move under the Jones Act can now be served by foreign tonnage. The volumes remain relatively small, and some Gulf Coast barrels may displace imports into other US regions. The net impact on international tanker demand therefore depends not only on the additional coastal movements, but also on the cargoes they replace.

The ton-mile contribution also varies significantly by route. USG-East Coast voyages are relatively short, allowing vessels to turn quickly, while West Coast cargoes involve longer Panama Canal transits and potentially substantial repositioning, generating more ton-miles and tying up vessels for longer. For the tanker market, the additional demand is likely to continue benefiting product tankers, particularly MRs, while the increase in crude movements provides additional cargo opportunities for Aframax-es. Yet the waiver may matter less for the tanker demand and volumes it directly creates than for the additional employment option it gives international vessels in US Gulf. Even modest volumes can affect the market at the margin if a domestic cargo provides an alternative to an export stem, changing the opportunity cost of fixing a prompt ship elsewhere. Its impact may therefore emerge through vessel positioning and availability in the Atlantic Basin rather than through growth in tanker demand. In this sense, the waiver temporarily affects the deployment map for international tankers in the US Gulf, broadening the deployment choices.

With the waiver still temporary and approvals granted on a voyage-by-voyage basis, the additional demand remains policy-driven. However, if foreign tonnage continues to fill capacity gaps and intensify competition, U.S. policymakers may move toward a broader reassessment of how the Jones Act is implemented.

## Tanker Chartering

The crude wet market moved higher last week, driven mainly by the surge in VLCC rates, while spot rates in the other crude tanker classes remained relatively stable. The BDTI rose 9% w-o-w to average 2,929.

The VLCC segment firmed considerably across all major regions last week as strong demand steadily absorbed available tonnage and shifted negotiating leverage towards owners. In the Middle East, consistent activity and private deals tightened vessel supply and drove rates higher. West Africa benefited from a thin tonnage list and a firmer Atlantic market, while USG and Brazilian cargo demand progressively absorbed additional tonnage. Sentiment remains firmly positive, although it remains to be seen whether demand can support current freight levels and sustain momentum. Average VLCC TCE earnings stood at \$325,024/day, 37% higher w-o-w.

Suezmaxes witnessed a mixed performance, as the West Africa and Guyana remained strong, with robust demand thinning the tonnage list. By contrast, the Black Sea faced increased vessel availability, while the Middle East saw subdued demand. In the US Gulf, competitive Aframax rates drew some cargoes away from Suezmaxes, while the delayed emergence of new business weighed on sentiment. Overall, average Suezmax TCE earnings stood at \$281,852/day, approximately 1% above the previous week.

Aframax spot rates remained broadly stable, as gains in some regions offset losses elsewhere. Strong Mediterranean demand was the main source of support, absorbing vessels and tightening the tonnage list, while the North Sea market was muted, with rates almost unchanged despite declining vessel availability. In Asia, strong demand and healthy earnings attracted ballasters from the weakening Pacific/TMX market. However, the resulting increase in tonnage supply could limit further rate gains. Overall, average Aframax TCE earnings stood at \$80,567/day, almost unchanged w-o-w.

## Dry Bulk Chartering

The dry bulk market remained broadly stable, except for the Kamsarmax/Panamax segment, where rates declined. The BDI averaged 2,820, down 4.5% w-o-w.

The Capesize market was largely unchanged, with the Pacific providing the main support as Australian miners increased cargo volumes and absorbed newly available ships. The South Atlantic was less constructive, as weaker demand from Brazil and West Africa failed to generate rate traction. The North Atlantic opened firmly, but the improvement proved brief. As cargo interest weakened, tonnage built up, eroding owners' leverage.

Kamsarmax/Panamax earnings drifted lower as available tonnage expanded faster than demand across the two basins. Pacific weakness originated in Southeast Asia, where limited Indonesian volumes left more ships competing for cargoes, outweighing healthier northern demand. In the North Atlantic, excess capacity pressured rates, although an improving USG grain programme <u>provided support and a better outlook.</u> The South Atlantic was a pocket of strength, with active fixing absorbing tonnage.

The Ultramax/Supramax segment continued its modest improvement, although gains remained uneven, with regional demand determining rate direction. Strength came mainly from the East, as North Pacific grain and Indonesian coal volumes absorbed tonnage and lifted rates. Across the Atlantic, weaker cargo availability intensified competition for employment, reversing North America's early strength and leaving the Continent and Mediterranean under charterers' control. The South Atlantic proved the exception, due to limited vessel supply.

The Handysize market edged higher, reflecting regional tonnage scarcity. The Pacific led, driven by healthy steel demand and limited tonnage, while the South Atlantic found support from improving cargo flows and fewer open ships. The Mediterranean and Continent remained subdued as demand declined. In the USG and NCSA, demand was flat, but constrained tonnage maintained rate levels.

## Demolition Market

India's recycling market has gathered momentum, with buying interest broadening from niche tonnage into more mainstream candidates even as Alang's pricing continues to lag that of regional rivals. Firmer domestic steel plate values have added to the improved tone, offering further support to bids. Persistent rupee depreciation against the dollar, compounded by inflationary pressure, is eroding the purchasing capacity of local yards and tempering expectations for how far the current improvement can be carried.

Gadani carried the previous week's fundamentals forward, with keen buying appetite meeting a thin list of recycling candidates and prices continuing to firm, leaving Pakistan the most competitive destination in the Subcontinent. With demand outstripping the limited tonnage on offer, competition for the units reaching the market has pushed bidding to increasingly assertive levels. The fall in Iranian steel imports has lent further support, alongside a constructive domestic backdrop in which mill demand remains strong and plate values are increasing.

In Bangladesh, the market retains a broadly positive tone, with active demand focused on mid- and smaller-sized dry bulk candidates. However, buyers remain cautious, monitoring the steel market's direction, currently sluggish with declining prices as monsoon-related adverse weather has impacted market mobility. On the economy front, signals are encouraging. Foreign reserves have accumulated to their highest level in recent years, while inflation eased m-o-m in July, adding a optimistic note to the broader outlook.

Turkey's market remains subdued, with currency weakness limiting buyers' purchasing power and consequently demand. Candidates list continues to be underpinned by vessels moving under EU regulation, though optimism persists for a further inflow of tonnage ahead. On a positive note, the local steel market presented improving signs.
