## Weekly Market Report

## Market Insight
<mark>By Yiannis Parganas, Head of Research Department</mark>

India's coal trade is moving in two different directions. Thermal coal imports are lower, while Russian coal arrivals are increasing. For dry bulk, the impact depends on which cargo is being lost and which cargo is replacing it. Indonesian thermal coal and Russian coal do not create the same vessel demand. Indian thermal coal imports were about 65 million tonnes in January-May, down 12% year-on-year. This was the lowest January-May volume in four years. The decline did not come from weak electricity consumption. Total power generation increased 5% in the first five months of the year. India still needed more power, but a larger part of that demand was covered by domestic coal and renewables.

Renewable generation rose 22% in January-May. In May, renewable output increased 29.31% year-on-year to 27.58 billion kWh and reached 17.9% of the power mix. Peak power demand exceeded 270 GW on 21 May, and total power demand in May rose 11.2% to a two-year high. Thermal generation also increased 10% in May.

The market had strong power demand, but imported thermal coal was not the main balancing source. This changes the position for Panamax and Supramax vessels trading in the Indian Ocean. Indonesian coal into India has been a regular employment source for these segments. The voyage is short, but the cargo flow is frequent. It supports fixing around Indonesia, Singapore, the Bay of Bengal and the Indian coast. When Indian utilities reduce imported thermal coal purchases, the first effect is lower regional cargo availability, not necessarily lower tonne-mile demand across the whole coal market. Owners should be careful with heatwave assumptions. Higher temperatures can lift electricity demand, but they do not automatically produce a large import response. If domestic coal stocks are available and renewable generation is strong, utilities can reduce spot import requirements. Coastal plants may still need imported coal, and sudden supply stress can still support prompt demand, but the link between power demand and seaborne thermal coal demand is weaker than before.

The other part of the trade is Russian coal. India's Russian coal arrivals are estimated near 3.16 million tonnes in June, close to record levels. Russia is becoming a larger supplier to India, partly through thermal coal and partly through metallurgical coal. The metallurgical coal angle is important for shipping. India has large domestic thermal coal reserves, but it does not have enough high-quality coking coal for steelmaking.

India's steel output is around 168 million tonnes, with a longer-term target of 400 million tonnes by 2035. Unless the steelmaking route changes much faster, imported coking coal remains necessary. That supports coal demand from suppliers outside the usual short-haul thermal coal trade. Russian coal can therefore grow even while thermal coal imports into the power sector fall. The vessel impact is different. Indonesian thermal coal into India is short-haul and repetitive. It creates steady employment but fewer tonne-miles per tonne. Russian coal involves longer distances and more operational issues. Routing, sanctions, banking, insurance and port handling can all affect execution. A cargo from Russia can absorb more vessel time than an Indonesian stem, but it can also be less predictable.

For Panamax and Supramax owners, the replacement effect is the main issue. If India removes part of the Indonesian thermal coal book and adds more Russian coal, vessel demand will depend on the balance between lost short-haul volume and added longer-haul cargo. A lower thermal coal import number does not always mean weaker dry bulk demand. It depends on origin, coal type, voyage length and execution risk. For charterers, the risk is that fewer total imports do not automatically translate into easier freight. If the remaining cargo programme includes more longer-haul Russian coal or more complex metallurgical coal flows, vessel availability can still tighten in specific positions. India should be tracked by coal type and supplier, not only by total coal import volume.

The main point is that India's coal trade is becoming less useful as a simple volume indicator. A fall in thermal coal imports is negative for short-haul Indonesian employment, but higher Russian coal flows can offset part of that loss through longer voyages and more vessel time. The effect will not be equal across segments or loading areas. Panamax and Supramax demand will depend on how much short-haul utility coal is removed, how much Russian or metallurgical coal is added, and where the replacement cargoes are loaded. For the next few months, Indian coal imports should be followed by origin and coal type before drawing conclusions on freight direction.

## Tanker Chartering

The crude tanker market ended the week on a firmer footing, with improving sentiment across the larger segments despite uneven activity and continued geopolitical uncertainty.

VLCC trading opened quietly, with charterers initially testing sentiment rather than committing in size. Enquiry appeared around key Middle East loading areas, but actual fixing in the Arabian Gulf remained limited as owners hesitated to act too early amid an uncertain backdrop. The Atlantic, however, became progressively more constructive as the week advanced. West-to-East demand strengthened, tonnage availability tightened in the natural fixing window, and owners gained confidence to ask for higher levels. Ongoing uncertainty surrounding Middle East diplomacy, regional disruption risks, and the Strait of Hormuz kept decision-making cautious, but late-week activity helped reduce available lists and left the segment with a stronger forward tone.

Suezmax sentiment also improved, supported by a relatively balanced Atlantic fleet position and firmer West African fundamen-tals. The Mediterranean and Black Sea markets were less dynamic, with owners more willing to secure employment where returns remained attractive rather than hold out aggressively. US Gulf sentiment strengthened in line with the Atlantic improvement, although Guyana activity remained thin and selective. In the Arabian Gulf, enquiry was present, but charterers remained disciplined as regional political uncertainty continued to weigh on confidence.

Aframaxes in the Mediterranean softened early as available tonnage outweighed cargo demand, but by the end of the week the decline appeared to have slowed. In the North Sea, charterers briefly gained leverage, yet owners resisted deeper losses, leaving sentiment broadly steady into next week.

## Dry Bulk Chartering

**Dry Bulk Chartering**

The dry bulk sector remained uneven this week, with larger segments facing regional pressure while smaller units maintained a firmer tone overall, driven by strong Atlantic basin.

The Capesize segment found support in the Pacific, as steady miner activity and increased coal stems from East Australia underpinned sentiment. Although rates eased on average up to mid-week, the overall movement remained relatively contained, suggesting a controlled correction. The Atlantic remained the weaker basin, pressured by limited fresh enquiry, increasing tonnage supply and different views between charterers and owners, preventing concluded deals. Overall, the market ended firmer slightly, led by Pacific strength, though sustained upside will require stronger Atlantic demand.

The Kamsarmax/Panamax market gradually lost momentum over the week, as positive Atlantic backdrop waw increasingly outweighed by weaker fundamentals in the Pacific. Atlantic sentiment remained comparatively resilient, underpinned by grain and mineral demand combined with tighter regional tonnage. The Pacific, however, lacked momentum, as subdued North Pacific and Australian demand left an ample prompt list competing for insufficient cargo volumes. Indonesia was a relative bright spot, showing comparatively better enquiry.

The Ultramax/Supramax segment maintained last week's pace, with gains mainly driven by firmer Atlantic conditions. North America, particularly the US Gulf, saw strong front-loading activity as charterers secured tonnage ahead of the holidays, while the Mediterranean also improved. Asia remained comparatively flat, offering limited fresh momentum.

Similarly, Handysizes remained on a firm footing this week, supported by continued strength in the Atlantic, particularly from the South Atlantic and US Gulf. Meanwhile, Asia and the Mediterranean held broadly stable, limiting further upside.

# Newbuilding Market

The newbuilding market maintained a healthy flow of deals last week, with 11 orders reported, primarily focused on the dry bulk and tanker sectors.

In the dry bulk segment, Erasmus Shipinvest exercised an option at Jiangsu New Hantong for 4 bulkers of 82k dwt each, priced at \$37m apiece and due for delivery in 2028-2029. Changhang Freight also contracted the same yard for 2 bulkers of 82k dwt each, with EEXI Phase 3, Nox Tier III. Sakura Ocean, meanwhile, commissioned three separate units in Japan, namely a bulker of 42k dwt at Tsuneishi Shipbuilding, a 40k dwt vessel at Imabari Shipbuilding and another vessel of the same size at Onomichi Dockyard, all against TC contract and for 2029 delivery.

On the tanker side, Yasa Holding agreed with Jiangsu New Hantong for the construction of 4 tankers of 319k dwt each, valued at \$125m each and scheduled for delivery in 2029. Asiatic Lloyd agreed with HD Hyundai for 3 tankers of 115k dwt each, at \$73m per unit and due in 2028-2029. Metrostar also booked a pair of 50k dwt MRs at HD Hyundai Mipo for \$53m apiece, with delivery set for 2028, while Ardmore Shipping proceeded with an order for 2 firm plus 2 optional 40.5k dwt tankers at Wuhu Shipyard, priced at \$44.9m per vessel and due in 2028-2029.

In containerships, Global Ship Lease turned to CSSC Huangpu Wenchong for 4 boxships of 6.2k teu each, priced at \$85m apiece and delivery estimated in 2029-2030.

Elsewhere, Sallaum Lines contracted Xiamen Shipbuilding for the manufacturing of a quartet of LNG/Ammonia ready PCCs of 8.6k ceu each, for delivery in 2028-2029.

## Demolition Market

Ship recycling fundamentals across the Sub-continent remained broadly steady last week, with monsoon conditions limiting activity and buyers monitoring geopolitical developments.

In India, conditions showed little change, with limited tonnage supply, monsoon-related operational constraints, and uncertainty over whether current price levels accurately reflect prevailing ship recycling conditions. News of a potential peace agreement and reopening of Hormuz has added a measure of optimism, supporting the broader economic outlook through a firmer rupee against the US dollar and lower energy prices. At the same time, the local steel market continues to show signs of underlying weakness, with demand insufficient to absorb available volumes, and prices under pressure.

Gadani maintained a steady footing this week, with overall conditions largely unchanged. The further firming of the Pakistani rupee, supported by de-escalation around Hormuz. In the steel market, mills are largely postponing purchases and maintaining a wait-and-see stance, in view of easing in energy prices. On the compliance front, Pakistan continues to progress, with two additional yards obtaining HKC certification, bringing the total number of compliant facilities to five.

At Chattogram, sentiment remains relatively constructive, underpinned by currency stability, available yard capacity, and a healthy flow of candidates. However, weakening signals from the steel sector are weighing on buying appetite. Sluggish demand is limiting mills' revenues, while rising electricity costs are placing further pressure on margins. The post-monsoon period is expected to offer a clearer indication of whether the current positive momentum can be sustained.

In Turkey the market softened, pressured by tepid demand and less competitive pricing. Aliaga continues to target a more niche, compliance-driven segment of the market. Macro challenges persist with currency weakness and elevated inflation continuing to cloud the outlook.
