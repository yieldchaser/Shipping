## Weekly Market Report

## Market Insight
By <u>Nikos Tagoulis, Senior Analyst</u>

Following its successful first trial in 2025, China is set to take a further step towards regular container operations via the Northern Sea Route (NSR). Last year, the **4,890 teu** Istanbul Bridge, operated by Chinese-backed Sea Legend Shipping, completed the China–Europe haul in around 21 days. Building on that passage, Sea Legend plans a seasonal service of eight roughly weekly sailings from mid-August to October, deploying seven predominantly feeder-sized vessels and targeting transit times of 20 to 22 days. It marks the first serious attempt towards an Arctic liner container service.

The route's principal advantage lies in transit time. By shortening selected Northeast Asia–Northern Europe voyages by **30–40%**, it offers a faster seasonal alternative to Suez routings and China–Europe rail, with potential fuel and emissions savings against conventional routes. That saving matters most for cargo that values speed over cost: higher-value, time-sensitive goods such as electric vehicles, batteries and solar equipment, for which faster delivery frees capital that would otherwise remain tied up in transit. Ship size reflects the cargo volumes and the seasonal nature of the trade. With neither the volumes nor the time to fill larger ships, the service runs feeders of roughly 1,500 to 5,000 teu, sustaining near-weekly frequency through the season.

This initiative comes amid a gradual and steady rise in NSR activity. According to the Norwegian Centre for High North Logistics, transit voyages rose from 97 in 2024 to 107 in 2025, roughly five times the level of a decade earlier, while containership transits increased from 11 to 15.

Several major economies are moving to expand their presence along the NSR. Russia continues to move Arctic LNG, crude oil, coal and other commodities to Europe and Asia via the route, supported by an expanding icebreaker fleet and state-backed plans for ports, terminals and emergency-response infrastructure. China is testing the route in Asia–Europe connectivity within its broader Polar Silk Road context, while South Korea is likewise building operational experience through government-supported trial voyages. Together these point to more sustained interest in Arctic shipping.

This interest is becoming more pertinent amid heightened geopolitical uncertainty and disruption to global energy and trade flows, a period in which the value of alternative maritime routes can exceed their immediate commercial scale. The NSR is not a direct response to events in the Strait of Hormuz, nor a broad alternative to the Suez Canal. Yet even with seasonal operations and limited volumes, it offers a degree of transport optionality and reduces dependence on a handful of maritime chokepoints.

The route holds potential, but for now remains constrained. Volumes are still marginal against established corridors, and navigation depends on seasonal ice conditions, suitable tonnage, specialised crews and reliable navigational support. Infrastructure is limited across much of the route, with few deepwater ports, safe harbours, bunkering facilities or emergency-response capabilities. Use of the NSR also requires engagement with Russia's administration and, where conditions demand, Russian icebreaker support, while sanctions on Russian counterparts add compliance risk. Moreover, rising traffic would increase local emissions, including black carbon, and the probability of an accident in a sensitive ecosystem where harsh conditions could complicate any response.

For now, the service's significance is largely demonstrative. The reliability of Sea Legend's schedule offers a test of whether scheduled Arctic operations can be repeated rather than remaining one-off demonstrations. Successful execution would mark another step after last year's Istanbul Bridge passage and, over the medium term, could support a specialised seasonal trade, generating demand for ice-capable feeder tonnage, navigational expertise and associated marine services. Warming temperatures and retreating ice may also extend the operational window. The NSR remains presently a niche seasonal corridor, though on a slow but steady upward trend.

## Tanker Chartering

Crude tanker markets recorded mixed results, supported by firming Aframax segment. The BDTI averaged 2,193, rising by 12.9% w-o-w.

VLCCs lost ground, reversing the previous week's firming, the VLTCE surrendering 4% on the week to settle at \$197,852/day. The Gulf was the exception: renewed hostilities after the MoU's collapse lifted rates, with some owners avoiding the region leaving Fujairah activity to carry on. Sentiment in West Africa stayed flat, a limited tonnage pool offset by scarce fresh demand and the drag of weaker returns in Brazil. USG suffered losses despite some private deals partially cushioning the decline.

Similarly, Suezmaxes retreated, with rates declining across most regions. The bright spot was again the Middle East, where geopolitical tensions lifted rates, though fixing activity stayed limited. West Africa saw an overall retreat despite firmer rates for prompt liftings, with softer levels for more forward dates as a larger tonnage pool builds and ballasters are expected from the East. The

Mediterranean held steady, supported by a robust Aframax segment, while in the Black Sea a strike on the CPC terminal finally dragged a long-resilient TD6 lower late in the week. Suezmax TCE read \$139,692/day, down by 3.4% w-o-w.

The Aframax segment was the clear outperformer last week, its benchmark earnings up by close to 60% to settle at \$85,447/ day, with the advance gaining pace as the week drew on. The momentum was driven by a surging Mediterranean, where a flurry of prompt cargo met an already sparse list of vessels, leaving owners firmly in control. The North Sea was buoyant too, robust fixing absorbing much of the tonnage while some units ballasted south. Asia held stable despite low key activity underpinned by Middle East uncertainty, while the USG firm ed.

## Dry Bulk Chartering

Dry bulk spot markets were weighed down by a sharp downturn in the Capesize segment, while the other segments remained more resilient. The BDI fell by 6.5% w-o-w to 2,752.

Last week the Capesize segment lost momentum, reversing the gains of previous weeks. Pacific market reflected a straightforward imbalance: even with the major miners active, fresh cargo was too light to clear the tonnage, and rates drifted lower as owners met firmer resistance. The Atlantic lent early support through the South American and West African trades, but that impetus faded as trading grew hesitant. By the close, the C5TC finished roughly 13% down, at \$33,653/day.

The Kamsarmax/Panamax market drifted through a quiet week, with little to move rates across most trades. The Atlantic was flat, well-supplied and lacking direction, with transatlantic demand easing as the week progressed. Asia struck the firmer note: owners held their ground despite prompt supply, with support emerging from <u>Australian mineral cargoes, North Pacific grain and a recovery in SE Asian volumes after cease of adverse weather.</u> The P5TC stood flat, down 0.2% w-o-w at \$20,236/day.

The Supramax segment edged higher, though the improvement was narrow and unevenly spread, the S11TC up 1.8% w-o-w at \$21,960/day. Asia was a pocket of strength: constrained available tonnage, a steady flow of Chinese steel cargoes and firmer Indonesian coal enquiry kept the Pacific well supported. The USG, by contrast, cooled from its recent highs, with rates slipping through the week. Elsewhere the picture was quiet and largely unchanged.

The Handysize market saw divergent trends last week, with rates overall declining 1.2% on a weekly basis and the HS7TC closing at \$16,261/day. Europe held steady, lending rates support, but the fall was driven by softening fundamentals, tepid demand and ample supply across the South Atlantic, USG and Asia.

## Demolition Market

At Alang, the market firmed last week on tightening steel supply, as the monsoon-driven slowdown in cutting activity limited plate volumes. Even so, the rebound varies by product and could prove fragile, given tepid downstream steel demand and the absence of a clear catalyst in the segment. Meanwhile, India's willingness to accept sanctioned vessels, in contrast with its Subcontinent peers, partly offsets its weaker pricing competitiveness. Sentiment is affected by last week's weakening of local currency versus US dollar. On the macro front, June inflation unexpectedly surged to its strongest reading since late 2024, as earlier oil disruptions transmitted into transport costs, compounded by firmer food inflation.

Chattogram drifted through another quiet week, with the stand-off between willing buyers and a hesitant market still unresolved. Enquiry for tonnage remains healthy, but appetite is increasingly selective, favouring bulkers and units free of compliance exposure after a tanker that had cleared all formalities only to appear on a sanctions list before making it to the yard sharpened caution among recyclers and cash buyers alike. Steel trading is largely dormant, though activity picked up once flooding subsided, and mills expect firmer demand after the monsoon. Gadani held steady, its fundamentals little altered from the prior week: candidates remain scarce while purchasing appetite stays firm, and an enduring scrap shortage continues to underpin healthy buyer demand. Recyclers' offers were unmoved. Local steel prices improved on firmer sentiment, though actual consumption has yet to follow. Optimism rests partly on fiscal measures, with a lower property withholding tax and subsidised housing schemes expected to lift construction after the monsoon. Meanwhile, Middle East geopolitical tensions are lifting oil prices, placing a growing strain on Pakistan and much of the region.

Turkey stayed subdued, with a tight pool of candidates and a weak currency eroding purchasing power and dampening sentiment.
