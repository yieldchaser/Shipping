## Weekly Market Report

## Market Insight
By Nikos Tagoulis, Senior Analyst

The Middle East conflict entered a new phase following the Houthis' imposition of a naval blockade against Saudi Arabia, adding to geopolitical uncertainty. Beyond attacks on vessels, recent reports that a Houthi strike forced the 400,000-bpd Jazan oil refinery to halt operations demonstrate that the Houthis are also willing to target energy infrastructure.

This escalation comes at a time when Saudi Arabia increasingly relies on its west coast to maintain commodity exports following the disruption of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Seaborne crude oil exports through Yanbu doubled in Q2 2026, reflecting increased flows through the East–West pipeline as the Kingdom sought to bypass Hormuz.

Against this backdrop, tanker shipping faces the most immediate exposure. For Saudi crude exports, the effects would differ by destination. European cargoes would remain largely unaffected, as they already move north through the Suez Canal. By contrast, the consequences would be considerably greater for barrels destined for Asia and the Indian subcontinent via Bab el-Mandeb. To avoid the Gulf of Aden, vessels would have to sail north through Suez before rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, extending voyages to approximately 2.5 times their normal length, adding tonne-miles and increasing voyage costs.

Such rerouting would tighten prompt vessel availability and push spot rates higher, while increased use of the Suez Canal would provide further support for Suezmax demand. VLCCs would have to transit Suez partially laden, potentially requiring STS lightering and resulting in greater operational complexity and longer transit times.

Given the above, the strategic importance of the SUMED pipeline, and of Egypt more broadly as an oil transshipment hub, increases The pipeline connects the Red Sea with the Mediterranean, and Saudi Arabia would be expected to send greater crude volumes to Ain Sukhna port for transportation through SUMED to Sidi Kerir on the Mediterranean coast. From there, cargoes could continue to Asian markets via the Cape of Good Hope, providing an alternative route that avoids disruptions in both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb.

Moreover, energy-security considerations could lead importers to diversify away from the Middle East and seek alternative suppliers, reshuffling energy trade. Demand could shift towards the United States, Brazil and West Africa, while Russian crude flows to China and the Indian subcontinent could strengthen further.

For the oil-products segment, the reported attack on refinery infrastructure is particularly concerning. If such strikes persist and cause broader disruption to output and exports, they would compound the effects of Russia's ban on diesel exports, further constraining seaborne oil-product export volumes and heightening energy-security concerns. Should Saudi refinery operations remain close to normal levels, the more immediate consequence would likely be a redirection of trade flows. Eastbound shipments from Saudi Red Sea ports would become materially less competitive, shifting a larger share of Saudi fuel volumes towards Europe.

Outside the tanker segment, the impact would be less pronounced. In dry bulk, grain shipments moving via Mediterranean and Suez into the Red Sea would be less affected, unless Houthi attacks on infrastructure escalate. By contrast, steel trade would be more exposed, given that a substantial share of steel products originates in China and transits Bab el-Mandeb.

For containerships, the Houthi move primarily interrupts the segment's cautious steps towards returning to the Red Sea. Regionally, Jeddah is exposed because of its growing transshipment role and participation in Gulf feeder networks. More broadly, the continuation of diversions via the Cape of Good Hope, which tie up active capacity in an otherwise oversupplied segment, is expected to remain supportive of freight rates.

In conclusion, the threat of Houthi action disrupting Red Sea trade marks a significant escalation in the Middle East crisis and poses a material threat to regional navigation and trade. If it causes broader disruption to energy flows, the consequences could extend to the global macroeconomic outlook by intensifying energy-security concerns, raising energy costs, adding to inflationary pressures and weighing on global trade and economic growth.

## Tanker Chartering

Tanker markets trended upward overall last week, driven by a strong Aframax segment. The BDTI averaged 2,418, up 10% w-o-w.

On the VLCC side, a main driver last week was developments in the Middle East, which created uncertainty over regional flows, redirecting demand towards neighbouring markets. In Arabian Gulf, the market was in a holding stance, with charterers delaying fixings while owners withheld tonnage in anticipation of firmer rates. West Africa and US Gulf faced similar fundamentals, with rising demand shortening the tonnage list. Overall, VLCC TCE closed the week at \$193,293/day, 2.3% lower w-o-w.

The Suezmax segment witnessed divergent trends, with the Middle East and the Black Sea both sources of disruption. In the Arabian Gulf, heightened geopolitical risk and uncertainty took a toll on activity, with owners reluctant to commit to fixtures. Meanwhile, drone attacks near Novorossiysk brought CPC loadings to a standstill, throwing the Black Sea market into disarray. This landscape drew ballasters from both regions towards West Africa, where enquiry was insufficient to absorb the tonnage build-up, weighing on rates there. Suezmax TCE closed at \$154,070/day, up 10% w-o-w.

Aframaxes continued to support the crude freight market, building upward momentum, driven by a soaring USG market, with TD25 posting strong gains. Developments in the Middle East favoured the Aframax Med market, which saw increased activity at the SUMED-linked Sidi Kerir and in Libya. The North Sea was positive, with healthy activity and prospects for further rate upside, as vessels are estimated to ballast towards the robust USG. In the East, the market was subdued, amid tepid demand, with owners cautious ahead of the rest of August programme. Aframax TCE surged 29% w-o-w to \$110,482/day.

## Dry Bulk Chartering

The dry bulk markets delivered a mixed performance last week, with the BDI closing at **2,743** points, marginally lower w-o-w.

The Capesize segment recovered from the previous week's losses, as both basins provided support following a weak start, with the emergence of new demand. In the Pacific, support came from higher participation of Australian miners and a rise in seaborne coal volumes, while in the Atlantic, the main drivers of enquiry were West Africa and South Brazil. However, the increase in fuel costs, due to heightened geopolitical risk, casts a shadow over the outlook.

The Kamsarmax/Panamax market faced constrained conditions, as weakening demand overall exerted downward pressure on spot rates. In the Pacific, limited enquiry from North Pacific, Indonesia and East Australia led to a build-up of prompt supply, worsening fundamentals, while in the Atlantic, transatlantic flows stayed sluggish despite a brief midweek pickup. Overall, charterers gained leverage, as mounting competition for limited stems weighed on rates.

An uneven week for Ultramaxes/Supramaxes, with regional differences. Pockets of support were found in the East Med, driven by grain enquiry, and in the Indian Ocean, on the back of petcoke volumes. Elsewhere, the market was subdued: in the Atlantic, the US Gulf witnessed oversupply, while the North Atlantic was muted amid the seasonal slowdown. In the Pacific, despite firmer demand in Southeast Asia, the wider Asian market was weighed down by a growing tonnage pool.

The Handysize market saw mixed trends but was resilient overall. Asia provided support, as fundamentals favoured owners, with declining tonnage availability and emerging cargo demand. Elsewhere, demand weakened, though the Med held steady, with limited supply matching enquiry, while the oversupplied US Gulf saw rates under pressure.

## Demolition Market

At Alang, the market was muted last week amid tepid demand, while rising energy prices and the weakening of the Indian Rupee against the US Dollar weighed on sentiment. However, the backdrop in the steel market is supportive, with steel plate prices rising, driven mainly by tightening supply, as steel processing operations are curtailed by the monsoons. Finally, on the policy front, the proposed listing of two Alang facilities is a positive signal towards the opening of a first approved channel for EU flagged recycling candidates to be directed to the Indian market.

Bangladesh is emerging from a period of deadly monsoon flooding impacting economic activity, with the ship recycling segment recovering in parallel, as operations at yards and steel mills resume operations. Despite limited activity, buying interest remains healthy, particularly for bulk carriers, although the recent case of a tanker sanctioned after clearance but before beaching has made buyers more reluctant and increased demand for compliance-clear tonnage. Overall sentiment is positive, supported by expectations that candidates delayed during the monsoon period will be offered to the post-monsoon market.

Conditions at Gadani hub remained broadly unchanged, defined by a limited pool of candidates and healthy buying appetite. The steel market is supportive, with prices firming on the back of supply constraints, as monsoon-related adverse weather continues to disrupt mills' operations. There is optimism that government tax relief on property transactions and support for affordable housing will boost construction activity, and in turn steel demand, once the monsoon passes. Meanwhile, news of the country's credit rating upgrade, on the back of foreign capital inflows, added to the positive sentiment.

In Turkey, despite improving conditions in the steel market, activity remains flat as buyers maintain a wait-and-see stance amid a limited number of candidates and further weakening of the local currency.
