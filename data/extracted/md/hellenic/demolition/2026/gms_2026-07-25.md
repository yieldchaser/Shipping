## Market Commentary

The thirteenth consecutive night of American strikes closed out the week, and for the first time since this conflict began, the map grew. Yemen's Houthisstruck two Saudi oil tankers in the Red Sea and threatened a blockade of the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the route Saudi barrels had been taking around a shut Hormuz. Washington answered with threats of military punishment for further attacks on shipping and floated billing the damage to frozen Iranian assets, while both sides ruled out near-term talks. Asked about a ceasefire, President Trump said Iran needs "more of the same." The war that spent months confined to one strait has developed ambitions.

Oil did what oil does when chokepoints multiply. Brent crossed USD 100 on Thursday for the first time since May before easing to USD 97.63 by Friday, with WTI near USD 89.77, still leaving crude up roughly ten percent on the week. A third front opened quietly in the Black Sea, where drone attacks on tankers loading at the Caspian Pipeline Consortium terminal on July 17, 19, and 21 halted operations, forcing Kazakhstan, which routes some four-fifths of its exports through the terminal, to cut production. Asian buyers have begun pricing Saudi crude via the Suez Canal and around the Cape of Good Hope, itineraries last considered state-of-the-art in 1869 and during the Ever Given's brief tenancy, respectively.

Freight, which spent last week ignoring the war, spent this week ignoring its own rally. The Baltic Dry Index fell more than six percent last week to 2,752 and kept going, touching 2,671 on Monday, its lowest since July 2, as the Capesize index dropped 5.6% and then another 5.1% to 3,889 on softer iron ore and coal enquiry and improving vessel availability. The Supramax index, contrary as ever, climbed to 1,738, its highest since August 2022. Last week's edition noted that cargo fundamentals were winning the argument "for another week." The market, rarely so punctual, took the deadline literally. Softer Capesize earnings are the first genuine nudge toward the beach in months; the problem now is not the intent to exit, but the routes out.

The data calendar offered no fresh inflation prints. June's readings, from Washington's 3.5% to Karachi's 11.1%, remain operative until August, each now arguing in advance with an oil price that spent Thursday above USD 100. The TCMB filled the vacuum by holding at 37% for a fourth consecutive meeting. Currencies did the talking instead: USD/INR touched 96.88 on Thursday, near May's 96.97 record, before recovering toward 96.50; the Taka held at 123.35; the Lira set fresh records near 47.35; and the Pakistani Rupee closed near 278.40, again inside a quarter-rupee range. At this point, it is less an exchange rate than a physical constant.

In Bangladesh, the human toll from this month's flooding has risen to 57 confirmed dead, most in Cox's Bazar, including thirteen Rohingya refugees, with more than a million people affected at the peak. The waters are receding: shelters have consolidated to 111, relief allocations continue to move, and forecasters are watching river basins rather than the sky. Recovery, not rescue, is finally the operative word.

Commercially, Chattogram is stirring. Port operations have largely normalised, and a threatened 48-hour container transport strike was announced and withdrawn within the same week, resolved, unlike the weather, by conversation. The week's sharpest lesson came from compliance: a tanker was sanctioned after arrival and after clearances were complete, caught between anchorage and beach, while appetite across the basin tilted firmly toward bulkers and clean-history units.

The war has opened a second front at sea just as the weather has closed its front on land, and the beach is drying out in time to receive whatever gets through. The market remains paused, not broken. The ships wait for passage. The yards, at last, wait only for the tide.
