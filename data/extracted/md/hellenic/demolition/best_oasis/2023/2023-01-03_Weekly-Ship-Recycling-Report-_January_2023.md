## #PartneringWithTheBest

### Highlights of the Week

We'd like to start out by wishing our readers a "happy new year". Although no one can predict the future, it is helpful to start a new year with an understanding of the main concerns that will probably dominate the global agenda. 2023 won't be a monotonous year, a fact that is already evident.

In 2023, Asia will see challenging economic conditions. After several years of rapid export development, the region will experience a decline as the US economy is predicted to contract significantly and the EU will go into recession. The outlook for Asia's domestic demand is also uncertain as local economies adjust to the increased interest rate implemented in 2022 to combat inflation. The likelihood of North Korea restarting its nuclear tests and Taiwan's election preparations will add to the geopolitical risk.

Southeast Asia's increased political instability will give India the chance to attract more attention from international manufacturers. The Indian government has its own political concerns, but from the perspective of investors, it also administers consistent policy and reduces the possibility of a change in leadership.

In 2022, supply chain disruptions caused problems in both the EU and the US due to ongoing problems with the gas and energy supply chains, this may persist across European nations in the following year. However, the current Infrastructure Law is expected to increase infrastructure investments, which will likely cause the US economy to gain momentum, particularly in the automotive and infrastructure sectors.

Regarding the steel industry In 2023, it's anticipated that the US steel market will stay stable. Turkey and other Central and South American nations are all scaling back their construction projects as a result of inflation and interest rates, and in these locations, the steel industry is expected to experience a decline in 2023.

China is currently experiencing a wave of coronavirus infections, yet Beijing continues to move on with its plans to relax its regulations. In a significant move toward reopening its borders, it declared this week that international arrivals will no longer be subject to quarantine restrictions beginning in early January.

In October, the International Monetary Fund predicted that in 2023, the rate of global growth will drop to 2.7%. The group issued a warning in November that since issuing that forecast, the picture had grown even "gloomier." The actions of central banks going forward, the effects of China's impending reopening, and energy prices may determine if a worldwide recession occurs.

# India

## Rescuer of the Ship Recycling Market

India has emerged as the ship recycling market rescuer and the steel market's savior. Foreign exchange availability is consistent and shows no signals of impending problems. India is driving steel consumption, as China's construction sector is still struggling and the US and Europe are probably about to enter recessions.

To compete with China as a manufacturing hub, India is attempting to modernize the roadways, rail networks, and ports. According to the World Steel Association, this will result in an increase in steel demand of 6.7 percent to over 120 million tons in 2023, the fastest growth rate among large nations.

Of the three destinations for recycling in the Indian subcontinent, India is still the most reliable, with requests to buy recycling ships to fill their yards with diminishing availability of steel.

The majority of the yards are currently empty, with numerous yards available for green recycling in the future as compared to Bangladesh and Pakistan.

# Pakistan

Diminished foreign reserves cast a shadow over the economy

The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) made the decision to loosen LC limitations by eliminating the requirement for banks to obtain prior approval before beginning import transactions. Considering the risk profile of each customer and the liquidity conditions prevalent in the foreign exchange market, banks may actively engage with each of their customers to process their requests. Only the cash-rich recyclers having a good standing with the banks will be able to open the letter of credit reducing the number of buyers for vessels.

The recyclers who purchased the vessels last week are facing a herculean task in opening and releasing the LC due to the foreign reserves being decreased to their eight-year low to just above $6 billion, marking their lowest level in eight years. This only leaves Pakistan with enough imports to last for 1.10 months as its struggling government tries to meet the nation's complicated external finance requirements.

In the near future, LC opening issues will persist for a while, and this market may see limited buying until there is further easing from the government and the availability of foreign exchange.

# Türkiye

## Spiralling inflation and tumbling Lira cumulating the hardships

After rising for 17 months, inflation reached a 24-year high in October, primarily as a result of President Tayyip Erdogan's unconventional low interest-rate monetary policy and the resulting currency crisis in 2016. Inflation decreased marginally in November, and it is anticipated that the decline will increase in December and in the first quarter of the year 2023 when the price hikes compared to the same period of last year provide relief for this year's annual inflation calculation.

Turkish businesses have been severely impacted by the depreciation of the Lira, which has increased manufacturing costs while depleting people's incomes because they can now only purchase less with their money.

Two ship recycling facilities in Turkey have been removed from the list of authorized recycling facilities by the European Union, leaving only six yards in Turkey on the list, as was anticipated during the October Tradewinds Recycling Conference.
