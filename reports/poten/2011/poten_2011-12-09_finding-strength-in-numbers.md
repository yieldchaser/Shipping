# Finding Strength in Numbers

Even though the industry may have been busy on this week's holiday party circuit, news suggests that business is still getting done between the networking and canapés. It was announced on Tuesday that Mitsui OSK Lines / Phoenix Tankers, AP Moller, SAMCO and Ocean Tankers have signed a letter of intent to form a new tanker pool. While the deal has yet to be finalized, this new pool is expected to begin operation in February 2012 and control a combined fleet of around 50 VLCC tankers by the end of next year. Economies of scale and increased market presence achievable through pooling have helped companies reduce costs, streamline operations, and improve information flow, three significant advantages in these troubled markets. While some speculate that further consolidation of the VLCC market is a bullish signal for freight rates, strength in numbers is not likely to be a quick fix against flabby fundamentals.

The size of the pool and the impact it would have on the commercial control of the VLCC market have made this development particularly buzzworthy. With a combined 68 VLCCs among them today, the hopeful pool partners together currently own one of the largest chunks of the VLCC fleet, as seen in the chart below.

However, the size of their owned fleet should be considered in the potential for growth of the pool, as ships could be committed following expiration of their current charters. The companies also own a combined 11 newbuildings, reflecting about 10% of the orderbook scheduled for delivery between now and 2013. In the recent press release, the companies stated that they aim to control a pooled fleet of 50 vessels by the end of 2012. About 60% of the total VLCC fleet is primarily employed in spot market trade. If this percentage holds into next year, the new pool would likely be the single largest operator of spot market VLCCs, controlling about 13% of spot vessels, bringing the combined market share of itself and Tankers International up to 25% of the total fleet.

However, when markets recover, pools can become victims of their own success. Increased market consolidation tends to raise antitrust concerns. The European Union increased pressure on tanker pool operators in 2007, causing many pools to restructure their terms. Dropping non‐compete clauses and reducing the period of notice required to withdraw vessels were two developments spawned by the increased heat. Today's market is unlikely to lend itself to as much scrutiny. The absolute market share of the proposed pool is unlikely to be large enough to raise red flags. Considering today's rate environment and the comparatively fragmented commercial control of the VLCC market, consolidation should be viewed more a survival mechanism than a means of market dominance.

### What's in it For Me?

The larger market presence of pools allows certain economies of scale that would not be available to owners of smaller fleets. Bunker purchasing is one example. Because of the volumes involved and frequency of their business, pools may be able to negotiate volume‐based discounts in their contracts with bunker suppliers. Additionally, the real financial pressure faced by many tanker owners has made credit more of a concern. The ability of pools to consolidate purchasing while minimizing their suppliers' outstanding lines of credit has become more of a necessity in today's credit climate.

This advantage of scale also holds true on the marketing end of the business. Commercially, large fleets are able to take on opportunities that would unlikely be offered to smaller owners. Pools achieve the critical mass necessary to ensure coverage required for larger contracts of affreightment (COAs). In theory, any owner can bid for a COA, but may find themselves in a pickle if none of their ships are in position to meet a cargo requirement. Commercial operators of large fleets are less likely to have this problem and are able to bid for these contracts with less risk.

A common notion is that the ability of pools to mask the amount of tonnage available at any given time may superficially tighten the market and embolden market makers to demand higher rates for their tonnage. But those looking back historically for evidence to support a bullish hypothesis in today's tanker market may be led astray. While VLCC rates did firm in the early 2000's following the formation of Tankers International, it is important to remember that correlation does not always imply causation. The Erika incident of December 1999 significantly increased charterer sensitivity to vessel age and quality, serving to tighten the supply of acceptable tonnage. This was compounded with global economic recovery following the Asian financial crisis of 1997 and significant increases in worldwide crude oil demand. Today there is still plenty of new steel on the water, and more to come. It is important to remember that the peculiarities of markets at a certain time can yield different results to similar plays.
