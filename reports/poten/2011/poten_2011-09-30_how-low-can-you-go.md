# How Low Can You Go?

September marks the end of a particularly brutal summer. Assessed earnings on the Arabian Gulf to Far East benchmark trade averaged less than $5,700 per day from July through September. As we stand at the threshold of the historically lucrative fourth quarter, the market is holding its collective breath for an uptick in rates. Tonnage oversupply has confounded typical seasonal and systemic weakness and has the market resounding with bearish grumbles. Current rate levels have owners in limbo; earnings are such that companies are effectively paying charterers for the privilege of employment. After this fall in rates, many are asking "Can they get back up?"

So far, 2011 has proven uglier than expected, with year to date earnings on the benchmark Arabian Gulf - Far East VLCC voyage having averaged only $14,000 per day. Additionally, the range of achievable earnings has been relatively narrow, as seen in the chart below.

### Weeks below Opex

In most rational markets, vessel operating expense will serve as a floor for rate levels. Operating expenses include day‐to‐day costs of running a ship such as crewing and supplies, and are assessed between $10,000 and $12,000 per day for a modern VLCC.

Owners always have the option to refuse offers when rates are in the doldrums. However, the chances to fix at profitable levels this year have been limited indeed. Based on weekly assessments, earnings on the Arabian Gulf - Far East benchmark trade have been below the level of operating expenses for 17 out of 38 weeks in 2011. In the not‐so‐distant past, peak time charter equivalent earnings have reached as high as $250,000 per day. However, the highest assessed rate this year was WS 80, achieved in February and equating to time charter equivalent earnings of $53,300 per day, indicating a limit to upside potential.

### Buying Time

While operating expense may reflect a short‐term floor, the more important number over the long term is the vessel breakeven cost. This yardstick reflects the rate required for owners to recover both the day‐to‐day operating expense as well as the original cost of their investment. The time charter market can serve to hedge the volatility of the spot market exposure, providing owners a steady stream of revenue. While protecting owners from periods of weakness, out‐chartering vessels can also limit owners' ability to profit from upward swings in the spot market, and usually generates returns at a discount to average spot market earnings, as seen in the chart below.

2005‐2008 Average 2008 2009 Jan‐11 Current In a "normal" market, rates lessen as the term of the charter increases, becoming more reflective of vessel breakeven. However, this trend reverses in a weak market. The past three years have seen time charter rates outperform the spot market across all sectors. However, many owners have resisted committing their tonnage, holding out for the possibility of an upward swing in the spot market. Over that time, the term market has significantly weakened. Current opportunities to fix are somewhat limited as many charterers are unwinding their time charter portfolio. The assessed rate for a one year time VLCC time charter, currently assessed at $18,250 per day, has fallen over $10,000 per day since the beginning of the year.

### Tricks of the Trade

Supply has been the driving factor behind weak rates. Expectations of continued fleet growth do no favors for the forward outlook, nor do rumors of additional newbuilding programs. The VLCC fleet has grown by over 64 ships since the end of 2009, equating to over 12% fleet growth, as seen in the chart on the following page.

But with about 80 additional VLCC newbuildings expected to deliver into the fleet between now and the end of 2012, owners may need to take more drastic measures to keep from hemorrhaging cash.

### Laying it Out

The past few weeks have seen increased utterance of the "L" word. A brand new Aframax tanker was reported as going straight from yard to layup, leading to increased speculation that more owners could choose to idle their tonnage in the coming months. Mothballing tonnage could decrease the day‐to‐day operating expense of the vessel by nearly 90%. However, putting a vessel into layup is unlikely to prove a viable short‐term solution to market weakness. Start‐up costs following layup, which include vetting and inspections required to resume trading, can be expensive. Additionally, ships lose vetting approvals after a period of time, providing some incentive to keep trading even in the worst of markets.

In the face of unfavorable prospects, more owners may choose to sell off aging tonnage for demolition or conversion, helping to balance supply. Layup is an option of last resort for most owners. Moreover, the increasing frequency of rumors to the contrary indicates little faith in the industry's health in the near‐term. Following the herd mentality and laying up modern vessels may not be the best course of action, particularly when there are still older ships to be culled.
