# Zombie Refineries

Theeey're baaack. Atlantic Basin refineries that the market had relegated to the graveyard of unfit plants in an expanding world of more-sophisticated Pacific Basin capacity, are coming back to life under new management. During the past week, three announcements reminded market participants that refinery capacity is hard to kill. Delta Air Lines Inc. agreed to buy the 185 kbpd Trainer, PA refinery from ConocoPhillips (Phillips 66) for $150 million. Vitol and the co-founder of Petroplus have jointly agreed to acquire insolvent Petroplus' 68 kbpd Cressier refinery in Switzerland for "substantially less than $50 million". Finally, trading firm Gunvor announced that it will be restarting Petroplus' 108 kbpd Antwerp refinery this month, after agreeing to purchase the plant in early-March this year. ### Shutdowns

Restarts Additions Sources: Poten, Various In addition to this week's announcements, the private equity firm, Carlyle Group LP, is rumoured to be interested in acquiring a joint venture stake in Sunoco's 335 kbpd Philadelphia refinery, slated for shutdown in July, while the French government pursues a Sarkozy-led initiative to keep Petroplus' 162 kbpd Petit-Couronne refinery running under an agreement with Shell. This implies that following 3,466 kbpd of cumulative Atlantic Basin shutdowns since 2009, another 940 kbpd of refineries have restarted or will be restarting, while refiners have added 1,016 kbpd of cumulative new capacity. The net 1,510 kbpd of cumulative refinery capacity removals represents only 2.7% of 2009 Atlantic Basin capacity, which may be insufficient for a region operating with capacity utilisation below 80%.

It was not supposed to be like this. The popular refining hypothesis was that the addition of more sophisticated refining capacity situated closer to the stronger oil demand growth of Asia, Middle East and Latin America would drive out less competitive capacity in the OECD Atlantic Basin. This market view, in turn, supported the tanker hypothesis that growing long-haul product trades would boost product tanker demand.

Of course, everyone loves a bargain, and the distressed state of the Atlantic Basin refining market drove asset prices lower and attracted buyers. The initial shutdown of these plants removed capacity, tightened clean product balances and boosted margins, thus improving the apparent economics of refinery purchases. Still, these particular refineries closed for a reason. Trainer and Philadelphia still lack sufficient diesel desulphurisation capacity required to meet tighter regional heating oil sulphur specs, and Antwerp is a basic hydrofining refinery with a Nelson complexity index of only 4.5. Granted, these refineries have specific ownership rationales and benefits to the new buyers, but their re-emergence in the Atlantic Basin markets are likely to suppress margins.

Gasoline spot crack spreads had spiked previously on product tightness from refinery outages, but the prospect of additional crude runs in an uncertain demand environment pressured sentiment. The market concern is that refiners will not remove sufficient Atlantic Basin capacity, pressuring utilisations and margins. For product tanker owners, this would limit gasoline import demand in the US and diesel flows into Europe, while longer-term, this might undercut the economic rationale for expected Middle East and Asian capacity additions post-2014 that would add to clean tanker demand.

Finally, the return of these previously-uneconomic refineries should also sound a cautionary note for shipowners. A popular tanker investment hypothesis is that the current ordering of more fuel efficient tonnage would create a two-tier market - that less-thirsty vessels would win spot and period fixtures on superior economics, setting the market-clearing price; while less fuel-efficient tonnage would earn lower returns, or be excluded from the spot and period markets until the supply of eco-ships is exhausted in a given market. This hypothesis presumes, however, that cost differentials and returns on capital remain static, whereas the refining market is providing a strong reminder that capital will seek returns from dramatically lowered asset prices. This could allow previously-uncompetitive assets to earn suitable returns for their new owners, while keeping capacity in the market and equalising margins. Although refining and shipping represent distinct asset markets, a common lesson may yet emerge about popular investment hypotheses and their resolution in the asset markets.
