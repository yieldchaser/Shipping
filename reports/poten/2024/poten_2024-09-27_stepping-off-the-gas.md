# Stepping Off The Gas

### The Atlantic gasoline market is changing?

It will surprise nobody that the United States is the largest consumer of gasoline in the world. It is not even close. In 2023, the U.S. consumed on average 8.8 Mb/d of gasoline. China is a distant second with 3.2 Mb/d, followed by Brazil with 1.0 Mb/d. The largest European consumer is Germany, with 466 Kb/d, ranking them 11 th . As the world is transitioning towards electric cars for personal transportation, it is unlikely that America's position as the top consumer will ever be challenged. The United States is not only the largest consumer of gasoline in the world, but it is also the largest exporter and importer of this refined product. This makes the U.S. the dominant player in the global gasoline trade and, consequently, a very important driver of product tanker demand, in particular in the Atlantic Basin. However, changes are afoot in this market, both on the demand and the supply side, which could have a dramatic impact on the product tanker market in the Atlantic.

The U.S. is still the gasoline capital of the world, but even in the U.S., electric vehicles are making significant inroads in total car sales. In 2023, electric vehicle (EV) sales (including plug-in hybrids) in the United States reached a record high of approximately 1.2 million units. This is 10% of total U.S. light vehicle sales. While EV sales in the U.S. are much lower than in China and Europe, this still represents a 50% increase over 2022. This trend is expected to continue, and as a result, it is generally accepted that U.S. gasoline demand has peaked in 2018 and is unlikely to return to pre-pandemic levels.

Slowing domestic demand has posed a challenge to the U.S. refining industry for several years and limited its growth. In 2022, China surpassed the U.S. as the country with the largest refining capacity in the world, but to date the U.S. still produces more refined products than China because its refineries have a higher utilization rate. Historically speaking, U.S. refineries are geared towards a high gasoline yield. Due to the shale oil boom, gasoline output of the refineries, in the U.S. Gulf area in particular, received a further boost as they started using more domestic light sweet crude. These crudes generally yield more light products. The solution to this problem, especially for the facilities located in the U.S. Gulf, is exports. The U.S. has exported significant volumes of gasoline to markets in South America. However, changes in regional refining capacity could change these markets.

Mexico has traditionally been the largest market for U.S. refiners (see chart 1), taking an average of 50% of total U.S. gasoline exports (an average of 190,000 b/d in 2024 YTD). However, this market is under threat from domestic refining expansion and (to a lesser extent) EV penetration. Sales of EVs and hybrids are still relatively small (~ 5% of total car sales), but they are growing quickly. The biggest threat to U.S. gasoline exports to Mexico is the dos Bocas refinery. This refinery, which is designed to process 340,000 b/d has been delayed and has had many startup problems. However, this refinery is considered an important national project by the Mexican government and will eventually (2025/2026?) replace a significant portion of U.S. imports.

The new Dangote refinery in Nigeria is another potential threat to U.S. gasoline exports. On the one hand, the start of Dangote's gasoline sales earlier this month is an import step for this mammoth 650,000 b/d refinery in Africa. While initial sales will all go to the domestic market, it will replace imports (mainly from Europe) that will look for alternative customers. At full capacity, Dangote will produce up to 365,000 b/d of high-spec gasoline, which is more than domestic demand requires. The surplus product will be competing for customers in the international market.

Some of the additional gasoline that is produced in Nigeria as well as the potential surplus from Europe could end up on the East Coast of the U.S. (USAC), which is a large market in itself (chart 2). Most of the USAC gasoline imports come from Canada and Europe, with regular volumes shipped from India to the eastern shores of the U.S. However, the U.S. market is not growing and if Dangote manages to get a foothold in the U.S., it will be interesting to see which barrels they replace. In any case, product flows in the Atlantic Basin will not be negatively impacted by these changes. Ton-mile demand for product tankers in general and MR's in particular is expected to remain strong.
