# Following the Herd

### Tragedy of the Commons

The tanker fleet is faced with a very large orderbook. The reason the market is in such a position today can be explained by a theory applied to population growth known as The Tragedy of the Commons . Under this premise, individuals will behave rationally, acting in their own best interest to maximize individual gains. However, in doing so, the individual ultimately destroys the underlying common resource and thus the very source of their gains.

The example is illustrated by a collection of herders that share a common grazing area. Each herder is incentivized to add to their herd, benefiting individually from the earnings of each additional animal. Yet the common grazing area is finite and can only support a finite number of animals. Thus, the commons will become overgrazed and each herder will end up in a worse position than if they had behaved with a collective responsibility. The challenge for the individual then becomes deciding whether to maximize individual gains at the expense of the commons or to prolong individual gains by protecting the commons. Despite the logic in preserving the commons, few herders are likely to stand on the sidelines as others share in the spoils. In a time of record earnings and vessel prices many shipowners and shipyards demonstrated herder mentalities by grazing in the same market.

### The Myths About Cancellations

Many are looking to the prospects of cancellations as a panacea for the impending oversupply situation. Regardless of whether owners explicitly cancel their contracts, the overwhelming majority of the ships on order will likely find a way into the market.

On a grand scale, governments have a lot at stake to support shipbuilding as an industry. China specifically has stated that it plans support several industries singling‐out ten in particular - light manufacturing, textile, steel, non‐ferrous metal, automobile, petrochemical, electronics, telecommunications, logistics and shipbuilding. Cancellations by orderers will be picked up as resales or end up being marketed by shipyards.

### Potential Cancellations

In reality, most of the orders for 2009 and early 2010 delivery are already under construction. The likelihood of existing projects being completely scrapped, after money has been sunk by orderers and shipyards, is slim to none. Orders that have been placed for 2011 and beyond delivery are more likely to be subject to cancellation or renegotiation because physical construction has yet to begin and less capital has been committed.

### Staying in Harmony

The challenge facing the orderbook is largely financial. At present shipowners are under pressure from banks to ante up higher equity portions to orders that may have been partially financed, in a rate environment that does little to support the values at which ships were ordered. On a one or two ship basis high equity requirements could be met, but this is not a sustainable solution for shipowners in the long‐run. At the same time, banks may be reluctant to push shipowners to meet such requirements. From a bank's perspective, owners in default could translate into large portfolios of physical ships. Neither party is likely to rock the boat too much in such a precarious situation.

For now, shipyards, banks and shipowners are likely to mutually respect each other in hopes that business relationships can be salvaged for the longer‐term. When one of these parties begins to feel a squeeze, the asset market may further unravel. En bloc sales of assets from banks will likely represent the floor to asset prices, but the accumulation of pressure to crack that level has yet to materialize.
