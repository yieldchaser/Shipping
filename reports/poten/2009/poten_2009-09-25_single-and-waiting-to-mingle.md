# Single and Waiting to Mingle

Heading into the fourth quarter, it is becoming apparent that 2009 is not shaping up to be the pivotal year as many had expected regarding the single hull fleet. Some shipowners hoped that the planned removal of single hull tankers would have provided support to freight rates and asset prices this year. While the expected phase‐out has certainly played a major role in fleet renewal and newbuilding activity over the past five years, the removal of this fleet may not be the immediate boon to the market as some had hoped.

The market for single hulls is certainly dwindling, however it is debatable whether regulations or market forces are at hand. ### On Incident

Prior to the Hebei Spirit incident in December 2007, South Korea was generally expected to allow non‐double hull tankers to trade in its ports through 2015. South Korea had long been the top discharge port for the single hull tankers that were the subject of International Maritime Organization (IMO) regulations requiring the vessels stop trading by 2015. The accident spilled 75,000 barrels of crude oil into the Yellow Sea, caused more than $500 million in damage and inspired South Korea to accelerate the ban on single hull ships to 2010. The United Arab Emirates followed suit by announcing that it too would prohibit the use of single hull tankers from 2010.

Other countries have also voiced concerns about single hull ships trading within their waters and have intimated regulations could be on the horizon, however this remains to be seen.

At present, IMO regulations ban single hull tankers from trading after 2010 or when they reach 25 years of age, but allow them to continue until 2015 with permission from port states and approval under the Conditional Assessment Scheme.

### Making the Market

The economics supporting the continued trade in single hull tankers are being seriously tested in the current market. Historically, the advantage offered by single hull tankers was that they traded at a discount compared to their double hulled counterparts. The spread between spot rates for the two hull types has narrowed by about 65 percent from last year, due largely to the historically weak freight environment and less tanker demand. Shipowners will take their chances and hold onto these ships in hopes they will be the beneficiaries of future market spikes. While demolitions will undoubtedly continue to take place, it is unlikely that market will see a marked increase in removals in the immediate term, especially given the fourth quarter is the traditional season for large swings in market rates.

The key to the future of the single hull markets is at the load terminals. The majority of single hull liftings are on the Arabian Gulf - Far East route and nothing to the contrary has stated that Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and many other key supplier nations will prohibit the single hull lifting of cargoes before 2015.

Even when the phase out of these ships hits the market, two factors will mitigate the widespread effects of a shock to the system. First, utilization and overall capacity of this fleet has proven to be of decreasing commercial relevance. Based on a sample of vessels, the frequency of with which these vessels are fixed puts the utilization in the 20 - 30 % range. For all intents and purposes, these vessels have been phased out of the market. Secondly, the nations that have explicitly moved to their phase‐out deadline forward have already drastically cut the level of imports on these vessels.

Single hull ships will likely get increased attention over the next several years, but it can be argued that the market is well‐prepared for their exit.
