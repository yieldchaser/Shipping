# Still in the Dark in Denmark

In the weeks and months leading up to the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) in Copenhagen, expectations ran high that the meeting would produce a concrete emissions‐reduction framework to replace that of the Kyoto Protocol. As the discussions progress, it becomes more apparent that a resolution may not be forthcoming. With the first of two weeks of the IPCC coming to an end, the consensus on climate change is now under siege and hopes for the current session have been scaled back to yielding just a political basis for future negotiations (mind your pockets, taxpayers). The divide between developed and developing countries has emerged as a dominant theme: while the two groups generally agree on the need for a massive reduction in worldwide carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, the impasse has not surprisingly been on the most critical question; who will pay for it?

The International Energy Agency estimates that capping CO2 emissions at target levels could cost more than $10 trillion between 2010 and 2030. The first week of debate at the UN conference has focused on distributing these costs between industrialized countries responsible for past emissions and the developing nations which are the likely source for the majority of future emissions growth. Each of the more than 190 nations attending the conference in Denmark has brought their own perspective to the issue, but thus far the most prominent division has been between China and the United States. As shown in the chart below, China and the United States account for more than 40 percent of global CO2 emissions, making their cooperation on the matter critical to success.

President Obama hopes Copenhagen will produce a new framework that includes legally binding emissions‐reduction targets for developing countries, especially China and India. It was the lack of such binding targets for developing countries which prevented Congressional approval of the 1997 Kyoto Protocol. The effort to coax these commitments from the world's emerging economies has been complicated by the uncertainty surrounding current U.S. energy policy. Legislation that narrowly passed the House of Representatives in June would commit the United States to a cap and trade system aiming to bring about a 17 percent reduction from 2005 emission levels by 2020. A companion bill currently stalled in the Senate increases the emissions reduction target to 20 percent in the same time period, but the 17 percent reduction approved by the House (equivalent to a three percent reduction from 1990 levels) constituted the Obama Administration's opening position at the Copenhagen talks. Whether it will hurt the United States' economy (or any other economy) in the process seems to have escaped the attention of decision makers.

### Intensely Level Playing Field

China has tried to change the metric for measuring progress on emissions reduction from total emissions to one of emissions intensity. The concept of carbon intensity measures emissions per unit of GDP and allows China to highlight that while it is the world's largest greenhouse gas emitter by volume, it has much lower per capita emissions than the United States and other industrialized countries. In November, China announced its intension to lower the emissions‐ intensity of its economy by between 40 and 45 percent by 2020 to balance environmental concerns with the need for economic growth.

The carbon intensity metric has made China the natural voice of a group of countries, including India, South Africa and others that are logically concerned that ad hoc regulation of carbon emissions would constrain their development. These countries have called for their industrialized counterparts to commit to a 40 percent emissions reduction from 1990 levels by 2020 and create a mechanism for funding clean energy technology in developing nations. The European Union has announced it will provide $150 billion to developing nations each year through 2020 and proposed a system under which poorer countries would be legally required to take specific actions as developed countries continue to work towards assigned national targets. Earlier this year, China suggested that developed nations commit one percent of their GDP to developing countries' efforts to limit their emissions. President Obama will likely be the next to throw up his hands (and American taxpayer money) in support of this as well.

### Its Only Money

When the Copenhagen conference began, industrialized countries responded by endorsing a proposal for a $10 billion per year fund to be transferred to developing countries to help them with the potential impact of climate change. In the opening days of the conference, the White House said a consensus was forming around the idea, which it said would form a core element of any agreement produced in Copenhagen. Such a fund would also buy world leaders two years of negotiating time before the Kyoto Protocol formally expires in 2012. US officials have pledged to pay into any such fund, but Todd Stern, the Obama Administration's chief negotiator at the talks, rejected the idea of "reparations" by developed countries to make up for decades of emissions. Stern said the $10 billion fund would focus on the poorest countries and made especially clear he did not expect the fund to compensate China for its emissions cuts. Where does this funding stop? How are funds allocated, overseen and administered? The leaders seem to be neglecting these hugely important details.

At a time when industrialized countries are still recovering from the economic crisis, it is unrealistic to expect them to pay for the developing world's transition to a cleaner economy as they attempt similar transitions themselves.

The fact that China does not fit comfortably into either the "developing" or "developed" country labels has been the source of much of the friction on display over the past week in Denmark. Beijing's efforts to lead developing nations and shift the focus from total emissions to emissions intensity may win ideological support, but does not change the underlying realities. Slower growth of emissions is not less emissions and focusing on carbon intensity may ultimately slow progress towards real economic growth.

With China's surging demand expected to be the source of about half of carbon emissions growth in coming decades, any effective international climate change agreement needs Chinese support. Two points highlight the lack of faith in the development of a meaningful framework: the inability of signers and supporters of Kyoto to actually meet the first set of emissions targets and the innate interest of many world leaders boondoggle again in Copenhagen.
