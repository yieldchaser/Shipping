# A Fistful of Barrels

Estimating the holdings of unseen petroleum reservoirs - especially those located deep beneath the seabed - is a daunting and imprecise task. Although estimates of established fields usually benefit from seismic and other geological information accumulated over time, estimates of reserves in new discoveries usually lack this luxury.

Media rumblings of significant new crude oil finds are typically reported as best estimates based on preliminary data. Sometimes these estimates stem from anecdotal inferences of reservoir size based on other well‐known fields deemed to be similar in nature. These guesses are, of course, subject to later tweaking and revision. With this caveat in mind, the gathering of guesses surrounding the reserve potential of oil in offshore Cuban fields - on face value - seems destined to give this island nation a great leap forward into the club of net oil exporters.

### North Cuban Basin

Cuba's present assessment of their offshore finds pegs the recoverable oil potential at up to 20 billion barrels. This estimate, though, is about quadruple the U.S. Geological Survey's estimate of Cuba's offshore reserves. To be sure, it will take years of additional exploration and development work on these deep offshore fields before commercial volumes of oil come to market.

Cuba's entry into the ranks of net oil exporters naturally depends on many diverse factors. But when - or if - this country does become one, its splash could create ripples in tanker use that could well cascade through vessel sectors and markets.

### Sailing through Seas mined with "What Ifs"

Cuba's estimate of offshore oil reserves amounts to nearly the quantity of the United States proven crude oil reserves in 2007. If the US produced some 4.6 million barrels per day of crude oil from its reserves of 21.3 billion barrels, how much oil would Cuba likely produce per day? Assuming a similar pumping to reserve ratio as the US uses, Cuba would be able to produce oil for a little over 12 years - just like the US.

One "what if" is presently not a politically feasible option. While the US would be a refinery‐ready market for Cuba's oil, current legislation makes US refineries neither willing nor able to process it. So with a logical market forbidden, where will Cuban oil go? If the properties of their new offshore reserves mirrors that of Cuba's typical heavy (below 20 degree API gravity) crude oil, any production left over from domestic use may have to travel far to find a home capable of upgrading it. Although it seems premature to even speculate on how much oil will move and where it might move to, tanker owners might take some comfort in the view that a new source of oil production needing transportation to distant markets might eventually appear.

However, what if the US does - for whatever reason - become an outlet for Cuban oil? With the steady decline in its own production, any influx of nearby Cuban oil would mute the hopes of tankers owners expecting an expansion of longer‐haul routes for replacement oil into the US.

How close reality comes to present estimates of Cuban oil reserves will play a role in its eventual production rate. The cost and ease of production as well as the crude oil's properties will also be reflected in this rate. So for now, tanker owners might consider bookmarking Cuba's oil discovery. The twist and turns in this unfolding story of the further exploration and development work to wrest oil from these deep offshore fields may yet impact tanker markets.
