# Venezuelas Struggles Continue

### U.S. tightens sanctions against former regional powerhouse

The reports of the imminent demise of the regime of Nicolas Maduro of Venezuela (like the death of Mark Twain in 1897) have been greatly exaggerated. Venezuela has been subject to ever tightening sanctions since the Maduro regime cracked down on civilian protests in 2014. Citing severe human rights violations, President Obama imposed sanctions on a number of Venezuelan individuals. Additional sanctions were imposed the following year. President Trump significantly broadened and tightened sanctions on Venezuela, in particular after the May 2018 presidential election in the country, which was deemed rigged. Throughout this period, the country's economy crumbled and crude oil production and exports fell as sanctions started to bite. Refining output has suffered as well. However, despite the pressure from (most of) the international community and growing protests from his own population, Mr. Maduro is still in power. At this point, one must conclude that to try to predict if (or when) he will leave is a fool's errand. However, it appears that until he does, Venezuela's (oil) trading partners will be limited to China, Russia and to a lesser extent India. This has already had a profound impact on the tanker industry (both for crude oil and product tankers).

Western companies that still do regular business with PDVSA (Venezuela's State Oil Company), are Swedish refiner NYNAS (a joint venture between PDVSA and Neste Oil) and American oil company Chevron. NYNAS takes about one Aframax cargo each month and has reportedly been granted a waiver to do business with PDVSA until October 25. Chevron, which has been operating in Venezuela since the 1920s, produces crude in several joint ventures with PDVSA and was recently granted a waiver to continue to operate for another three months. Other Western companies that still appear to do business with PDVSA are Spain's Repsol and Italy's ENI.

Russia's Rosneft and China's CNPC are the two other large JV producers in Venezuela. They have been exporting crude from Venezuela under pre-existing agreements to pay off debts. Previously, it was unclear whether such agreements fell outside the scope of U.S. sanctions, but it appears that the new executive order from President Trump closed that loophole. This opens the door to secondary sanctions on Rosneft and CNPC.

The latest round of sanctions by President Trump seems to have more of an impact on the tanker market than observers may have originally expected. A number of owners who previously loaded in Venezuela have indicated that they will no longer entertain voyages from this country. They fear that they will run afoul of U.S. sanctions. The Office of Foreign *Including Caribbean Terminals (Aruba, Bonaire, Curacao) Asset Control (OFAC), part of the U.S. Treasury Department, has increased its interest in the shipping industry as a potential way to circumvent sanctions on North Korea, Syria, Iran and Venezuela. OFAC has identified ship-to-ship transfers and disabling AIS systems as ways to circumvent sanctions.

In the case of Iran, the country can address some of these challenges by using its own considerable tanker fleet to move its crude. Venezuela does not have this option. It does not have a meaningful tanker fleet and most of the tankers that it does control are old and not well maintained. They are also not the right size. Most of the crude that Venezuela still exports goes to long-haul destinations in China and India, which are primarily VLCC trades. PDVSA controls mostly Aframaxes in addition to a handful of product tankers. Many of PDVSA's tankers appear to be idle (or disabled their AIS) while some may be engaged in moving crude oil to export terminals in the Caribbean and to do ship-to-ship transfers. PDVSA also still supplies some crude to Cuba's Cienfuegos refinery. Originally a Soviet-era facility configured to run Russian crude, it was upgraded by PDVSA to run Venezuelan crude. It is currently running well below its 65,000 b/d capacity because Cuba has been receiving less crude from Venezuela.

As the situation in Venezuela becomes more tense, it is hoped that negotiations between the government and the opposition will lead to a peaceful transition of power and a lifting of sanctions, so the Venezuelan people can start rebuilding their country.
