# Building New-clear Energy

While worldwide crude oil demand remains near record levels driven by increased economic activity, the overall energy demand mix that is projected to emerge is perhaps unexpected. Though crude oil was once a primary source of all energy, changes in the market, especially higher prices for crude, have made alternatives commercially viable in certain capacities. For example, natural gas has competed with petroleum for use in residential heating and industry. Most surprising may be the recent development of nuclear energy as a viable growing alternative. This has been driven by some noteworthy technological advancements and their implementation in many parts of the world. While the increasing use of non-petroleum energy sources is a reality in the United States, a paradigm shift away from oil on a worldwide scale is however highly unlikely in the foreseeable future.

### On the Horizon

The chart on the previous page represents the EIA's outlook for total energy consumption in the United States in terms of quadrillion Btus. The total demand for petroleum products and nuclear power are both expected to increase in the next five to ten years.

While it is true that no new refineries have been built in the United States for the past couple of decades, it is interesting to learn that plans to build several nuclear power plants are well under way. Four of these proposed projects would be in Texas, while others will be built elsewhere in the Southeast.

### Gaining Perspective

In the United States and other developed nations, nuclear energy is used primarily for electricity generation, as it does not have much commercial use in the transportation sector or as a feedstock. Nuclear energy has been demonstrated as best suited for large grid electricity generation, a characteristic that inhibits worldwide adoption of the technology. Globally, nuclear energy has seen an increase in the percentage share of electricity generation, while petroleum has seen a decline in its percentage of market share. The recent sentiment among developed nations is to conserve petroleum for transportation and feedstock uses, rather than burning it for electricity generation.

Tanker owners and oil companies need not fear just yet that nuclear energy will become an alternative to petroleum-based energy. This is due to the flexible nature of oil products and the lack of flexibility of nuclear power. Oil products are better suited for developing economies, as these nations are not likely to have the upfront capital resources needed to implement nuclear reactors. With economic growth in these regions expected to remain strong, overall demand for energy will continue to grow. Most importantly, especially for those in the tanker market, shipment of crude oil products to these remote areas of the world is often the most economic alternative for transportation. In summary, nuclear energy is coming back, but with all that tanker owners and oil companies have to worry about these days this is one issue not to fret over.
