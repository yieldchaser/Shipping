The global economy is navigating a complex adjustment

October 19, 2026  |  October 19, 2026  |  https://www.breakwaveadvisors.com/insights/2026/10/18/the-global-economy-is-navigating-a-complex-adjustment

ByMichalis Voutsinas

The global economy is navigating a complex adjustment, with strongerthan-anticipated activity coexisting with persistent inflationary pressures, geopolitical disruption and increasingly divergent regional trajectories. In its October 2026 World Economic Outlook, entitled “Resilience Under Strain, Urgent Choices,” the IMF projects global growth of 3.1 percent in 2026 and 3.4 percent in 2027, modestly above its previous forecasts. Technology-related investment, resilient international trade and the adaptability of businesses have helped offset the effects of geopolitical tensions and higher energy costs. At the same time, persistent inflation, elevated financing costs and limited fiscal flexibility complicate the outlook. The resulting picture is one of continued expansion rather than broad-based acceleration, with the distribution of growth becoming as significant as its aggregate pace. For commodity markets, this divergence creates a more nuanced environment in which investment, industrial activity and the geographical reconfiguration of trade will shape the distribution of incremental demand. Against this backdrop, the IMF expects global growth to moderate from 3.5 percent in 2025 to 3.1 percent this year before recovering to 3.4 percent in 2027. Advanced economies are forecast to expand by 2.1 percent in 2026, while emerging and developing economies are projected to grow by 3.7 percent. The latter reflects, in part, the exceptional disruption experienced in the Middle East, where output is expected to contract by 0.3 percent before rebounding by 6.3 percent in 2027. Elsewhere, emerging Asia remains the principal engine of global expansion, supported by manufacturing investment and the region's growing role in technology supply chains. Regional divergence reflects differences in energy exposure, industrial composition and access to investment rather than a uniform deterioration in global demand. Nevertheless, the policy environment is becoming more restrictive. Elevated sovereign debt and borrowing costs limit governments' capacity to cushion further shocks, while competing requirements for infrastructure, defence and energy security place additional demands on public finances.

In this juncture, international trade has proved stronger than anticipated despite geopolitical fragmentation. The IMF forecasts trade volumes, including goods and services, to increase by 4.6 percent in 2026 and 4.7 percent in 2027, following growth of 5.0 percent in 2025. The currentyear projection has been revised upwards by 1.1 percentage points since July, reflecting stronger activity. The composition of this expansion, however, is changing. Semiconductors, computing equipment and related technology infrastructure account for a significant share of incremental demand. This provides a powerful source of support for trade, although its relationship with conventional industrial production and bulk commodity consumption is less direct. At the same time, tariffs and strategic competition are reshaping sourcing decisions. Businesses are diversifying suppliers, relocating selected production and adjusting inventory strategies to reduce exposure to individual markets. These developments may create new investment and commodity flows as production capacity is established in alternative locations, although the benefits will emerge unevenly and over different time horizons. The reconfiguration of supply chains is therefore neither an unqualified drag on trade nor an automatic source of additional demand. Its implications depend on the scale of new productive capacity, the durability of commercial relationships and the extent to which alternative sourcing generates additional activity rather than simply displacing existing flows.

The sustainability of this trade expansion will also depend on the evolution of inflation and financial conditions. Global consumer-price inflation is forecast to rise from 4.1 percent in 2025 to 4.7 percent in 2026 before easing to 4.1 percent in 2027. The IMF assumes an average oil price of $95.74 per barrel this year, approximately 41 percent above the 2025 average, while nonfuel commodity prices are projected to increase by 17.3 percent. The trajectory will depend on whether higher energy and food prices remain a relative-price shock or become more persistent through wages, production costs and inflation expectations. If the initial disruption fades without significant second-round effects, central banks may retain scope to ease policy gradually. Conversely, sustained price pressures would prolong restrictive financing conditions, weighing on consumption and investment while increasing debt-servicing costs. The consequences are particularly significant for energy-importing economies, where higher import bills can constrain household purchasing power and industrial margins. Yet elevated prices may also accelerate investment in energy security, alternative supply sources and infrastructure, creating longer-term demand for selected commodities. The balance between these opposing effects will depend on the duration of the shock and the extent to which higher costs induce additional investment rather than simply suppress economic activity.

Against this broader backdrop of divergent regional growth, the United States is forecast to expand by 2.3 percent in both 2026 and 2027, underpinned by consumer spending, business investment and technology-related capital expenditure. As the principal centre of the artificial-intelligence investment cycle, the US is driving substantial spending on computing infrastructure and associated equipment, generating spillover effects across global manufacturing and trade. Domestic energy production also provides a degree of insulation from external supply disruptions. However, the sustainability of this expansion will depend on whether substantial capital outlays translate into lasting productivity gains. Any moderation in technology investment could reduce one of the principal drivers of global trade growth, even if domestic demand continues to underpin the wider economy. Meanwhile, persistent fiscal deficits and elevated Treasury yields risk keeping financing costs high, with implications for investment decisions well beyond the United States.

Across Asia, the outlook is increasingly shaped by the differing trajectories of China and India. China's growth is projected to moderate from 5.0 percent in 2025 to 4.5 percent in 2026 and 4.0 percent in 2027. The property-sector adjustment and subdued household consumption continue to restrain domestic activity, but the broader picture remains differentiated. China's manufacturing scale, export competitiveness and position in technology supply chains continue to underpin economic output, while infrastructure spending and industrial investment provide potential offsets to weakness in construction. The balance between these components will shape the composition, rather than simply the pace, of growth. A more durable recovery in household consumption would broaden the domestic foundations of expansion, while continued manufacturing strength could sustain export activity and selected industrial demand. For commodity markets, the implications vary by product: construction-related demand remains exposed to the property cycle, whereas manufacturing, infrastructure and energy requirements follow different trajectories. India offers a complementary growth profile, with the IMF forecasting expansion of 7.0 percent in both fiscal 2026/27 and 2027/28. Domestic consumption, investment and services underpin this momentum, while infrastructure development and industrialisation provide scope for rising raw-material requirements. Higher imported energy costs remain a consideration, but the economy's expanding industrial base offers a significant source of additional demand over the medium term. Southeast Asia adds a further dimension, as manufacturing diversification and technology investment redistribute production across the region. Together, these developments point towards a broader, more geographically varied pattern of Asian growth, with implications for the volume and origin of seaborne commodity flows.

Despite economic headwinds and persistent challenges, both the global economy and the dry bulk market have performed remarkably well so far this year. Looking ahead, the outlook will increasingly depend on whether this momentum can be sustained amid divergent regional growth, shifting investment patterns and the reconfiguration of international trade. Developments in China’s steel sector, expanding India’s industrial base, infrastructure investment across emerging Asia, expanding iron ore exports from Brazil and Guinea, and shifting coal and grain flows across the Atlantic and Pacific will shape cargo availability and vessel employment. Ultimately, sustaining the market’s positive trajectory will require more than operational inefficiencies and temporary supply-side disruptions; durable support must also come from underlying cargo demand and sufficient growth in tonne-mile activity to absorb the expanding fleet.
