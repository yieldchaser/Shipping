Market Insight  
By Nikos Tagoulis, Senior Analyst 
The Middle East conflict entered a new phase following the 
Houthis’ imposition of a naval blockade against Saudi Arabia, add-
ing to geopolitical uncertainty. Beyond attacks on vessels, recent 
reports that a Houthi strike forced the 400,000 -bpd Jazan oil re-
finery to halt operations demonstrate that the Houthis are also 
willing to target energy infrastructure.  
This escalation comes at a time when Saudi Arabia increasingly 
relies on its west coast to maintain commodity exports following 
the disruption of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Seaborne 
crude oil exports through Yanbu doubled in Q2 2026, reflecting 
increased flows through the East –West pipeline as the Kingdom 
sought to bypass Hormuz. 
Against this backdrop, tanker shipping faces the most immediate 
exposure. For Saudi crude exports, the effects would differ by 
destination. European cargoes would remain largely unaffected, 
as they already move north through the Suez Canal. By contrast, 
the consequences would be considerably greater for barrels des-
tined for Asia and the Indian subcontinent via Bab el -Mandeb. To 
avoid the Gulf of Aden, vessels would have to sail north through 
Suez before rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, extending 
voyages to approximately 2.5 times their normal length, adding 
tonne-miles and increasing voyage costs. 
Such rerouting would tighten prompt vessel availability and push 
spot rates higher, while increased use of the Suez Canal would 
provide further support for Suezmax demand. VLCCs would have 
to transit Suez partially laden, potentially requiring STS lightering 
and resulting in greater operational complexity and longer transit 
times. 
Given the above, the strategic importance of the SUMED pipeline, 
and of Egypt more broadly as an oil transshipment hub, increases 
The pipeline connects the Red Sea with the Mediterranean, and 
Saudi Arabia would be expected to send greater crude volumes to 
Ain Sukhna port for transportation through SUMED to Sidi Kerir 
on the Mediterranean coast. From there, cargoes could continue 
to Asian markets via the Cape of Good Hope, providing an alterna-
tive route that avoids disruptions in both Hormuz and Bab el -
Mandeb. 
Moreover, energy-security considerations could lead importers to 
diversify away from the Middle East and seek alternative suppli-
ers, reshuffling energy trade. Demand could shift towards the 
United States, Brazil and West Africa, while Russian crude flows 
to China and the Indian subcontinent could strengthen further.  
For the oil -products segment, the reported attack on refinery in-
frastructure is particularly concerning. If such strikes persist and 
cause broader disruption to output and exports, they would com-
pound the effects of Russia ’s ban on diesel exports, further con-
straining seaborne oil -product export volumes and heightening 
energy-security concerns. Should Saudi refinery operations re-
main close to normal levels, the more immediate consequence 
would likely be a redirection of trade flows. Eastbound shipments 
from Saudi Red Sea ports would become materially less competi-
tive, shifting a larger share of Saudi fuel volumes towards Europe.  
Outside the tanker segment, the impact would be less pro-
nounced. In dry bulk, grain shipments moving via Mediterranean 
and Suez into the Red Sea would be less affected, unless Houthi 
attacks on infrastructure escalate. By contrast, steel trade would 
be more exposed, given that a substantial share of steel products 
originates in China and transits Bab el-Mandeb. 
For containerships, the Houthi move primarily interrupts the seg-
ment’s cautious steps towards returning to the Red Sea. Region-
ally, Jeddah is exposed because of its growing transshipment role 
and participation in Gulf feeder networks. More broadly, the con-
tinuation of diversions via the Cape of Good Hope, which tie up 
active capacity in an otherwise oversupplied segment, is expected 
to remain supportive of freight rates. 
In conclusion, the threat of Houthi action disrupting Red Sea trade 
marks a significant escalation in the Middle East crisis and poses 
a material threat to regional navigation and trade. If it causes 
broader disruption to energy flows, the consequences could ex-
tend to the global macroeconomic outlook by intensifying energy -
security concerns, raising energy costs, adding to inflationary 
pressures and weighing on global trade and economic growth.

Tanker markets trended upward overall last week, driven by a 
strong Aframax segment. The BDTI averaged 2,418, up 10% w -o-
w. 
On the VLCC side, a main driver last week was developments in 
the Middle East, which created uncertainty over regional flows, 
redirecting demand towards neighbouring markets. In Arabian 
Gulf, the market was in a holding stance, with charterers delaying 
fixings while owners withheld tonnage in anticipation of firmer 
rates. West Africa and US Gulf faced similar fundamentals, with 
rising demand shortening the tonnage list. Overall, VLCC TCE 
closed the week at $193,293/day, 2.3% lower w-o-w.  
 
The Suezmax segment witnessed divergent trends, with the Mid-
dle East and the Black Sea both sources of disruption. In the Ara-
bian Gulf, heightened geopolitical risk and uncertainty took a toll 
on activity, with owners reluctant to commit to fixtures. Mean-
while, drone attacks near Novorossiysk brought CPC loadings to a 
standstill, throwing the Black Sea market into disarray. This land-
scape drew ballasters from both regions towards West Africa, 
where enquiry was insufficient to absorb the tonnage build -up, 
weighing on rates there. Suezmax TCE closed at $154,070/day, 
up 10% w-o-w. 
 
Aframaxes continued to support the crude freight market, build-
ing upward momentum, driven by a soaring USG market, with 
TD25 posting strong gains. Developments in the Middle East fa-
voured the Aframax Med market, which saw increased activity at 
the SUMED-linked Sidi Kerir and in Libya. The North Sea was pos-
itive, with healthy activity and prospects for further rate upside, 
as vessels are estimated to ballast towards the robust USG. In the 
East, the market was subdued, amid tepid demand, with owners 
cautious ahead of the rest of August programme. Aframax TCE 
surged 29% w-o-w to $110,482/day.

The dry bulk markets delivered a mixed performance last week, 
with the BDI closing at 2,743 points, marginally lower w-o-w. 
The Capesize segment recovered from the previous week's loss-
es, as both basins provided support following a weak start, with 
the emergence of new demand. In the Pacific, support came from 
higher participation of Australian miners and a rise in seaborne 
coal volumes, while in the Atlantic, the main drivers of enquiry 
were West Africa and South Brazil. However, the increase in fuel 
costs, due to heightened geopolitical risk, casts a shadow over 
the outlook. 
The Kamsarmax/Panamax market faced constrained conditions, 
as weakening demand overall exerted downward pressure on 
spot rates. In the Pacific, limited enquiry from North Pacific, Indo-
nesia and East Australia led to a build -up of prompt supply, wors-
ening fundamentals, while in the Atlantic, transatlantic flows 
stayed sluggish despite a brief midweek pickup. Overall, charter-
ers gained leverage, as mounting competition for limited stems 
weighed on rates. 
An uneven week for Ultramaxes/Supramaxes, with regional dif-
ferences. Pockets of support were found in the East Med, driven 
by grain enquiry, and in the Indian Ocean, on the back of petcoke 
volumes. Elsewhere, the market was subdued: in the Atlantic, the 
US Gulf witnessed oversupply, while the North Atlantic was mut-
ed amid the seasonal slowdown. In the Pacific, despite firmer de-
mand in Southeast Asia, the wider Asian market was weighed 
down by a growing tonnage pool.  
The Handysize market saw mixed trends but was resilient overall. 
Asia provided support, as fundamentals favoured owners, with 
declining tonnage availability and emerging cargo demand. Else-
where, demand weakened, though the Med held steady, with lim-
ited supply matching enquiry, while the oversupplied US Gulf saw 
rates under pressure.

At Alang, the market was muted last week amid tepid demand, 
while rising energy prices and the weakening of the Indian Ru-
pee against the US Dollar weighed on sentiment. However, the 
backdrop in the steel market is supportive, with steel plate pric-
es rising, driven mainly by tightening supply, as steel processing 
operations are curtailed by the monsoons. Finally, on the policy 
front, the proposed listing of two Alang facilities is a positive 
signal towards the opening of a first approved channel for EU 
flagged recycling candidates to be directed to the Indian market.  
Bangladesh is emerging from a period of deadly monsoon 
flooding impacting economic activity, with the ship recycling 
segment recovering in parallel, as operations at yards and steel 
mills resume operations. Despite limited activity, buying interest 
remains healthy, particularly for bulk carriers, although the re-
cent case of a tanker sanctioned after clearance but before 
beaching has made buyers more reluctant and increased de-
mand for compliance -clear tonnage. Overall sentiment is posi-
tive, supported by expectations that candidates delayed during 
the monsoon period will be offered to the post -monsoon mar-
ket. 
Conditions at Gadani hub remained broadly unchanged, defined 
by a limited pool of candidates and healthy buying appetite. The 
steel market is supportive, with prices firming on the back of 
supply constraints, as monsoon -related adverse weather con-
tinues to disrupt mills' operations. There is optimism that gov-
ernment tax relief on property transactions and support for af-
fordable housing will boost construction activity, and in turn 
steel demand, once the monsoon passes. Meanwhile, news of 
the country's credit rating upgrade, on the back of foreign capi-
tal inflows, added to the positive sentiment. 
In Turkey, despite improving conditions in the steel market, ac-
tivity remains flat as buyers maintain a wait -and-see stance 
amid a limited number of candidates and further weakening of 
the local currency.
